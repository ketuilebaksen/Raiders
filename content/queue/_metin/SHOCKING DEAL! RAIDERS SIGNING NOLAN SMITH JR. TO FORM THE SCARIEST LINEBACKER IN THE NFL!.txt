THE SMITH FIT, ONE LEVEL DEEPER

And before we move on, let's spend one more minute on WHY the Nolan Smith fit is so clean, because the scheme details matter. Rob Leonard's 3-4 doesn't just want edge rushers — it wants edge rushers who can win in multiple ways, because the whole design philosophy, as we've covered since March, is generating pressure WITHOUT blitzing every down. Crosby gives you the relentless power-and-hands game on one side. Paye gives you the early-down, run-setting physicality. But what this room lost when Crawford went down was juice — pure, twitchy, bend-the-corner speed. That's Smith's entire calling card. He's the change-of-pace fastball this rotation didn't have, and in an NFL where you face quick-release quarterbacks every single week, the difference between pressure in 2.7 seconds and pressure in 3.1 seconds is the difference between a punt and a touchdown.

Think about the ripple effects too. Malcolm Koonce slides back into the situational role that fits him best instead of being stretched as the clear number three. Patrick Johnson keeps his special-teams-plus-packages lane. Sloan keeps developing on the practice squad with no pressure to be rushed up. And Crosby — for the first time in YEARS — gets to rush against true one-on-ones in crunch time, because offensive coordinators can no longer slide the entire protection his way without paying for it on the other side. Ask any elite rusher what they want most, and they'll tell you: a running mate. Maxx just got one. And one more layer I love: Smith arrives from a champion's building, with a ring in the bag and championship habits in the routine. On a roster this young, in a locker room learning how to win, that has value no stat page captures.

THE CLAIM FIVE TEAMS WANTED: DARRELL LUTER JR., UNDER THE MICROSCOPE

Now let's give the spotlight to a move that deserves way more attention than it got — because when the Raiders made their ONE waiver claim, some of you shrugged. "A depth corner? That's it?" Well, here's the detail that should change your mind: reportedly FIVE teams put in for Darrell Luter Jr. Five. The Raiders just happened to be standing at the front of the line with that number one priority — which, by the way, is exactly how we told you they'd weaponize it: quietly, selectively, on a player other teams actually wanted.

So who is he? A former fifth-round pick out of South Alabama who spent three seasons with the 49ers — and whose release genuinely surprised people in San Francisco, because his preseason tape was CLEAN: just two receptions allowed on seven targets. The front office's reasoning, as explained by the personnel department, is simple: Luter brings regular season experience, special teams chops, and real competitiveness to one of the youngest secondaries in football.

And "youngest" is not an exaggeration — count the room with me. Eric Stokes and Darien Porter starting outside. Jermod McCoy, the rookie coming off the ACL and off those preseason fade-route lessons. Hezekiah Masses, the seventh-round rookie who already stole an interception from Cousins in practice. Trayden Stokes winning the free safety job as a rookie. And remember, this group already lost Chigozie Anusiem to a season-ending injury in the Houston game. That's a secondary with sky-high upside and almost zero collective NFL scar tissue. Dropping a 26-year-old with three years of real Sundays into that room — someone who's covered NFL speed, played meaningful special teams snaps, and been through a full season's grind — is exactly the kind of stabilizer young units need. Sometimes the best veteran addition isn't a star. It's a professional.

But let's go one level deeper, film-room style, because the tape tells you what kind of player this is. Start with the tackling — his superpower. The data says he missed only THREE tackles across 271 snaps, and for a cornerback, that number is gold. Corners who tackle turn six-yard gains into six-yard gains; corners who don't turn them into sixty-yard touchdowns. There's a rep against Atlanta where Luter diagnosed a run to Bijan Robinson — BIJAN, one of the hardest backs alive to corral in space — maintained his outside leverage like a coaching manual, and made the open-field stop clean. There's another against Jacksonville where he's aligned in the slot, sniffs out the run instantly, closes on Brian Thomas Jr., holds his ground, and the pile he creates helps generate an interception opportunity. That's not workout-warrior stuff. That's football IQ and want-to.

Now the honest flip side, because this channel doesn't sell fairy tales: coverage is the weakness. Against quicker, twitchier receivers, Luter loses one-on-ones in man, and when he does, quarterbacks find it immediately. That's why his day-one role will lean heavily on special teams while the staff works on his technique. But here's the comparison that makes Raiders fans' ears perk up: the aggressive, downhill, physical style is genuinely reminiscent of what this team used to get from Nate Hobbs. If the coverage refinement comes — and he's young enough that it can — this claim stops being a special-teamer and starts being a rotational defensive back you won on a technicality. Zero resources spent. Priority used perfectly. Five teams disappointed. That's asset management, Raider Nation.

Start with Alex Cappa. Then Raheem Mostert — and this one genuinely tugs at me, football-wise. Even at 34, the man's speed never looked like it aged; he never became the stereotypical declining back. On the right team, in the right rotation, he could still contribute. But nobody made that call all preseason, and unless injuries create a desperate need somewhere, retirement starts entering the conversation. Of the three names here, he's got the best odds of one more chapter — but the league is telling him something, and it's telling him loudly.

After leaving Tennessee for a fresh start with the Raiders under Pete Carroll, he closed 2025 on the roster with limited production. Now? Reportedly not a single team brought him in for a workout this entire preseason. Approaching 34, watching the league bet on younger legs at every turn. None of this erases what he accomplished — but football is a ruthless business, and it doesn't send farewell cards.

Here's why I'm telling you about three players who AREN'T Raiders anymore: because their limbo is the negative-space portrait of exactly what Spytek is building. A younger roster. It's also how the Chiefs, the Eagles, the modern winners all operate. The Nolan Smith excitement and the Lockett silence are two ends of the same philosophy — and if it delivers Januarys to Las Vegas, this fan base will make peace with the cold.
