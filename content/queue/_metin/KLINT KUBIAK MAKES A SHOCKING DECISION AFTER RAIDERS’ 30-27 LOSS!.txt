Raider Nation, I've listened to this press conference three times now, and I want to tell you something that might sound strange after a loss.

This was the most encouraging twenty minutes of the season.

Not because of the result. The Raiders are 3-1 and Kansas City left Allegiant Stadium with first place in the AFC West. That hurts and it should. But if you've followed this franchise through the last five years of losing, you know exactly how those press conferences used to sound: deflection, vagueness, a coach explaining why nothing was really anybody's fault, and a quarterback saying the right words with dead eyes.

Instead, Klint Kubiak walked up to a microphone and said four words you almost never hear from an NFL head coach.

"I blew it."

Today we go through the whole thing, quote by quote, and after each one I'll tell you what it actually means for where this team is headed. Because this press conference told us more about the next three years than the scoreboard told us about Sunday.

I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. Smash that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we push toward 6,000 believers. Let's get into the podium.

FIRST, THE INJURY

Kubiak opened with the news, and it's a real problem: Jalen Nailor has a concussion.

Here's why that matters more than it would for most teams. Jack Bech is already on injured reserve with a fractured forearm. No Raiders wide receiver averages even four catches a game. And now the one with the largest contract in the entire room, three years and $35 million, is in the concussion protocol with a road trip to New England coming.

So let's count the healthy receivers this offense can rely on next Sunday. Tre Tucker, who has had one enormous afternoon. Cody White, who has three touchdowns on three catches and was just promoted to the 53-man roster. Tyler Scott, who the team signed to the practice squad eight days ago and hasn't played a regular season game since 2024. And whoever else can be elevated.

That's it. That's the receiver room against a Bill Belichick-tree defensive staff on the road.

And this is exactly why I keep saying the November 10th deadline matters. Carter Renan and Dante Thornton Jr. are eligible to come off short-term injured reserve around Week Five, which is right now, and this is the week that matters. Thornton is 6-foot-5 with a 4.35 forty. If there was ever a moment to find out what you have, it's this one.

"I'M REALLY PROUD OF THE FIGHT OF OUR TEAM. NOT SURPRISED BY IT."

The first thing out of his mouth after the injury news was this: he's really proud of the fight of his team, and not surprised by the fight of his team. He said they have a lot of really hardworking guys who care about winning, and they fell short.

I want to isolate that middle part, because it's the tell: not surprised.

Think about what that phrase actually claims. He's saying the fight wasn't a pleasant discovery, it was the expected standard. That's a coach who has spent eight months installing a behavior and watched it hold up on a night when his team lost to the best organization in the conference.

Last season this team won three games. The idea of a Raiders head coach saying he wasn't surprised that his group fought for sixty minutes against Kansas City would have been laughable in January. Now it's the baseline.

"I DON'T REGRET IT. I REGRET NOT GETTING IT."

Now the decision everybody is arguing about in the comments. In the third quarter, the Raiders had points on the board, a penalty brought the field goal back, and Kubiak took the three off the board and went for it. They didn't get it.

His explanation: they were going to be aggressive going into this game, that was part of the plan, and then the line of the night. "I don't regret it. I regret not getting it."

Raider Nation, that sentence is a philosophy.

There is a massive difference between a coach who regrets a decision and a coach who regrets an outcome. The first one stops being aggressive the next time, because he's protecting himself from criticism. The second one calls it again next week, because he believes the math.

And the math is real. Against a team with Patrick Mahomes, trading touchdowns for field goals is how you lose by four. If you're going to beat Kansas City, at some point you have to take something. Playing it safe against that franchise has a thirteen-year track record and the record is 14-2 in their favor.

Now, I'll give you the honest counterpoint, because somebody in the comments is already typing it. In the fourth quarter he had a similar fourth-and-short and he kicked the field goal to go up by two. Asked what was different, his answer was blunt: he went for points, he fell short the first time, he wanted points.

So was that inconsistent? A little, yes. But I understand it. The first decision came with more clock and a chance to put a drive-killing hit on their belief. The second came in a situation where a lead was worth more than a touchdown attempt. You can disagree with either call. What you can't say is that he was coaching scared.

And Cousins' answer on the same question was the most professional thing in the entire press conference: he leaves those decisions to the staff, it's always going to be a would-have, could-have, should-have, and it's the players' job to execute and make the coach right.

"I BLEW IT."

Now the moment that defines this presser.

At the end of the game, the Raiders had a chance, and the play didn't get in fast enough. Kubiak explained it twice, unprompted both times. There's a legal shift built into the play call, and the call wasn't getting to the quarterback quickly enough. And then, when a reporter asked him to expand: he didn't get the play call in fast enough, it was a total missed opportunity, and "I blew it."

I need you to understand how rare that is.

Head coaches in this league have an entire vocabulary designed to avoid that sentence. "We'll look at the tape." "There were a lot of moving parts." "We've got to execute better." Those phrases exist specifically so that the man with the most power in the building never has to say the thing Kubiak just said into a microphone in front of the city.

And here's the part that matters for the future: when the head coach takes the hit publicly, every player in that locker room learns what accountability looks like. You cannot ask a rookie corner to own a blown coverage if the coach won't own a late play call. Culture isn't a poster in the weight room. It's who stands up when something goes wrong.

Cousins, for his part, refused to let his coach carry it alone. He said that's just Clint, he's always going to point the thumb at himself. And then the quarterback did the exact same thing: he said in hindsight, among what he called hundreds of mistakes, he should have taken the delay of game. Who cares about five yards, he said. We have to get into the right play and get out of bounds. He was trying to snap the ball to avoid the penalty, and looking back he's asking himself whether the penalty would actually have been better.

Then he said he probably wouldn't sleep that night thinking about it.

That's a 38-year-old quarterback who has played in this league for over a decade, lying awake over five yards. That's why they brought him here.

"THERE'S MORE MEAT ON THAT BONE"

Twice Kubiak was asked about the run game, and twice he refused to hide.

First answer: they have to find that answer, because it'll be a big factor in them being a better team. Second answer, when pressed on what needs to improve against the run: they have to go to the drawing board and figure it out, and then this, which I loved. They have really good backs. They have a good offensive line. "There's more meat on that bone."

Let's translate. He is publicly saying the talent is good enough and the production isn't, which means the problem is execution and scheme fit rather than personnel. That's a bold thing to say out loud, because it puts the responsibility on his own staff.

And I think he's largely right. Ashton Jeanty was the sixth pick of the 2025 draft. Mike Washington Jr. is averaging nearly seven yards a carry. Tyler Linderbaum got three years and $81 million. Kolton Miller has been solid on the edge. Jackson Powers-Johnson is a starting-caliber center who missed this game with a groin injury, which pushed Caleb Rogers into the lineup at right guard.

Cousins gave the most specific diagnosis of anybody, and it's worth hearing because it's a technical answer rather than a vibe. He talked about getting the combo blocks right, about continuing to get better at them as the group works together longer, about the running backs continuing to learn, and about how this is a constant build that happens with more time on task together. He believes the run game comes to fruition over time as that continuity develops and the group understands the nuances.

That's an offensive lineman's answer from a quarterback. A combo block is two players working in tandem on a defensive lineman before one of them climbs to the linebacker, and it is entirely about timing and communication. Get it a half-second wrong and the back has nowhere to go.

So here's the honest read for the future: this run game is not fixed by a new play. It's fixed by the same five men practicing together for six weeks. And the Cesar Ruiz addition fits exactly that logic, as long as everybody understands he needs reps before he's an upgrade.

"THAT'S A BIG FACTOR IN WINNING AND LOSING. WE GAVE THEM TOO MANY OPPORTUNITIES."

Here's the number that actually decided this game, and Kubiak didn't dance around it.

The Raiders had been on the plus side of the takeaway battle all year, leading the NFL with nine. On Sunday they were minus two.

His answer: that's a big factor in winning and losing, they know that, and obviously they gave Kansas City too many opportunities.

I've been warning about the takeaway cliff for two weeks, but this is the other half of it that I should have emphasized more. Turnover margin isn't only about taking the ball away. It's about not giving it away. For three weeks this offense protected the football nearly perfectly: Cousins had three interceptions total and the offense wasn't losing fumbles. Against the one opponent that punishes every mistake, the margin flipped, and a 3-0 team became 3-1.

Asked about one of those fumbles specifically, and whether the player looked tentative, Kubiak gave the most professional answer available: he'd have to watch it on film, and he thought the Kansas City defender did a good job punching it out.

Notice he didn't throw his own player under the bus on a national microphone, and he also didn't pretend it didn't happen. That's the balance good coaches find, and bad ones never do.

"WE KICKED TOO MANY FIELD GOALS"

The quarterback's version of the loss was the sharpest summary anybody gave.

Cousins said the defense did a great job keeping them in the game, and then this: "We kicked too many field goals, didn't score enough points."

That's the game in nine words. The defense held up well enough to keep a lead for long stretches, and the offense turned drives into three points instead of seven.

And he connected it to the opponent in a way that I think every Raiders fan needs to internalize. He said if you make a mistake or if you don't take advantage of an opportunity you have, the Chiefs tend to make you pay for it, and then it becomes hard to win.

That's the difference between Kansas City and the three teams this team beat in September. Miami, the Chargers and New Orleans let the Raiders survive their mistakes. Kansas City converts them. The entire gap between 3-0 and being a real contender lives in that sentence.

He also made sure to protect his kicker on the way through, saying Matt Gay has been outstanding, that they've put him in tough spots with the length of his kicks, and that he keeps responding. That's true. Gay hit a career-long 59-yarder in New Orleans. The problem was never the kicker, it was the red zone.

"WE'RE GOING TO WIN THIS GAME"

Here's the detail from this presser that I think will follow Kubiak all season, and I love it.

Apparently at halftime he told his team they were going to win the game. A reporter pointed out that he has stayed away from bold proclamations since he arrived and asked what made him say it.

His answer was five words: "That's how I felt. So I just said what I felt."

And then a second reporter pushed further, with the best question of the night: it seems like you're not trying to convince this team it can compete, you're coaching it as though it already knows it's going to win. How important is that?

Kubiak's response: that's why you play the game, that's why you suit up, that's what they expect every week, they have really good players and talented coaches, and that's the expectation every week.

Think about what that does to a young roster. There's a version of coaching where you manage expectations downward to protect your players from disappointment, and it produces teams that hope to stay close. There's another version where the standard is simply stated as fact, and it produces teams that get annoyed when they lose to the Chiefs.

Cousins described the player's side of that perfectly. He said if you come in here trying to just hang around with a good football team, it may not pan out the way you want, so they felt they needed to attack. Then he immediately took the blame again, wishing there had been better execution by him in the moments that would have given them a chance to win.

"IT'S A NEW YEAR. IT'S A NEW SEASON."

One more exchange I have to highlight, because it was a loaded question and the answer was perfect.

A reporter asked whether, regardless of record, regardless of how well they play week to week, this team will spend the whole season fighting the notion that these are the same old Raiders.

Kubiak's answer: no, it's a new year, it's a new season, and they don't think about that going forward.

That's the correct answer and it's also a boundary. He is refusing to let a decade of franchise history become a weight his current players carry. Those young defenders didn't lose those games. Nakobe Dean and Quay Walker and Hezekiah Masses have no history with the Chiefs. The only people who keep bringing up the old Raiders are the ones outside the building, and he just told them he isn't playing that game.

THE TIGHT END ROOM, AND THE COACH NOBODY MENTIONS

A reporter noted that the tight end room accounted for well over 100 yards and two of the team's touchdowns, and asked about the trio.

Kubiak named them: Brock, Mike, Ian. He called them an impressive group, said Luke Steckel is an impressive coach, and said they count on them every week.

Two things there. First, the obvious: Brock Bowers and Michael Mayer and the third tight end carried this passing game on a night when the receiver room was depleted. That's the two-tight-end identity we've been tracking since the Mayer extension, and it produced a hundred-plus yards and two scores against one of the best defensive coordinators alive.

Second, and this is the part fans never talk about: he credited the position coach by name. Luke Steckel doesn't get mentioned on any broadcast, and the head coach made sure he got mentioned at the podium after a loss. That's how you build a staff that wants to work for you.

Rob Leonard got the same treatment. Asked about the defensive game plan, Kubiak said Leonard and his staff have been phenomenal throughout, and then immediately credited Kansas City for making a lot of big plays too.

THE BLITZ THAT GOT BURNED

One honest accounting from the defensive side. There was an all-out blitz at Mahomes that resulted in an explosive downfield play to Tyquan Thornton.

Kubiak's answer: again, they were going to be aggressive in this game, and sometimes you win those situations and sometimes it goes the other way, and that's part of being a competitor and doing what you feel is right in the moment.

I want to be fair about this, because it connects to everything we've said about this defense for a month. This unit's entire identity is pressure without blitzing: second in the league in pressure rate while blitzing on less than a fifth of its dropbacks. The one time they sold out against the one quarterback in football who punishes it most, it went the other way.

That's not a scheme failure. That's a calculated risk that lost. And the lesson for the next seven weeks is the one this defense already knew: get home with four, keep seven back, and make the quarterback wait.

"YOU FIND OUT WAY MORE ABOUT YOURSELF THROUGH ADVERSITY"

Asked what he hopes to see from this team in response to its first defeat, Kubiak said you find out way more about yourself through adversity, they have some adversity right now, and now they get a chance to go to the next opponent. That's New England.

That's the whole week in one answer, and it's why I'm not worried today.

Cousins said the same thing from the player's chair, and said it better than any analyst will this week. He said there's a lot to build on. He talked about process over outcome, about the habits they keep Monday through Saturday being what he actually pays attention to, because if you do the right things there you give yourself the best chance on Sunday. And then he said the line I'm going to be repeating all season: tough times don't last, tough people do. Protect the football, play through the smoke, and give yourself a chance at the end.

They had a chance at the end. They didn't capitalize. That's the honest version.

THE DETAIL THAT MATTERS MOST FOR 2028

And then, buried in an answer about learning from experience, Cousins said the thing that made me sit up.

He said he'll sometimes mention things to Fernando in the middle of a game. "Hey, you see that there?" Take note, because someday it's going to be you, and you're going to want to make sure you do that and not this.

Raider Nation, stop and appreciate what's happening there.

The first overall pick is standing on the sideline in his helmet, listening to the play calls going in, reciting them back to the quarterbacks coach. And the 38-year-old veteran in front of him is turning live NFL snaps into a lesson plan in real time, in the middle of a one-score game against Kansas City.

That is the single most valuable thing happening in this organization right now, and it doesn't show up in a box score. The team that beats Kansas City in 2029 is being built in those sideline conversations in October of 2026.

WHAT COUSINS SAID ABOUT HIS OWN BODY

One last thing, because it was easy to miss and it matters.

Cousins took a beating in this game. Asked about it physically, he credited the wellness team, said he trusts them to handle it, described the team as tough-minded, and then said something I'd never heard a quarterback say: movement is medicine. Getting out on the field and practicing and moving makes him feel better.

He also gave Steve Spagnuolo his due, calling him a tremendous defensive coordinator who does a great job with his rush, his coverage and his pressures, and noting how multiple that defense is.

Here's the practical point for the next seven weeks: a 38-year-old quarterback who absorbed that many hits behind an interior line missing its starting guard is the biggest injury risk on this roster. Every argument I've made about fixing the interior offensive line starts there. It isn't about Jeanty's yards per carry. It's about keeping the man who made NFL history in his first three games here upright through December.

WHERE THIS LEAVES US

So let's put the whole press conference together.

The head coach publicly took the blame for the end-of-game sequence. The quarterback refused to let him have it alone and took blame of his own. The run game was called out honestly, with the talent defended and the execution questioned. The turnover margin was named as the deciding factor. The coordinators and even the tight ends coach got credit by name. The "same old Raiders" narrative got shut down in one sentence. And the message about New England was out of his mouth within minutes of the final whistle.

Nobody made excuses. Nobody blamed officiating. Nobody pointed at a player.

Twenty-four hours ago I told you the most encouraging thing about this loss was that the head coach was furious. Today I'll go further: the anger turned into ownership in less than a day, and that's the part that actually predicts what happens next.

This team is 3-1 with a trip to New England, then Buffalo, the Rams, the Jets, San Francisco, Seattle and Denver. Nailor's in the protocol, Bech is on IR, and the interior line needs reps. Nobody is going to feel sorry for us.

But if the standard in that building is a head coach saying "I blew it" out loud and a quarterback losing sleep over five yards, I'll take my chances with the rest of the schedule.
