## THE MOVE: JAMARI THRASH IS A RAIDER

Here's the news. According to NFL insider Mike Garafolo, **Las Vegas is signing former Cleveland Browns wide receiver Jamari Thrash to the practice squad.**

Garafolo reported on X that Thrash was waived off injured reserve by Cleveland the previous month and is now healthy and joining a new team.

So let me give you the full profile, because most of you have never heard this name.

**He's 26 years old. He was selected in the fifth round of the 2024 NFL draft. He has appeared in 18 career games. And his speed is the thing that makes him genuinely intriguing.**

Eighteen games means he's not a complete unknown — he's been on an NFL field, he's taken NFL snaps, he knows what the speed of this league feels like. That matters more than you'd think for a player who might have to contribute on short notice.

## LET'S KEEP EXPECTATIONS HONEST

Now, the part where I refuse to oversell you something.

**This is a practice squad signing, not a guaranteed solution to the Raiders' problems at receiver.**

Practice squad means he is not on the 53-man roster. He cannot simply walk onto the field Sunday. He has to be elevated or signed to the active roster first, and before any of that he has to prove in practice that he knows what he's doing in this offense.

If you were hoping Spytek had landed a 1,200-yard receiver this morning, that didn't happen. I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

## BUT THE TIMING MAKES COMPLETE SENSE

Here's why I still think this is a smart, worthwhile move.

Las Vegas needs more depth at the position — urgently — especially with several players dealing with availability concerns. Adding Thrash gives this team another option to evaluate and potentially develop into a contributor.

And look at the specific trait: speed. We've spent weeks on this channel explaining that the one thing this offense cannot do is force a safety to stay deep. A 26-year-old with real speed is at least a *candidate* to be that threat, and he costs nothing to find out.

Remember Cody White. A practice squad receiver who got elevated on a Saturday, caught two passes, and turned them both into touchdowns. Remember Roman Hemby, promoted off the practice squad because Jeanty's foot wasn't right. This organization has repeatedly found real contributors at the bottom of its own roster.

Thrash could potentially earn an opportunity if the staff believes he's ready. But he'll have to prove himself in practice first — and that's the same standard everybody else in this building is being held to right now.

## THIS IS WHAT "DONE IT AGAIN" ACTUALLY MEANS

And here's the thesis of today's video, because I think most people are going to miss it.

Spytek has now made a series of small, cheap, targeted moves in the space of three weeks. A receiver signed to the practice squad for special teams depth. A running back claimed when the backfield thinned. A veteran receiver brought back. A rookie back promoted when Jeanty's foot flared up. And now Thrash.

**None of those moves generate a headline on their own. Together they describe a philosophy: patch every hole at the cheapest available price, keep every option open, and refuse to pay a premium out of panic.**

That's what "he's done it again" means. Not a splash. A pattern. And whether you love it or hate it depends entirely on whether you think this 3-2 team is a contender that needs to be aggressive, or a construction project that needs to protect its draft capital.

We'll come back to that question, because it's the one that decides this season.

## SO WHO IS ACTUALLY AVAILABLE ON SUNDAY?

Let's go through the receiver situation precisely, because there's real news here.

Kubiak said on October 12th that **he expected Jalen Nailor to return to practice during the week.** That's the $35 million receiver who missed New England in concussion protocol, and getting him back on the field is quietly the single biggest upgrade available to this offense this week.

He also discussed Dante Thornton Jr., who is now **entering the second week of his practice window to return from injured reserve.**

And Kubiak repeated the standard we talked about yesterday: **the player needs to compete in practice and demonstrate that he is ready.**

Raider Nation, that distinction matters enormously, and I want to make sure nobody misunderstands it. **Returning to practice does not automatically mean a player will be available Sunday.** The coaching staff has to evaluate his condition and his readiness before deciding whether he can contribute. Thornton is in week two of his window, which means the clock is running — but a clock running is not the same thing as a door opening.

## THE FALLBACK LIST

So here's the honest worst case.

If Nailor and Thornton aren't ready, the Raiders could have to rely on **Tre Tucker, Cody White, Dariq Young, Malik Benson and Devin Tomkins** as their available wide receivers against Buffalo.

Read that list again. One proven vertical threat in Tucker. One practice squad hero with five career catches this season and three touchdowns. And three players with almost no NFL production between them.

That is the entire receiving corps for a team trying to stop a two-game losing streak against the Buffalo Bills.

**That situation is exactly why adding depth matters**, particularly after an offensive performance that couldn't generate consistent momentum. Spytek isn't signing Thrash because he's bored. He's signing him because the math at that position has become genuinely dangerous.

## HOW A PRACTICE SQUAD RECEIVER ACTUALLY HELPS

Let me explain the mechanics, because this is where fans get frustrated for no reason.

A practice squad player can be elevated to the active roster for game day a limited number of times per season without being signed permanently. That's exactly how Cody White got on the field at SoFi, and it's how he caught those two touchdowns.

So the realistic path for Thrash looks like this: he spends this week learning the base concepts and showing the staff he can run the route tree, he probably watches Sunday from the sideline or isn't dressed at all, and then — if Nailor or Thornton has a setback, or if somebody gets hurt — he becomes an emergency option in Week Seven.

That's not glamorous. But in a season where this team has needed four different receivers to step up already, having one more professional in the building who knows the playbook is worth something real.

## AND THE TEAM MAY NOT STOP THERE

Now the bigger conversation, because Spytek has made it clear he isn't finished.

With the NFL trade deadline approaching on **November 10th**, a larger move remains a genuine possibility. When asked about upgrading the roster through a trade, Kubiak said, **"All things are being considered."**

Raider Nation, that's now the third consecutive week this head coach has publicly refused to close that door. "All options are being considered." "Everything is being evaluated." And now "all things are being considered."

Coaches have a hundred ways to shut a question down. He has not used a single one of them.

To be clear, that doesn't confirm the Raiders are negotiating a deal. What it tells you is that the organization is keeping its options open — and that nobody inside that building is pretending the receiver room is fine.

## THE FOUR NAMES, AND AN HONEST LOOK AT EACH

According to Fox Sports' Ralph Vacchiano, multiple NFL sources believe four receivers could potentially become available on the trade market: **Brian Thomas Jr. of Jacksonville, Chris Godwin Jr. of Tampa Bay, Quentin Johnston of the Chargers, and Jerry Jeudy of Cleveland.**

Let me take each one properly.

**Brian Thomas Jr.** would be intriguing for a team looking for size, athleticism and explosive playmaking. He's the highest-ceiling name on this list. But the Raiders would need to determine whether Jacksonville is even willing to move him and what compensation would be required — and the reporting so far has suggested a price in the second-round range with little interest in selling. My line hasn't changed: a third-rounder is a reasonable balance of risk and upside. A second is too much for a player having this kind of season.

**Chris Godwin Jr.** could offer experience and another established receiving option. He's the professional of the group — a man who knows how to win inside and outside and won't need six weeks to learn where to line up. But his contract, his health and his potential role would all need to be considered, and the Raiders would have to decide whether the cost fits both their immediate needs and their long-term plans. For a team that needs to survive twelve weeks, "established and reliable" has real value.

**Quentin Johnston** is a genuine option because of his physical tools and his ability to provide a different dimension to this passing game. He's big, and this offense has nobody who wins a contested ball on the boundary. His availability and asking price would depend entirely on whether the Chargers are willing to trade — and remember, that's a division rival at 0-5, which means the division tax applies exactly like it does with Khalil Mack.

**And then Jerry Jeudy**, whose name keeps appearing in every one of these conversations for a reason. The Raiders would need to evaluate his production, his contract situation and his fit in this offense. And the fit is the part I keep coming back to: Klint Kubiak was Denver's passing game coordinator and quarterbacks coach in 2022 while Jeudy was there. Nobody on this list has a cleaner connection to this staff.

**The caveat, as always: these are potential possibilities, not confirmed deals, and there is no guarantee any of these players will actually be available.** The Raiders would have to explore the market and figure out which options genuinely make sense.

## THE DAY-THREE QUOTE — AND THE PART PEOPLE SKIP

Vacchiano also reported comments from an NFL executive suggesting that **some receivers with size, speed and talent could be available for as little as a day-three draft pick.** The executive's reasoning: a change of scenery can sometimes help a player reach his potential.

That could create real opportunities for a team needing help at receiver, and it's the scenario I've been telling you to wait for all month.

But here's the nuance that almost every channel is going to skip, and I want you to have it: **that does not mean these four specific players would be available at that price.** Their teams will set the asking price based on contracts, production and interest from other clubs. The executive was describing a market condition, not quoting a price list.

In other words: cheap receivers will be available. The four best names on the board probably won't be among the cheap ones.

## THE QUESTION SPYTEK ACTUALLY HAS TO ANSWER

So for the Raiders, the key isn't "who's the biggest name." The key is identifying the type of receiver who would make the biggest difference.

Do they need **more speed to stretch the field**? A **bigger target who can win contested catches**? Or a **reliable route runner who can consistently get open and help sustain drives**?

And Raider Nation, I love this framing, so let me give you my actual answer.

**This offense needs the big contested-catch target most, and it needs the speed least.** Here's why. Tucker already provides the vertical element — he's shown it with a 119-yard afternoon and a 40-yard touchdown. What this offense has never had is someone who can win a jump ball on the sideline on third-and-eight, or a back-shoulder throw in the red zone where there's no separation available. Those are the throws Cousins makes best, and right now the only player on this roster who wins them is a tight end who's getting bracketed by two defenders.

That's why Thornton's 6-foot-5 frame matters. That's why Johnston's size is interesting. That's why I'd rank the profile over the name.

**The best move isn't necessarily the biggest name. It's the player who fits the offense, offers meaningful production, and can be acquired at a reasonable cost.** Put that on the wall of the war room.

## AND HERE'S THE WARNING

The front office also needs to remember that wide receiver isn't the only issue.

The offensive line struggled badly against New England, and **adding a receiver won't solve anything if the quarterback doesn't have enough time to throw.** You can sign the best route runner in football and it means nothing at 44.8 percent pressure.

Las Vegas needs to improve its overall offensive execution rather than relying on one new player to fix everything. Ideally, the Raiders get healthy contributions from Nailor and Thornton, give Thrash a chance to compete, keep evaluating the market, and move on a trade only if a genuinely suitable opportunity appears.

That's the disciplined version. And honestly? Based on the pattern of moves we just walked through, that looks exactly like what Spytek is doing.

## NOW THE HARD HALF: THE DEFENSE IS UNDER FIRE

Okay. Let's talk about what actually happened in Foxborough, because the receiver conversation is the pleasant one.

Las Vegas entered Week Five at 3-1 and left Gillette Stadium with a 45-17 defeat, dropping to 3-2. The final score was bad enough. **The bigger concern was how completely New England controlled that football game.**

**The Patriots finished with 536 total yards**, repeatedly finding weaknesses in this defense and taking advantage of a team that struggled to adjust.

And that's where defensive coordinator Rob Leonard enters this conversation.

Giving up 45 points and more than 500 yards raises serious questions about the defensive game plan, the communication, and the preparation. The Raiders struggled to disrupt the quarterback, struggled to close passing lanes, and could not prevent New England from consistently moving the chains.

## THE LINEBACKERS, HANDLED FAIRLY

**Linebackers Quay Walker and Nakobe Dean also had difficult afternoons as the Patriots attacked the middle of the field. Walker in particular struggled in coverage and against the run.**

Now, I want to be careful and fair here, because Nakobe Dean has been the best defensive player on this roster for five weeks and I did an entire episode arguing he's the heartbeat of this defense. One bad Sunday against a Super Bowl caliber offense doesn't erase 98 percent snap counts, league-leading tackle numbers and three fourth-quarter forced fumbles.

But the middle of the field was open all afternoon, and linebackers are responsible for the middle of the field. That's just true.

**And this isn't about blaming individual players.** When an offense repeatedly finds favorable matchups, the coaching staff has to recognize the problem and make adjustments. That's the job. Remember Kubiak's own answer when he was asked whether they adjusted the coverage help: "Obviously not."

## WHERE LEONARD ACTUALLY STANDS

So here's my honest assessment of the pressure on him.

**One bad performance doesn't automatically mean the entire system is broken.** This is the same coordinator whose unit leads the NFL in takeaways, held three straight opponents scoreless in the fourth quarter, and produced the best three-game defensive start this franchise has had since 2002.

But another outing like this would make these concerns much harder to dismiss. What this defense needs is better communication, more consistent execution, and an approach that can adapt mid-game when an opponent starts finding success.

**Rob Leonard now faces real pressure to prove this defense can respond.** That's not a hot-seat conversation — he's a 41-year-old first-time coordinator who's been excellent for most of this season. It's a credibility conversation, and Sunday is the test.

## THE OFFENSIVE LINE AND THE SPECIAL TEAMS

The line was the other major problem. **Spencer Burford, Tyler Linderbaum and Kolton Miller were all among the names under scrutiny** as Las Vegas struggled in protection and in the run game.

**The Raiders averaged just 2.1 yards per rushing attempt**, which made it impossible to establish balance or keep that defense guessing. And when a line can't create running lanes or protect long enough, the whole offense degrades: drives stall, the playbook becomes predictable, and the pressure on the quarterback multiplies. That's the circular trap we've described all week.

Special teams contributed too. **Matt Gay missed a field goal, and a costly punt positioning mistake handed New England another advantage.**

Those might seem minor next to a defensive collapse, but they change momentum and make a hard game harder. And for Gay specifically — he was 10 for 10 on the season and I called him the one certainty on this roster. When the surest thing you have finally misses, you know it was that kind of afternoon.

The Raiders simply didn't execute well enough in any phase. That's what Kubiak and his staff have to address before it becomes a pattern.

## THE ONE REAL BRIGHT SPOT

But there was a genuine bright spot, and it deserves its own section.

**Brock Bowers finished with eight receptions for 110 yards and a touchdown.**

In a 28-point loss, against an elite defense, with four receivers unavailable and the defense knowing exactly where the football was going — eight catches, 110 yards, a score.

That performance proves Las Vegas still has a playmaker capable of producing against the best competition in football. Even when this offense can't find a rhythm, Bowers remains a reliable option.

**But the Raiders cannot expect him to carry this offense alone.** They need better protection, a more effective running game, and more consistent contributions from everybody around him. His production is encouraging, but the real goal is turning individual performances into sustained drives and victories — and 110 yards from a tight end in a 45-17 loss is a statistic, not a result.

## ACCOUNTABILITY ONLY COUNTS IF IT TURNS INTO RESULTS

Kubiak faced hard questions after that defeat, and his willingness to accept responsibility publicly was a genuinely positive step. I've said so every time he's done it, and he's done it four or five times now.

**But accountability only matters if it leads to improvement.** That's the honest version.

The staff has to evaluate the defensive scheme, fix the communication problems, improve the offensive line and eliminate the special teams mistakes. Saying "that's on me" is the first step, not the whole job. The next call sheet is where we find out whether the confessions meant anything.

## WHERE THIS SEASON ACTUALLY STANDS

The season is far from over at 3-2. But the Raiders cannot afford to ignore what happened in New England, and the next performance is enormously important.

**A strong response would suggest that loss was an isolated failure. Another disappointing showing would increase the pressure on Rob Leonard, on the offensive line, and on this coaching staff as a whole.**

So that's where we are. The Patriots exposed serious problems with the defense, the offensive line, the running game and the special teams. And the signing of Jamari Thrash, plus the possibility of a trade, shows that this front office is actively looking for ways to strengthen the receiving group.

The priority now is turning evaluation into results. Leonard's defense has to respond. The line has to improve. Nailor has to get back on the field. And Kubiak has to make sure this entire team is better prepared than it was at 10 a.m. Pacific in Foxborough.

At 3-2, there is still a real season available here. But they have to learn from this loss quickly, because Buffalo is not a forgiving opponent and Allegiant Stadium is going to be loud with people who want answers.
