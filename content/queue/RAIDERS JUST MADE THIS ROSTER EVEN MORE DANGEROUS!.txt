The Rewatch: What the 27-13 Tape REALLY Showed — No Bowers, No Problem, a Defensive MVP Nobody's Talking About, and the Bech Moment 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, I did what I always do after a win: I calmed down, poured a coffee, and watched the ENTIRE game again — and I'm telling you, the rewatch was better than the live experience. Because the final score isn't even the most exciting part of this 27-13 victory. There was one thing that kept jumping off the tape, over and over, from the opening drive to the fourth quarter — something I honestly did NOT expect to see from this team in Week One. By the final whistle I was sitting there thinking: okay... this might actually be DIFFERENT. Today we're going deep — beyond the milestones we celebrated yesterday, into the details the box score hides: the distribution, the scheme, the defensive star nobody's giving enough credit, and the rookie moments that tell you where this season is heading.

THE THING NOBODY'S TALKING ABOUT

Here's the headline of the rewatch, and I'll say it plainly: the Raiders won this game with NO Brock Bowers, no monster passing day, no perfect defensive performance, and six penalties' worth of self-inflicted wounds — and it never really felt in doubt. Read that again. Coming into Sunday, everything about this team was theoretical: the new system, the new identity, the Kubiak blueprint — all talk until it met another NFL team. Then the opening drive happened, and theory became FILM: Kirk Cousins completed his first NINE passes, and the offense marched 16 plays and 93 yards over nearly nine minutes. That's not a lucky shot play. That's patience, conversions, pace control — an offense that walked in with a PLAN and executed it against live bullets.

And those first nine completions deserve their own sentence, because they reveal something about the coaching. Every NFL staff scripts its opening plays — the rehearsed sequence designed all week to attack specific looks. When your quarterback goes nine-for-nine through the script, it means the staff READ the opponent correctly days in advance: the looks Miami showed were the looks Kubiak prepared for, and the answers were already loaded. That's not quarterback brilliance or receiver heroics — that's PREPARATION showing up on tape. Last year's team never once looked pre-solved like that. This one did on its first drive of the season.

And the Bowers layer makes it more impressive, not less. Let's confirm what the tape showed: 89 did not play — the knee watch we've been covering kept him in street clothes. You cannot remove a player like that and expect someone to magically replace him one-for-one. So the Raiders didn't try. They DISTRIBUTED. Michael Mayer — whose opportunity window we called the moment the injury news broke — caught six passes for 32 yards and was woven through the entire game plan. Is he Bowers? No, and I won't insult you by pretending. But the offense used him like a real weapon, exactly as we projected. And then the moment that made me shout at my television: JACK BECH — touchdown, ON THE OPENING DRIVE. The X-factor we profiled two weeks ago, the second-rounder carrying the "not a starter" label, cashed the season's first receiving score by a wideout. He was quieter the rest of the day, sure — but that first strike mattered strategically, because it told Miami from drive one: you cannot key on one option. Nailor drew his targets, Tucker had a beautiful play wiped away by a penalty, and the theme kept repeating: pieces, everywhere, all involved. That's the offense we were promised.

THE JEANTY BLUEPRINT: NOT A RUNNING BACK — A PROBLEM

Now let's talk about HOW they used Ashton Jeanty, because the usage impressed me more than the numbers — and the numbers were 23 carries, 100-plus yards, six catches, two touchdowns from a man who was a genuine question mark days ago. The Raiders did not treat him like a running back who trots on for run downs. They treated him like a MATCHUP WEAPON. The tape shows him aligned all over the formation: split sequences with Mike Washington Jr. on the field at the SAME time — and Washington ripped off a big run in exactly that look. There's even a sequence where Jeanty lines up at FULLBACK with Connor Heyward behind him — a two-back stack that forces a defense to guess who's lead-blocking and who's carrying. There's a flip to Jeanty for a first down. Motion. Shifts. Play-action layered on all of it.

And understand the WHY, because this is pure Kubiak: the point isn't complexity for its own sake — it's forcing the defense to REACT instead of anticipate. Every pre-snap shift is a question Miami had to answer in four seconds; every wrong answer was a crease. When you have multiple credible ball-handlers on the field, the questions multiply. That's how a rookie-laden offense with a thin receiver room produced sustained drives against an NFL defense: they won before the snap. File this away, because better defenses will answer faster — but the design language is installed, and it's beautiful.

Quick word on the quarterback and the men in front of him: Cousins finished 21 of 30 for 160 and three scores, and the rewatch confirms both halves of yesterday's verdict — early-game surgical, second-half sloppy, with two forced interceptions that had no business being thrown. But the pocket itself? Clean, most of the day. The line gave him time to work through progressions, which let the run and pass feed each other instead of dumping everything on the quarterback's shoulders. And on the Mendoza question, let me give you the spectrum honestly: yesterday's national panel screamed "sooner the better." The calmer film-room take says the opposite — unless injury forces it, let the rookie sit ALL season behind a functioning bridge. My position stays where it's been planted since episode one, between the two: protect him, develop him, and let the season tell us — my Thanksgiving stamp isn't a demand, it's a forecast. What Sunday proved is that there's no EMERGENCY. That's all the fortress ever needed to prove.

THE DEFENSIVE MVP NOBODY'S TALKING ABOUT

Now the side of the ball that surprised me most — and a name I need Raider Nation to put some respect on. Everyone's celebrating Crosby's 70th-sack milestone, and they should. But run the tape back and the defensive MVP of this game was KWITY PAYE: six tackles, TWO sacks, two tackles for loss, and FOUR quarterback hits. Four! That's a game-wrecking stat line, and it's exactly the eruption we forecast in the stock report — remember the thesis: Paye is the quiet beneficiary of all the gravity around him. Sunday was the proof of concept: while Miami's protection worried about 98, number 51 lived in the backfield.

And the Willis story arc deserves its own chapter, because it played out EXACTLY like our preview said it would — with one scary beat in the middle. Early in the game, Willis escaped for about fifteen yards, and every Raiders fan flashed back to our "track meet" nightmare. But then the adjustments came, and the cage closed. Nakobe Dean was magnificent in that role — six tackles, five solo, a sack, two tackles for loss, a quarterback hit — using pure closing speed to erase the scramble lanes. The discipline visibly improved as the game went on: rush lanes tightened, spies stayed home, and Willis was forced to beat coverage from the pocket, which — 220 yards, one interception, five sacks later — he could not. And remember, this wasn't sell-out containment: with De'Von Achane's speed on the field, the defense had to balance everything, and it did.

The back end wrote its own headlines too. Jeremy Chinn played exactly the hybrid role the depth chart promised — living near the line, five tackles, and late in the game a momentum-snapping FORCED FUMBLE, popping the ball loose with the kind of violence this defense was starved for last year. And the rookie... oh, Raider Nation, the rookie. Trayden Stokes, in his first NFL start at free safety: an INTERCEPTION and two pass deflections — and he was a fingertip away from a second pick. The job we celebrated him winning two weeks ago? He didn't just hold it Sunday. He put the league on notice.

One more coaching detail I LOVED: with the game in control, Kubiak gave Maxx Crosby a five-or-six-play breather during a Miami possession — and the defense held with Malcolm Koonce out there, while Fatukasi made his presence felt inside and Thomas Booker stacked good reps. People will skip past that. Don't. A year ago this franchise ran Crosby into the ground on a torn meniscus for 935 snaps because it had no choice. Now? The depth is real enough to protect the superstar, and the superstar came back in fresh and nearly recorded a SAFETY. That's what roster-building looks like when it works — and it's one more nail in the trade rumor's coffin, because a defense this deep doesn't need to sell its heart.

And do the sack math with me, because it answers a worry we've carried for weeks. Five sacks total: two from Paye, one and a half from Crosby, one from Dean, the rest shared — pressure from the EDGE, the SECOND LEVEL, and the interior push that let them finish. That distribution matters enormously, because our standing concern about this front was that a thin tackle rotation would force Leonard into blitz-heavy, box-stacked football that exposes the young secondary. Week One said otherwise: the pressure came from winning individual matchups and coordinated fronts, not from selling out coverage. One game, one opponent, one data point — but the scariest version of this defense was always "pressure without paying for it," and for sixty minutes, that's exactly what the tape showed.

THE HIDDEN WEAPON: THE RETURN GAME

And give me a minute for special teams, because the rewatch says we might have a WEAPON here. The kick returns looked genuinely dangerous all afternoon — Dylan Laube, whose 50-yarder swung the game's biggest sequence, looked completely at home under the new kickoff rules, winning one-on-ones and making the first man miss. And Cody White — the practice-squad stash elevated for game day — nearly delivered the moment of the season: a return taken deep into Miami territory where he looked GONE, until Miami's fastest man ran him down with a touchdown-saving tackle. The points didn't come, but the field-position math did — and in a season where this offense will grind for every yard, a return unit that flips thirty yards a game is worth a win or two by December. Quietly, special teams might be this roster's most improved unit.

HONEST FILM: THE FIXES

Now the corrections list, because the film room doesn't do parades. Six penalties — including the one that erased Tucker's moment — kept giving Miami free outs. The two Cousins interceptions were both avoidable, both forced, and both the kind of gifts that elite teams convert into losses. There were at least two drives that should have ended in points and didn't. Add it up and here's the honest framing: the Raiders played a B-minus game and controlled a division-of-labor victory anyway. They didn't need one hero. They didn't need Bowers. They didn't need perfection. They got contributions from EVERYWHERE — offense, defense, special teams, bench, practice squad elevations — and that, more than any milestone, is the thing I did not expect to see in Week One. Complementary football isn't a highlight. It's a HABIT. And habits, good or bad, compound.

NEXT: THE HERBERT TEST

One game proves nothing — I need to see it AGAIN, and Week Two will tell us far more than Week One did. The Raiders fly to Los Angeles to face the Chargers, and the difficulty spikes immediately: Justin Herbert is a completely different caliber of passing test than anything Miami offered, and the Chargers are coming off a Week One loss — a wounded, motivated team in their own building, in a DIVISION game. The cage that held Malik Willis gets a much smarter prisoner. The secondary that bent-but-didn't-break gets a surgeon. And the offense that survived two giveaways will not survive them twice against that roster. That's not pessimism — that's the assignment. Win that one, and "this might be different" starts becoming "this IS different."
