## ONE SNAP, SEVENTY-SEVEN YARDS

I'll start with the play that swung the most yardage, because it set the tone for how this defense gets remembered.

Kenneth Walker's 77-yard touchdown came down to Kwity Paye failing to hold the edge.

And that's the basic job. Set it, keep your gap, force the back to cut inside where help is waiting. He didn't. And a good runner with blockers in front of him does not need a second invitation.

One missed responsibility, one explosive play, and suddenly Las Vegas is playing from behind in a game that was there for the taking. For that assignment, I'm giving it a D. It's one snap, and I'm not going to pretend Paye has been bad this season, because the man had a two-sack debut in silver and black. But one snap was all it took to move the entire margin of this football game.

**Grade: D on the assignment.**

## THE DETAIL NOBODY TALKED ABOUT

Now here's what makes that run more interesting, and it's one of the most underrated details of the entire afternoon.

It went away from Maxx Crosby.

Raider Nation, that isn't a coincidence. Offensive coordinators around this league are drawing up plays that deliberately avoid his side, because they know exactly what happens when you run toward him. Crosby finished with three tackles and kept getting home against Patrick Mahomes.

When your opponent's biggest play of the day is designed specifically to dodge you, that is the highest compliment a defender can receive in this sport.

I'd put him at an A-minus, and I don't care that the team lost. He is playing like one of the very best edge players in football, and the respect he commands is now a structural fact that every game plan has to account for.

**Grade: A-minus.**

## THE COVERAGE WIN A LOSS BURIES

The work on Travis Kelce deserves real credit, because this is exactly the kind of thing that disappears when you lose by three.

Taron Johnson and Jeremy Chinn spent a lot of the day on him, and between the two of them they largely kept him quiet.

Understand what that means. Kelce is still one of the most dangerous route runners alive and he's the player Mahomes turns to when a play breaks down and the structure dissolves. Limiting that production is a genuine defensive victory, and it required a corner and a safety communicating all afternoon without a single blown exchange on him.

**Grade: B-plus for both.**

## THE MOST OVERLOOKED GAME ON THE ROSTER

Inside, Adam Butler might have had the most overlooked performance on this entire team.

He recorded half a sack across 28 snaps and was reportedly graded as the best defensive tackle on the roster. And that matters more than usual this week, because Jonah Laulu was a healthy scratch, which meant Butler absorbed a bigger share of the interior snaps and handled them.

That's a veteran doing exactly what veterans are paid to do: when the rotation thins, the workload goes up and the quality doesn't drop.

**Grade: B-plus.**

I'll also mention Tommy Eichenberg, who played only 13 defensive snaps but got used heavily in run-stopping situations against Walker. The staff clearly trusts his instincts in those spots, and that trust shows up in the snap distribution.

And I want to be fair about the linebackers here: I'm not hanging Walker's big numbers on them. That play was about leverage and edge integrity, not about anything the second level did wrong.

Now the part of this tape that's harder to watch.

Eric Stokes allowed three catches for 57 yards on five targets. That's not a disaster line in isolation. But one of those catches was a third-down conversion to Xavier Worthy that kept a Kansas City drive alive, and the timing of it hurt badly in a game decided by a field goal.

**Grade: C-minus.**

Then there's the rookie. Hezekiah Masses gave up two touchdowns.

Two.

And I want to handle this the right way, because this is the same young man who had two interceptions at SoFi and won conference honors in September. Rookies are going to struggle against an offense like this one. He is learning the speed of this league in real time against the best quarterback of his generation. That's the context, and it's real.

But two scores allowed in a three-point loss is the kind of outing that makes a coaching staff pick up the phone. There's chatter that Darien Porter could see more playing time this week because of it, and I want to be completely clear: that is speculation, not a confirmed move. But after watching this tape, I wouldn't be shocked if that conversation is already happening inside the building.

## SPECIAL TEAMS: THE PART THAT FRUSTRATED ME MOST

Let me start with the good, because it deserves to come first.

Matt Gay was four for four on field goals and is a perfect 10 for 10 on the season. In a game decided by three points, a kicker who simply does not miss is a lifeline, and he's handing this team one every single Sunday.

**Grade: A. No notes.** He does his job, and he does it without drama.

Everything surrounding him is where this falls apart.

Dariq Young fumbled a kickoff return. Kansas City recovered and turned it straight into a touchdown that made the score 30-19 at that point. A special teams turnover in a game you lose by three isn't just a bad play. It might be the entire margin of defeat.

And it wasn't the only warning sign. On Kansas City's very first drive, the Chiefs went for it on fourth and nine and converted — and the Raiders unit was reportedly already drifting back as if a punt were coming before the snap.

Raider Nation, that is not a one-off mental slip. That's a communication problem and a preparation problem, and it lands on coaching just as much as on the men wearing the pads. Joe DeCamillis has real work ahead of him, and I don't think that's a controversial thing to say after studying this tape.

**Grade: D for the group.** The kicker is excellent. The rest of the operation needs a serious look.

## THE BACKFIELD, AND THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS MOST

This brings me to where the questions have been loudest all week.

Ashton Jeanty carried 21 times and had 23 total opportunities, compared to 10 touches for Washington Jr. on 11 opportunities. So no, this is not a committee. It is still Jeanty's backfield, and anyone telling you he's being phased out isn't looking at the usage.

But the number that should bother you far more than the carry count is this one.

**Jeanty faced roughly twice as many stacked boxes as Washington did.**

Sit with that for a second. Kansas City was loading up specifically to stop Jeanty, daring the Raiders to throw, and the Raiders kept handing him the football into traffic.

And league-wide, the Raiders have reportedly seen the second-most stacked boxes of any team in football.

That is not a fluke from one week. That is a pattern defenses identified weeks ago and are now leaning on every Sunday. Remember this number, because it's the key to everything in the final section of this video.

## THE OFFENSIVE LINE, AND WHERE I PUSH BACK

Which leads me to the offensive line, where I want to disagree with the analytics.

PFF reportedly graded Spencer Burford and Tyler Linderbaum as the Raiders' two best offensive players in this game. I watched the tape, and I just don't see it.

Linderbaum is the highest-paid center in the league, and he is a talented player. No question about that. But the best player on the field in a 30-27 loss where the run game got swallowed by stacked boxes? That's a tough grade to justify when you actually look at where the push wasn't coming from.

Advanced metrics are a tool, not gospel. Grading systems evaluate whether a lineman won his individual assignment, and a center can technically win his block while the run still dies because the front was numerically superior before the snap. This is a clean example of why you still need eyes on the film.

The group was functional in pass protection at times, and I'll give them that. But it was not the dominant unit the numbers wanted you to believe in.

**Grade: C.**

## SO HERE'S THE BLOCKBUSTER MOVE

Now let me bring this all the way home, because everything above was the setup for this.

Put the grades side by side. On one side: an elite tight end group, a dominant top pass rusher, strong coverage on Kelce from a corner and a safety, and a kicker who never misses. On the other: an offensive line that didn't deserve the grades it got, a rookie corner who had a rough night, a special teams unit that directly handed over points, and a run game that is being schemed into the ground by loaded boxes almost every week.

That's the real story of this loss. The Raiders did not get dominated. They beat themselves in specific, identifiable places while other players were doing very good work on the same field.

So which move would be genius?

Everybody's instinct is the defensive splash. Khalil Mack coming home. And I love that story, I spent an entire video on it, and if the price stays sane I'd still do it. But look at the grades again. Crosby is an A-minus. Butler is a B-plus. The Kelce coverage was a B-plus. The pass rush is not what lost this football game.

**The genius move is the receiver. And not for the reason most people think.**

Here's the insight the film gives you that the box score never will. The Raiders have faced the second-most stacked boxes in the entire league. Jeanty saw twice as many loaded fronts as his backup. The run game isn't broken because of the back and it isn't entirely broken because of the line — it's being erased by arithmetic. When a defense puts an extra body in the box, the offense is outnumbered before the ball is snapped, and no amount of effort fixes a numbers problem.

So how do you unstack a box?

You do not do it with a better handoff. You do not do it by benching your rookie running back. You do it by putting a receiver on the field that a defense is physically unwilling to leave uncovered — someone who forces a safety to stay deep and makes a coordinator choose between stopping the run and surviving the pass.

**That is why a true number one receiver is not a luxury addition for this team. It is the run game fix.**

And look at the cascade it creates. The safety has to back off, so the box empties, so Jeanty gets lighter fronts and his yards per carry climbs without his ankle having to do anything heroic. The line stops blocking outnumbered fronts, so suddenly those PFF grades actually mean something. Defenses can't bracket Bowers with multiple bodies anymore, so an A-grade tight end room gets even more dangerous. And Cousins gets a real outside target instead of threading everything into traffic on the final snap of a one-score game.

One addition. Four problems improved. That's what genius looks like in roster building — not the biggest name, the move with the most downstream effects.

Which is exactly why the names matter in this order: Nico Collins is the best player available if Houston's 0-4 start ever truly makes him gettable, Marvin Harrison Jr. is the best value on a conditional pick with escalators, and Jerry Jeudy is the most likely to actually happen given Cleveland's direction and Kubiak's history coordinating his passing game in Denver. Any of the three unstacks the box. That's the point.

## A QUICK EXPLAINER, BECAUSE THIS TERM GETS THROWN AROUND

Let me make sure everybody watching understands what a stacked box actually is, because it's about to be the most important phrase of this season.

The box is the area right behind the line of scrimmage where run defenders live — roughly from tackle to tackle, a few yards deep. A standard look puts six defenders in there against a normal offensive formation. A stacked box means a seventh, sometimes an eighth, meaning the defense has more bodies committed to stopping the run than the offense has blockers to account for them.

When that happens, someone comes free. Not because a lineman got beaten, but because there was nobody left to block him. Your center can execute a textbook combo block, your guard can win his one-on-one, and the play still dies at the line because an unaccounted-for defender arrived in the backfield.

Defenses only do this when they believe the throw won't hurt them. That's the whole transaction. A coordinator empties his secondary and gambles that your passing game can't punish him for it. For four weeks, defenses have been making that bet against Las Vegas and winning it.

And that's why the Jeanty debate has been framed wrong all week. People are arguing about touches and ankles and committees. The tape says the carries were fine and the fronts were not. You don't fix a numbers problem with effort. You fix it by making the gamble too expensive.

And watch the first third down of the second half. This team's clearest pattern of failure has been converting momentum into points after halftime, and that's where one erased field goal turned into a three-point loss.
