THE ROOMS THAT SURPRISED ME — IN A GOOD WAY

Let's start with the easiest position group to feel good about, because when I look at the tight ends, I honestly don't know how you ask for more. It obviously starts with Brock Bowers — and at this point we all understand exactly what kind of player the Raiders have there. Sixty-four catches as the focal point of every defensive game plan last season, and that was the DOWN year. But what makes this room special is that it doesn't stop with Bowers. Michael Mayer is right there next to him, and when you put those two together, you hand Klint Kubiak a full toolbox: twelve personnel that forces defenses to pick their poison, play-action with two legitimate seam threats, run formations that look identical to pass formations. You're not talking about one great tight end and a prayer behind him — you've got two guys you can legitimately BUILD around. And the depth holds up too: Carter Runyon, whom the Raiders clearly like enough to stash on short-term injured reserve rather than let walk, and the veteran Ian Thomas as another option. Fewest questions of any room on this roster — and knowing what Kubiak's system historically does with tight ends, this could become one of the core identities of the offense.

Running back is the same story, and you know we've been planting this flag all summer. Ashton Jeanty is the name everyone focuses on — understandably, because 1,321 scrimmage yards behind last year's broken line is franchise-cornerstone stuff, and Kubiak's smile about his Week 1 status is still the best news of the month. But Mike Washington Jr. is what makes this room genuinely interesting. What that man showed in August — the 53-yard house call, the efficient runs week after week — made him look like a NATURAL fit for this outside zone scheme, and suddenly this isn't an offense where everything has to flow through one back. Add Connor Heyward at fullback, the sledgehammer piece Kubiak's play-action game loves, and Dylan Laube's special-teams-plus-touches versatility, and you've got a room with different skill sets that all fit the same blueprint. Tight end and running back are the two groups where I look at this roster and think: there's something REAL to work with here.

THE DEFENSIVE FOUNDATIONS

Flip to defense, because linebacker deserves more attention than it gets. Nakobe Dean and Quay Walker give this group a legitimately exciting foundation — and I love the detail that the chemistry isn't theoretical: these two go back to GEORGIA together, back to the most dominant college defense of the decade. That familiarity shows up in the communication, in how they pass off routes, in how fast they play. Behind them you've got Seun Olubi — the signing we broke down when it happened — plus Cody Lindenberg and Tommy Eichenberg. Real options. And the versatility matters even more now, because Quay Walker could be asked to do a little extra off the edge after what happened to Kieran Crawford. That Achilles still stings — a rotation piece this defense would much rather have — but it's forcing a creativity that might accidentally become a feature.

Which brings us to the edge room itself. Having Maxx Crosby at the top changes EVERYTHING — protections tilt, game plans warp, other rushers eat. Kwity Paye brings the run-defense physicality that sets the tone on early downs. Patrick Johnson is a useful piece, Malcolm Koonce has an opportunity in front of him that he simply has to seize, and Sloan is developing on the practice squad. Is it thinner without Crawford? Absolutely. But I don't look at this room and see trouble — I see top-end talent to build around, with role players who need to prove they can support it consistently. And of course, there's the elephant in the room: if the Nolan Smith Jr. reports we covered yesterday land the way we all hope, this group jumps from "solid" to "genuinely scary" overnight. Keep your notifications on for that one.

Now the interior offensive line — and here's where things get more complicated. On paper, I like a lot of individual pieces. Tyler Linderbaum is a major presence at center; that $81 million wasn't charity, it was the foundation of the entire run game. Spencer Burford gives you a real option at left guard. Jackson Powers-Johnson won the right guard job in camp, exactly as we tracked all August — though Caleb Rogers keeps showing he can play, and that competition isn't fully cold. Atonio Mafi had a stronger offseason than people give him credit for, especially as a run blocker, even if the pass protection still wobbles. So the talent is there. My hesitation is the UNCERTAINTY: some inconsistency, the injury history with Powers-Johnson, and the fact that we still haven't seen the final combination play meaningful snaps together. Remember the number we flagged this week — seventeen preseason snaps for Trey Zuhn III at center, the only true backup behind Linderbaum. I don't think this is a bad line. I actually think it can become a STRENGTH. I'm just not signing the certificate before I see it survive real Sundays.

QUARTERBACKS AND TACKLES: CONFIDENCE, WITH CONDITIONS

The quarterback room is one of the most unusual situations on this roster — and by now, you know the shape of it. Kirk Cousins doesn't need to become a top-five quarterback for this to work; the Raiders need COMPETENT, professional play from a fifteen-year veteran while the bigger picture develops. And the bigger picture is Fernando Mendoza — the number one overall pick, the guy with moments that look franchise-altering and moments that look exactly like a rookie. Honestly? That's precisely what you should expect, and it's why the structure is right: Cousins starts, Mendoza develops without the fire drill, and Aidan O'Connell gives you legitimate insurance as the third — the proven backup we explained keeping was the correct move. This room isn't about where it ranks TODAY. It's about whether the Raiders built the right environment to extract the best version of Mendoza. I believe they have — and my Thanksgiving prediction is still stamped and dated.

Offensive tackle gives me both confidence and caution. Kolton Miller is the anchor — when healthy, a left tackle you trust with your quarterback's blindside and your season. The bigger question is around him, because DJ Glaze had stretches this offseason where he got picked at, and right tackle is where preseason pressure leaked. But I like the young talent stacking up behind this group: Trey Zuhn III has my attention, Charles Grant is another young lineman worth developing — and don't forget the stash we celebrated last week, Evan Neal sitting on the practice squad as the buy-low lottery ticket. It's the theme this roster keeps repeating: more depth than we've seen in years, but young depth that still has to prove it.

THE SECONDARY — AND THE LUTER FACTOR

That same description fits the secondary. At safety, Jeremy Chinn is the piece that makes it all work for me, because of the versatility — you can move him around, disguise him, and with Quay Walker taking edge snaps, I can absolutely see Chinn deployed closer to the box in certain packages. That flexibility is gold in Rob Leonard's scheme. Trayden Stokes winning the free safety job as a rookie remains one of camp's best stories, and behind them Dalton Johnson, Tristin McCollum, and Isaiah Pola-Mao give you depth options — with plenty still to prove.

Cornerback is similar. Eric Stokes has already shown what he brings since arriving in silver and black, and Taran Johnson has been a valuable addition. But then you get to Darien Porter, Jermod McCoy, and Hezekiah Masses, and you're relying on young players with real uncertainty attached. That's exactly why Darrell Luter Jr. matters more than his headline suggested — remember, he was the only player on waivers with MULTIPLE claims, five teams by the reporting we broke down yesterday. Don't assume he disappears at the bottom of the depth chart. There's a real possibility he plays more than people expect, and after the film session we did on his tackling, you know why I'm in.

Defensive tackle, though — this is where the roster starts looking legitimately THIN. I'm a big believer that championship defenses are strong up the middle, and I just don't know if the Raiders have enough there yet. The one great surprise has been Foley Fatukasi: he hasn't been in the building long, but the impact is obvious, and there's a real argument he's already the best defensive tackle on this roster — which says something good about him and something concerning about the room. Tonka Hemingway intrigues me, and Adam Butler is a solid third tackle. But if an injury hits this group in October, watch how fast the front office goes shopping.

THE ROOM THAT KEEPS ME UP AT NIGHT — AND THE MAN WHO FIXES IT

And that brings me to the position that concerns me more than any other: wide receiver. I like Tre Tucker — if I'm picking the one receiver I trust most right now, it's him. But the bigger question is what happens after that. Jalen Nailor is playing on that three-year, $35 million bet. Jack Bech enters year two with all the toughness and none of the guarantees. Dont'e Thornton Jr. is on injured reserve. Malik Benson was a sixth-round pick, and Dererick Young profiles as a special teamer. So who becomes THE GUY? Right now there's no obvious answer on the 53 — and that matters double when you remember the long-term plan is maximizing Fernando Mendoza.

This is why the reports feel so right: the front office diagnosed the same weakness we all did, and Spytek told us out loud he intends to fix it at the elite level.
