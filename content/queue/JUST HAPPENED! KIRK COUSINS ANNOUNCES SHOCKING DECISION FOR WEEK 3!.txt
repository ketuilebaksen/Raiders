One unit that just made a statement against one of the best quarterbacks in football. And one problem that got noticeably worse from Week One to Week Two, and that every defensive coordinator on our schedule has already noticed.

OVERREACTION ONE: CODY WHITE COULD BECOME A REAL WEAPON

Let's start with the name nobody had on their list.

If you'd told me before kickoff that Cody White would be one of the biggest stories of this 2-0 start, I would have asked you to explain exactly how that was supposed to happen. Now I'm paying attention.

Two catches against the Chargers. Both touchdowns. Both on third down. And on the film, it wasn't luck: the route craft was clean on both plays, and one of them was an outstanding contested grab in a moment where the Raiders desperately needed somebody to make a play.

But here's the detail that turns this from a fun story into something that might actually matter: White spent time with Klint Kubiak in Seattle last season.

That connection explains a lot. It explains why a receiver with seven offensive snaps was on the field in scoring situations at all. It explains why a first-year head coach calling his own plays trusted him with a third-down route in a game the Raiders had to have. When a coach already knows how a player works, how he studies, and how he runs a concept, that player doesn't need thirty snaps a game to be useful. He just needs the right five.

And think about what this offense needs. Bowers still hasn't played. Nailor had one catch for nine yards. There's a real hole on the perimeter. If White can consistently separate and win contested catches, the Raiders suddenly have another piece defenses have to account for, and it costs them nothing.

There's also a practical roster angle here that I want you to understand, because it could force a decision soon. A practice squad player can only be elevated to the game-day roster a limited number of times in a season before the team has to make a real choice and sign him to the 53. White has now produced in back-to-back weeks, counting the special teams play he made against Miami. That clock is ticking, and somebody else on this roster would have to come off to make room.

So here's my verdict on this overreaction: I'm not calling him a future number one receiver off two catches. But I'd absolutely put him on the 53-man roster. Sometimes the small roster decisions end up mattering more than the big ones, and this looks like one of them.

OVERREACTION TWO: THIS SECONDARY IS MAKING A STATEMENT

Now the defense, and this might be the most encouraging development of the entire start.

Remember the situation going in. Darien Porter, out with an injury. A secondary already thin, now missing a starter, on the road, against Justin Herbert. Every single one of us was nervous about it. We spent an entire pregame episode asking who would even line up opposite Eric Stokes.

Here's how it ended: Herbert finished with 183 net passing yards, one touchdown, and two interceptions. Both of those interceptions came from Hezekiah Masses.

Read that again. A young corner making a start on the road picked off Justin Herbert twice.

But I want to zoom out from Masses for a second, because the bigger story is the philosophy. Rob Leonard isn't asking these young defensive backs to survive. He's putting them in positions where they can attack the football. There's a difference between a coverage call that says "don't get beat" and one that says "here's your help, now go make a play." When you tell a young player the second one, you get interceptions instead of hesitation.

And they're doing it without living in blitz packages. This defense is generating pressure on more than half of opponent dropbacks while blitzing only about a quarter of the time. That means the extra bodies stay in coverage, quarterbacks hold the ball, and young defensive backs get chances at it.

Now the honest part. Two games is two games. And look at what's coming. Tyler Shough and the Saints this week, then Patrick Mahomes the week after. Those are two completely different kinds of tests. Shough's offense will try to find the young corner and attack him repeatedly. Mahomes will do something nobody has drawn up in any meeting room all week.

So the questions I'm watching are simple. Can Masses keep making plays when the whole league has seen this tape? Can these young defensive backs keep creating turnovers? And can this defense hold up without depending entirely on Maxx Crosby and the pass rush to bail out the coverage?

If the answer is yes, the Raiders don't just have a good defense. They have an opportunistic one, and opportunistic defenses win games their offense has no business winning.

OVERREACTION THREE: THE RUN GAME IS A REAL PROBLEM

And now the brakes, because this channel doesn't sell you a 2-0 start without the receipts.

The running game had a bad afternoon. Not a quiet one. A bad one. As a team, the Raiders averaged about two yards a carry. Ashton Jeanty carried it 21 times for 48 yards.

Compare that to Week One: more than 100 rushing yards, plus a major impact as a receiver with six catches and two touchdowns. That's a massive drop-off in seven days, and it wasn't only about Jeanty. We went through the film: the interior of the offensive line wasn't winning, Linderbaum didn't play to his standard, Miller had rough moments in the run game, and Burford and Powers-Johnson weren't good enough either.

Here's why I'm not shrugging this off. Every defensive coordinator left on our schedule just watched the Chargers do it. The recipe is on tape now: load the box, stay disciplined in your gaps, don't let the run game get started, and make Kirk Cousins beat you throwing.

And Kubiak's entire offense is built on the opposite of that. Two-back personnel, play action, manageable down-and-distance. All of it depends on the defense believing you can hurt them on the ground. If they stop believing, the play action loses its teeth, the third downs get longer, and a 38-year-old quarterback has to win games from behind the sticks against real pass rushes.

The good news is that this is exactly what September is for. There's time to adjust, time to find out whether the answer is scheme, personnel, or just better execution. Maybe that means more snaps for the backup back when Jeanty isn't finding it. Maybe it means different run concepts that attack the edge instead of the middle, where this line is struggling most. Maybe it means leaning on the passing game a little longer until Bowers is back and defenses have to spread out.

But this is the thing I'll be watching most closely on Sunday. Not the final score. The rushing chart.

And let me put one number on the table so you know what "fixed" looks like. If Jeanty finishes the Saints game somewhere around four yards a carry, with the offensive line generating movement on early downs, then Week Two was a bad day against a front that sold out to stop it. If he's under three again, this is a structural problem, and the front office has a decision to make about the interior of that offensive line before the trade deadline. Two data points make a line, and Sunday gives us the third.

THE CONTEXT: WHAT OUR WIN DID TO THEM

Before we get to the quarterback conversation, one quick piece of context, because it tells you how the rest of the league read Sunday.

The Chargers are now one of only four teams in NFL history to start 0-2 while facing two opponents that won three or fewer games the year before. Both of those opponents were us and Arizona. And the national panels were merciless about it: five turnovers in two weeks, an offensive line getting beaten, a passing game with drops everywhere, and a team that replaced both coordinators this offseason and looks worse on both sides of the ball.

The line that landed hardest was this one: Los Angeles has now been out-coached at home two weeks in a row by first-time head coaches, by teams that combined for a 6-28 record last season.

One of those first-time head coaches is ours, and he's still undefeated.

I bring this up for one reason. When the national media talks about the Chargers being a disappointment, some of that is our doing. Rob Leonard's pressure package made Justin Herbert uncomfortable all afternoon. Kubiak's offense scored on a receiver who was on the practice squad. That wasn't Los Angeles handing us a game. That was a coaching staff winning one.

THE MENDOZA CONVERSATION JUST CHANGED COMPLETELY

There's one more thing from the national coverage I have to bring you, because it's about the future of this franchise.

A former quarterback on one of those panels made the case that this is the best possible scenario for Fernando Mendoza, and he used an example that should make every Raiders fan feel good. Patrick Mahomes sat an entire season behind a veteran who was playing well and getting his team to the playoffs. People wondered whether that was a waste. Then Mahomes played, and it turned out fine. More than fine.

That's the case for what Kubiak and Spytek are doing. Cousins has six touchdown passes in two games and is playing winning football. Mendoza gets to watch a veteran operate an NFL offense from the sideline, absorb the details, and step in when he's actually ready instead of when the calendar says he should be.

And the same panelist made a second point I hadn't thought about, and I want to share it with you because it's the sharpest thing I heard all week. When a team starts a rookie quarterback before he's ready, everybody else's development slows down too. The offensive line has to compensate. The receivers have to adjust to a quarterback who isn't seeing it yet. The whole operation bends toward protecting one player. Right now, nothing on this roster is bending. The line, the receivers, the backs, and the defense are all just playing.

That's the luxury winning buys you. And when Mendoza does take over, he'll be walking into a functioning football team instead of a rescue mission.

SO ARE THE RAIDERS ACTUALLY THIS GOOD?

Let's finish with the big question.

Klint Kubiak has not lost a game as an NFL head coach. The Raiders have opened a season with two straight wins for the first time since 2021. That alone changes the entire atmosphere around this building.

But is everything fixed? No. Two games is a tiny sample. The run game has to become consistent. The offensive line has to create more on the ground. The young secondary has to prove the Chargers game wasn't one perfect afternoon. And our best offensive player still hasn't played a snap.

Which, by the way, is the most exciting sentence I get to say all week. Brock Bowers didn't play in either of these wins, and Kubiak said after the game that he's very close to returning. A 2-0 team that's about to add its best weapon is a different kind of problem for the rest of this league.

So here's where I land. Are the Raiders a great team? We don't know yet, and anybody telling you they're certain is selling something. Are they a real team? Yes. They're making plays on both sides of the ball, young players are stepping up, the coaching staff is adjusting in season, and Raider Nation has something it hasn't had in a long time at this point in a season: momentum.

Now the pressure changes shape. It's no longer about starting fast. It's about proving this is the beginning of something that lasts.
