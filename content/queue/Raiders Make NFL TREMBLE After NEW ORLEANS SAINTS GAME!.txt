IS TRE TUCKER THE WR1 NOW? And at 3-0 With Bowers Back, Does This Team Still Need Jerry Jeudy? 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, three weeks in, this offense has answered questions faster than any of us expected, and it has created a brand new one.

Tre Tucker has turned into a legitimate downfield threat. Brock Bowers came back and immediately caught ten passes for 116 yards. Cody White scored two touchdowns on seven snaps. Kirk Cousins has nine touchdown passes and this team is undefeated.

So here's the double question for today: is Tucker actually the number one receiver on this roster? And if he is, with Bowers healthy and the trade deadline coming on November 10th, does a 3-0 football team still need to go buy a veteran receiver like Jerry Jeudy?

I'm going to give you my honest answer on both, and I'm going to tell you where my own position has changed in the last seven days, because that's what happens when you follow the tape instead of your own takes.

I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. If you're a die-hard like me, hit that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we build toward 6,000 believers. Let's get into it.

FIRST, THE QUARTERBACK NOBODY WANTS TO CREDIT

Before the receivers, one more moment for Cousins, because his season is being underrated even by our own fan base.

At SoFi he went 19 of 29 for 253 yards, three touchdowns, one interception, and a passer rating of 113.1. That's not a game manager riding a good defense. That's a quarterback operating with rhythm and confidence. Then in New Orleans he threw three more touchdowns without an interception.

Three touchdown passes in each of his first two games, and the last Raiders quarterback to do that was Kenny Stabler. Think about the names who have played this position here since: Daryle Lamonica before him, Rich Gannon, Derek Carr. You have to go all the way back to the Snake.

And about that one interception at SoFi: it came on the play where Tucker was running wide open down the field and slipped. That's not a bad read. That's a receiver losing his footing on a ball that's a touchdown nine times out of ten. If anything, that throw should make you more confident in what Cousins is seeing.

THE CASE THAT TUCKER IS THE WR1

Now to the main event, and the case is real.

Against the Chargers: seven targets, five catches, and a 100-yard afternoon, reported as either 119 or 115 yards depending on the source. Plus a touchdown that had nothing to do with anybody falling asleep. No blown coverage, no miscommunication. He ran away from an NFL secondary in a straight foot race and won.

Here's why that specific kind of production matters more than a big receiving total. A receiver who wins with route running and technique can be schemed against. You bracket him, you double him, you take away his leverage and make him work for every inch. It's much harder to solve a player who is simply faster than your cornerback while he's in phase. There's no coaching answer for that. You either have a corner who can run with him or you don't, and if you don't, you have to send help.

That's where the ripple effect comes in. If Tucker keeps stacking these performances, defensive coordinators have to commit a safety to the top of the coverage, and everything underneath opens up: Bowers and Michael Mayer in the intermediate middle, Jack Bech in the slot, Ashton Jeanty out of the backfield, and more room for the run game when a safety is backpedaling instead of filling a gap.

So is he officially the WR1? My answer: functionally, yes, he's the most dangerous wide receiver on this roster right now. Formally, I want one more month. One game doesn't make a body of work, and a receiver whose value is tied to speed has to prove he can produce when a defense is specifically built to stop it. The next few weeks against better secondaries will tell us everything.

BUT HERE'S WHAT CHANGED IN NEW ORLEANS

And this is the part that reframes the entire receiver conversation.

Brock Bowers came back and caught ten passes for 116 yards and a touchdown. Ten. In his first game off a knee procedure.

That's not a complementary piece. That's the number one target on this offense, and it doesn't matter that he lines up at tight end. When the Raiders needed a play in a tough situation in that fourth quarter, the ball went to number 89, and New Orleans had no answer for him all afternoon.

So the honest picture is this: Bowers is the WR1 in function, Tucker is the WR1 on the outside, and the offense that scored 35 points in the Superdome had both of them on the field at the same time for the first time this season.

That changes the trade math completely, and I'll explain why.

THE CODY WHITE SQUEEZE

Quick note on Cody White before we get to Cleveland, because his situation just got harder in the best possible way.

He was elevated off the practice squad for the Chargers game, which tells you the team needed bodies at receiver. He caught two passes and both of them were touchdowns, on third down, 24 yards total. That's about as efficient as a receiving performance gets.

Is a touchdown on every other target sustainable? Of course not. Nobody scores at that rate long-term. But that's not the point. The point is that when you get an emergency elevation and convert both of your chances into the end zone, you make it very hard for a front office to quietly send you back down. He turned a temporary roster move into a real audition, and he passed it.

The complication is that Bowers is now healthy and the targets have to come from somewhere. That's the kind of problem winning teams have.

THE JEUDY FILE, UPDATED

Now Cleveland, and let's lay out the full picture.

Jerry Jeudy was the 15th overall pick, originally a Bronco, traded to Cleveland in 2024. That first season with the Browns was excellent: 90 catches, 1,229 yards, four touchdowns, a Pro Bowl selection, and sixth in the entire NFL in receiving yards. That earned him a three-year extension worth north of $50 million.

Then 2025 went the other way. Fifty catches, 602 yards, and ten drops charged to him. That's a real regression, the kind that makes a front office ask hard questions about fit and scheme.

And 2026 has gotten worse instead of better. Two catches for 26 yards in Week One. Then zero catches on a single target in Week Two. A receiver who was putting up 1,200-yard seasons two years ago now can't get the ball to find him.

Add the draft capital. Cleveland spent a first-round pick and a second-round pick on receivers this offseason. When a front office invests premium picks at a position, that's a signal about who it's building around, and it usually isn't the veteran who signed an extension two years earlier.

The framework that's been floated is a 2027 fourth-round pick from Las Vegas, upgrading to a third if Jeudy plays at least 65 percent of the Raiders' offensive snaps for the rest of the season. That's a sensible, team-friendly structure: cheap if he doesn't contribute, more expensive if he does. But Browns general manager Andrew Berry could just as easily decide he wants an outright third-round pick with no conditions, and that changes the entire calculation.

I'll say it again for the people who skip to the comments: no trade has happened, and none has been reported as agreed. This is a scenario, floated because the circumstances make sense on paper.

THE KUBIAK CONNECTION, AND WHY IT KEEPS MATTERING

There is one factor here that isn't speculation, and it's the reason I take this idea seriously at all. Klint Kubiak worked with Jeudy in 2022, when Kubiak was on the Denver staff.

We've now seen that pattern twice in three weeks. Cody White, who spent time with Kubiak in Seattle, gets seven snaps and scores twice. Jaxon Smith-Njigba took a major leap with Kubiak on that Seattle staff. Coaches who have unlocked a player before are usually the ones willing to bet on doing it again, and in the middle of a season, a receiver who already knows the concepts can contribute immediately instead of learning for a month.

Familiarity is the whole reason a midseason receiver trade can work at all.

WHERE MY POSITION HAS CHANGED

Now let me be straight with you, because a week ago on this channel I said yes to Jeudy at a conditional fourth without much hesitation. I'm adjusting, and here's why.

When I said that, Bowers hadn't played a snap. The receiver room's ceiling was Tucker having a good day and a practice squad player scoring twice. Now Bowers is back and immediately produced ten catches, Tucker has proven he can win a foot race against NFL corners, and this offense just put 35 points on the board, the most in this franchise's last 40 games.

So my updated position is this. At a conditional 2027 fourth-round pick, I still make the call. That's a lottery ticket, the player already knows the coach's system, and a contender can always use another professional who can win a matchup. If Cleveland wants an outright third, I now say no, and I'd take that same draft capital to the interior of the offensive line instead.

Because that's still the real hole. Linderbaum, Burford and Powers-Johnson all had rough afternoons at SoFi, and Jeanty went for 21 carries and 48 yards with a long of nine. Some of that is on the back, but a lot of it is on the blocking, and yards for a running back are often a product of what's happening in front of him. There's also DJ Glaze at right tackle, who allowed a sack and missed a run assignment in that game. Those are the issues that get magnified when the elite pass rushers still on this schedule show up, and Kansas City is next.

If Spytek has exactly one move in him before November 10th, I want it spent where this offense actually breaks.

WHAT A 3-0 START DOES TO THE PRICE OF EVERYTHING

There's a piece of deadline logic that I think gets missed in every one of these conversations, and it works against us.

Two weeks ago, the league looked at Las Vegas and saw a rebuilding team that might sell. Now it looks at a 3-0 team tied for first in the AFC West with Kansas City. That changes how every general manager negotiates with us. Sellers ask contenders for more, because contenders are the ones who feel urgency, and urgency is the most expensive thing in sports.

So winning has a cost at the deadline: it raises the price on everything we might want to buy. That's the trade-off nobody mentions when they tell us to go get a star.

The counter is that this front office has been remarkably disciplined about exactly that. Foley Fatukasi for the veteran minimum. Kwity Paye in free agency. Nolan Smith Jr. added to the edge rotation. Michael Mayer extended before the price went up. John Spytek has not paid list price for a single thing this year, and I don't expect him to start in November because a talk show told him to.

THE JEANTY REMINDER

One last thing, because I've seen some ugly comments about our running back and I'm not having it.

Twenty-one carries for 48 yards with three drops is a night anybody would want to forget. But one bad Sunday does not erase the vision and the traits that made him the player this franchise invested in. He ran for over 100 yards and caught two touchdowns in Week One. He's a rookie-contract back in his second season learning behind a line that's still finding itself.

You judge a running back over a body of work, not over one afternoon in Los Angeles. And with a rookie in Mike Washington Jr. now showing he can rip off a 35-yard touchdown run when the crease appears, this backfield has more ways to hurt you than it did a month ago.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Three games, three wins, a quarterback in the same sentence as Kenny Stabler, a rookie corner with two interceptions and a Player of the Week award, a tight end who came back and dominated, and a receiver who might be turning into a real number one.

The deadline is November 10th. This front office has earned the right to be a buyer. But the target should be the position that's actually costing us yards, not the one that's suddenly producing.
