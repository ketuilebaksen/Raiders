WHY NOLAN SMITH CHANGES THIS DEFENSE IMMEDIATELY

Let me explain exactly what this does, because it's bigger than adding a name to a depth chart.

Here's the problem every opponent has been solving against us. Maxx Crosby has drawn more double teams than any player in the league over the first two weeks of this season. Offenses slide their protection toward number 98, they chip him with a tight end, they help with a back, and they accept whatever happens on the other side of the formation.

That math is why Kwity Paye had two sacks and eight pressures in his debut against Miami. It's why Malcolm Koonce has turned into a closer since the staff moved him into pass-rush situations, with the strip sack that flipped the game in New Orleans. When the protection commits to one man, somebody else eats.

Now add Nolan Smith to that rotation. Suddenly a coordinator isn't choosing between Crosby and one other problem. He's choosing between Crosby, Paye, Koonce, and Smith, and he only has five blockers and one tight end to work with. There is no combination of help that covers everybody. Somebody rushes clean on every single third down.

And think about the specific matchup on Sunday. Kansas City's whole offense is built on timing and rhythm, on a quarterback who manipulates a pocket better than anybody who has ever played the position. The way you beat that is not by blitzing, because blitzing against Mahomes is how you end up on a highlight reel. The way you beat it is by getting home with four rushers and keeping seven defenders in coverage.

That's exactly what this defense already does. The Raiders rank second in the league in pressure rate at 36.3 percent while blitzing on only 19.5 percent of dropbacks. Smith makes that number more sustainable, because the fourth quarter of a game is where a fresh rusher turns a three-step throw into a sack.

There's a depth argument too, and it matters more than people think in October. Crosby is being managed this season. Kubiak was asked at the podium about Crosby taking snaps off during games, and the answer was that Crosby understands it's about being available for the whole season, and Max is about winning. You can only do that if the man replacing him on those snaps is a real NFL rusher instead of a body. Now he is.

Now, I'm not going to oversell this, because that's not what we do here.

A player joining a defense in Week Four doesn't get the full playbook. Leonard's system borrows heavily from what Mike Macdonald built in Seattle, with disguised fronts, line games, and pressure that changes after the snap. That takes time to learn. So I would expect a managed snap count on Sunday, with Smith used in clearly defined pass-rush situations rather than being asked to play every down and set a hard edge against Kenneth Walker.

And that's fine. That's how you use a new weapon correctly. Twelve to twenty snaps of pure rushing, aimed at the moments when Kansas City has to throw, is worth more than forty snaps of a player thinking instead of reacting.

But understand the signal this sends. A 3-0 team going out and adding a Super Bowl champion edge rusher before the biggest game of its season is a front office saying out loud: we are not waiting for next year. John Spytek has spent all season fixing holes early and cheaply, from Foley Fatukasi at the veteran minimum to Linderbaum at center, and this is the same move at a different position. Identify the weakness, fix it before it becomes a crisis.

Welcome to Las Vegas, Nolan. Your first assignment is the best quarterback of this generation. No pressure.

NOW THE AUDIT: THIS START IS REAL, BUT THE RECORD LIES A LITTLE

Okay. Let's be adults about the rest of it, because today I want to give you the honest version of this 3-0 start, the parts that are sustainable and the parts that aren't.

Start with the receipts, because the record does lie a little. Miami, the Chargers, and New Orleans own a combined record of 1-8, and Las Vegas handed out three of those losses. I'm not going to crown a team for beating that group, and neither should you.

But the national rankings can't ignore it anymore either. NFL.com jumped the Raiders from 22nd to 10th. ESPN moved them from 20th to 14th. Nick Shook of NFL.com wrote that he might be overreacting to the Raiders' hot start, and then put them in his top ten anyway.

So which is it? Let's go to the arithmetic, because that's where this gets interesting.

THE TAKEAWAY MATH THAT EXPLAINS EVERYTHING

Here's the single most important set of numbers in this entire episode.

In 2025, the Raiders forced 16 takeaways across 17 games. Fewer than one per game. At that pace, this defense would have about three takeaways after three weeks.

It has nine. The most in the league, and the most for this franchise through three games since 2002.

That's a transformation, not a trend. And it's the reason this team is 3-0 instead of 2-1 or 1-2. Subtract the six extra takeaways from last year's pace and the New Orleans win disappears entirely, because that game turned completely on late turnovers.

Now here's the honest companion stat, and I'm not going to hide from it. Turnover luck swings back over a long season. Nobody sustains three takeaways a game for seventeen weeks. Some of those forced fumbles bounce to the other team. Some of those interceptions get dropped. That's football.

But there's a reason I think a meaningful part of this is repeatable rather than random: takeaways follow pressure, and pressure is a skill. A quarterback who has a defender in his face throws early, throws off-platform, and holds the ball when he shouldn't. That's how Koonce's strip sack happened. That's how Masses got in front of a throw. When your front is second in the league in pressure rate while barely blitzing, takeaways aren't an accident, they're a byproduct.

THE SCORING LEAP IS THE OTHER HALF

Now the offense, and this number is almost hard to believe.

Last season, the Raiders averaged 14.2 points per game. Dead last in the NFL. This season they're averaging 29.3 points per game. That's more than double.

Think about what doubling your scoring output in one offseason actually requires. A new head coach calling plays. A quarterback who gets the ball out on time. An offensive line with a real center. A tight end who commands thirteen targets in a game. And a scheme that creates easy completions instead of asking a quarterback to manufacture everything himself.

For a franchise that finished last in scoring, the 29.3 is every bit as remarkable as the 3-0.

THE THREE CRACKS HIDING INSIDE 3-0

Here's where I have to be the adult in the room, because there are three real problems inside this start, and Kansas City is built to exploit all three.

Crack one: New Orleans moved the ball at will. The Saints piled up 381 total yards and 148 rushing yards. They went four for four in the red zone. Tyler Shough threw four touchdown passes, every single one to a tight end. For three quarters, that was their football game, and only the takeaways saved it.

Crack two: the timing of those takeaways. Las Vegas did not force a single turnover in New Orleans until the fourth quarter. A defense that spends three quarters bending and then produces four takeaways in fifteen minutes is not a defense you can plan around. Against Mahomes, bending for three quarters usually means you're down by 17 before your takeaways arrive.

Crack three: the receiver room. Not a single wide receiver on this roster averages even four catches per game. Think about that. Tre Tucker has had his big afternoon, Cody White has three touchdowns on three catches, but there is no volume receiver, no player a defense has to game-plan for across sixty minutes. And now Jack Bech is on injured reserve with a fractured forearm.

Those cracks are real. They're also exactly why the way these wins happened is so interesting, so let's walk through all three games.

WEEK ONE: THE TEMPLATE

It started at home against Miami, and it established the formula that's still holding.

Las Vegas built a 27-13 lead through three quarters and then shut the Dolphins out the rest of the way. Miami converted only two of its ten third downs. Malik Willis spent the entire afternoon behind the chains, which is the polite way of saying this defense made every down a problem.

And understand the context: a new coordinator, new linebackers, new calls, new everything, and that unit looked organized from the opening snap. Organization in Week One is a coaching achievement, not a talent achievement. For a franchise coming off a 3-14 season holding the first overall pick, that clean win mattered more than the margin.

WEEK TWO: BREAKING TWO CURSES AT ONCE

Then came the first real road test, at SoFi Stadium, where the Raiders had lost five straight games in Los Angeles and four straight in the series.

They won 26-14 and ended both streaks in one afternoon.

Justin Herbert threw for only 192 yards, took three sacks, and threw two interceptions, and both of those interceptions went to the same rookie we'll get to in a minute. And Cousins made a throw in that game that deserves its own monument: a touchdown to Cody White on third-and-ten in the red zone that tracking data gave only a 33.7 percent chance of being completed.

Think about what that number means. Two out of three times, that pass falls incomplete. The receiver was a practice squad elevation, the situation was third-and-long in the red zone on the road, and the quarterback threw it anyway because he trusted what he saw. That's not game management. That's a veteran believing in his guy.

WEEK THREE: THE GAME THAT CHANGED THE CONVERSATION

Week Three is where this stopped looking like a soft schedule.

Shough threw four touchdowns. New Orleans was perfect in the red zone. The Raiders trailed by 11 points in the second half inside one of the loudest buildings in football.

And then the defense took the ball away on each of the Saints' final four possessions. Las Vegas closed the game on a 19-0 run and won 35-27.

Here's the stat from that game that I can't stop thinking about. Through three games, this defense has not allowed a single point in a fourth quarter. Not one. That matches the longest such streak to start a season in franchise history.

Three fourth quarters, zero points allowed, in the league that invented the comeback. Whatever else you want to argue about this start, that number is not luck. That's a team that has learned how to finish, and finishing is the exact thing this franchise forgot how to do for the last three years.

THE EXITS THAT MADE ROOM FOR ALL OF THIS

To understand the rebuild, you have to look at who left.

Pete Carroll lasted one season and went 3-14. Chip Kelly was fired as offensive coordinator 11 games into 2025, and ESPN noted that the Raiders averaged just 74.9 rushing yards per game that year, the fewest in a season over the past 25 years. Twenty-five years. Geno Smith was traded to the Jets after leading the league with 17 interceptions and absorbing 55 sacks. Defensive coordinator Patrick Graham left for Pittsburgh. And receiver Jakobi Meyers had already been sent to Jacksonville.

None of those departures hurt on the field. Look at the numbers attached to each of them: last in scoring, worst rushing output in a quarter century, league-leading interceptions. That wasn't a roster being stripped. That was a building being cleared out.

But there's one exit that almost happened, and it would have changed everything.

THE TRADE THAT DIED ON A MEDICAL TABLE

On March 6th, the Raiders agreed to send Maxx Crosby to Baltimore for the 14th pick in the 2026 draft and a 2027 first-round pick.

This wasn't a rumor. It was agreed. Crosby flew to Baltimore. He posted that he was in town. He took a physical and an MRI on March 10th.

And that night, the Ravens backed out over his left knee, which surgeons had repaired in January to fix a torn meniscus. Crosby flew home to Las Vegas on a private plane. A blockbuster that looked finished on a Friday was dead by Tuesday night.

Sit with how close that was. Two first-round picks. The best player on this roster, gone, and a rebuild extended by two more years. One medical exam in Baltimore is the only reason the player drawing the most double teams in the NFL is still wearing silver and black.

And look at what keeping him has produced. Crosby has 17 pressures and two sacks through three games despite that double-team volume, and because protections slide toward him, everybody else gets cleaner rushes, which is how this defense sits second in pressure rate while blitzing less than a fifth of the time.

His own description of the spring was simple: this has been a long road to recovery.

Two first-round picks would have helped a rebuild. Keeping the engine of this pass rush may have ended one.

THE ARRIVALS: A COACHING STAFF NOBODY SAW COMING

Now the other side of the ledger, starting at the top.

Klint Kubiak spent 2025 calling plays for Seattle, won Super Bowl 60, and was named head coach of the Raiders on February 9th. General manager John Spytek and minority owner Tom Brady led that search. He is 39 years old, a rookie head coach, and he just started 3-0.

His first big decision was promoting defensive line coach Rob Leonard to defensive coordinator: a 41-year-old first-time coordinator running a system that borrows heavily from what Mike Macdonald built in Seattle. Two first-year coaches, one shared language, and a roster that bought in from the opening week. That's the part nobody predicted. New systems usually take half a season to install. This one looked finished in September.

The quarterback decision looked like a stopgap at the time. Kirk Cousins arrived in April after two seasons in Atlanta, 38 years old, with the first overall pick sitting behind him.

Through three games: 67.4 percent completions, 661 yards, nine touchdowns, three interceptions, and a 107.2 passer rating. He's tied for the league lead in touchdown passes. And here's the detail that explains the fast start: he already knows this offense, because he ran a version of it with Kubiak in Minnesota.

After New Orleans, Kubiak told the Associated Press that Cousins has the experience, he has the maturity, and he's making plays.

And on a historical note that I think gets underrated: no quarterback in NFL history had thrown three touchdown passes in each of his first three games with a new team. Not one, in the entire history of this sport, until Kirk Cousins did it in silver and black.

THE ARRIVALS: WHERE THE MONEY WENT

The defensive money went to speed.

Nakobe Dean signed for three years and $36 million. Quay Walker came over from Green Bay. The former Georgia teammates now lead this team with 27 and 24 tackles, and Dean wears the green dot, relaying every call. That's why this defense plays faster than its talent level: the communication is clean, and you can see it on tape.

Kwity Paye debuted with two sacks and eight pressures against Miami, becoming the first Raider other than Crosby with eight pressures in a game since Malcolm Koonce on Christmas Day 2023. Read that again. This franchise went nearly three years without a non-Crosby pass rusher having that kind of afternoon.

On the offensive line, center Tyler Linderbaum signed for three years and $81 million, and the difference between last season's interior and this one is the difference between 14.2 points a game and 29.3.

Free agency built the floor. The draft is building something stranger.

THE DRAFT CLASS THAT'S ALREADY FORCING DECISIONS

Las Vegas made ten selections in April, and the most famous one still hasn't started a game.

Fernando Mendoza, the Heisman winner who led Indiana to an undefeated national title, went first overall and sits behind Cousins. Kubiak said all offseason that he preferred a veteran to start while a young quarterback learns, and so far the plan is working perfectly. The case against it is simple and it gets louder every week: the longer Cousins wins, the harder the question of when Mendoza plays becomes to answer.

Two other picks are already forcing decisions.

Mike Washington Jr., a fourth-rounder from Arkansas taken at pick 122, has 102 yards on 15 carries. That's 6.8 yards a carry, including the 36-yard touchdown run in New Orleans. Meanwhile Ashton Jeanty, the sixth pick of the 2025 draft, is averaging 3.3 yards per carry on 63 attempts.

That gap is too wide to ignore much longer, and Kubiak already acknowledged it at the podium, saying Washington "definitely earned more opportunities." I'll say what I've been saying: give Jeanty 15 or 16 carries and Washington eight to 10, keep both fresh, and let Jeanty keep doing the things he genuinely does well, which are catching passes and protecting the quarterback.

Second-round safety Trayden Stokes, taken at pick 38, intercepted a pass in his debut, missed Week Three with a concussion, and has now cleared protocol for Sunday. His return is the single most important health update of this week, because the middle of the field was wide open without him.

And then there's pick 175.

PICK 175: THE HONEST VERSION

Hezekiah Masses, a rookie cornerback out of California, picked off Justin Herbert twice in Week Two, becoming the first Raiders rookie with a multi-interception game since Henry Williams in 1979, and he won AFC Defensive Player of the Week. He added a third interception against the Saints and is now tied with Jevon Holland of the Giants for the league lead.

One hundred and seventy-four players were valued more highly than him in April. Kubiak stood at a podium this week and called him, with no qualifiers, "our starting corner," even with Darien Porter healthy.

Now the part that most channels will skip, and I'm not going to, because you deserve the full picture. The tape in New Orleans was less flattering than the box score. Pro Football Focus graded that game poorly for him. He allowed catches on all four of his targets. And the interception itself came after Koonce's hit on Shough, which means the pressure created the opportunity as much as the coverage did.

That's not me tearing down a rookie. That's me telling you what to watch on Sunday. Three interceptions in three games is phenomenal and genuinely changes this defense. Allowing catches on every target against a good passing game is a warning that Kansas City will have studied. Both things are true, and the second one is the reason Stokes coming back matters so much: a young corner playing aggressively needs a real center fielder behind him.

THE KANSAS CITY TWIST NOBODY ELSE IS TELLING YOU

And now the storyline that makes Sunday so strange.

Kansas City arrives at 3-0, and they bring a weapon that Klint Kubiak knows better than almost anyone alive, because last February he was the one calling that player's number on the biggest stage in the sport.

Kenneth Walker was Kubiak's running back in Seattle last season, on the team that won Super Bowl 60. Kubiak built game plans around him. He knows how Walker cuts, how he reads a hole, what he does when he's fresh and what he does when he's tired. He has probably drawn up fifty plays designed to maximize this exact running back.

And now he has to stop him.

That's why the word out of the building this week is that the number one objective on the whiteboard isn't Mahomes. It's Walker, who has 65 carries for 360 yards, 19 runs that produced first downs, 440 total yards from scrimmage and four touchdowns. Take Walker away and the safeties stay deep. Leave him alone and the safeties come down, and that's exactly when Mahomes turns one play into seven points.

Unresolved: the interior offensive line, where Jackson Powers-Johnson missed practice with a groin injury and both guards have been inconsistent. And the receiver room, where nobody averages four catches a game and Bech is on IR.
