Raider Nation, the season doesn't turn on the Kansas City loss. It turns on the next seven days.

Here's where we are. The Raiders are 3-1. They handed sole possession of the AFC West back to the Chiefs. And the most important thing that happened all weekend wasn't on the field, it was at the podium, where Klint Kubiak walked out looking like a man who'd lost a Super Bowl instead of a man sitting at 3-1 in year one of a rebuild.

Think about that for a second. This franchise won three games across all of last season. Three, in seventeen weeks. They matched that number in four weeks this year. And the head coach was furious.

That's not a bad sign. That's a program that has stopped measuring itself against rock bottom.

So today isn't another autopsy. We've done the autopsy. Today is about the response: the five things that have to get fixed before this team walks into New England, the specific diagnostic questions this coaching staff has to answer this week, the receiver reinforcements that are eligible to arrive right now, and the exact signals that would tell me to start actually worrying.

## THE REACTION MATTERS ALMOST AS MUCH AS THE RESULT

Let me stay on Kubiak for one more minute, because I think this is the single most underrated story of the season.

He wasn't measured. He wasn't philosophical. He wasn't giving us the coachspeak this organization has specialized in for years, the kind where a three-win season gets wrapped in calm, even tones like it's all just part of a process. This was raw. This was a head coach who fully expected to win that football game and was angry his team didn't close it.

And then, within twenty-four hours, that anger turned into ownership. On the end-of-game sequence, he said he didn't get the play call to the quarterback fast enough, called it a total missed opportunity, and said "I blew it." Four words that head coaches in this league have an entire vocabulary designed to avoid.

Now pair that with what Maxx Crosby said before the Chiefs game. He talked about needing to earn a full silver and black crowd in that stadium, and he made a point of saying that being 3-0 at the time still meant there was a lot of work to do.

That's a veteran leader setting exactly the tone his head coach set at the podium. Nobody in that building is treating three wins, or now three wins and a loss, as a finish line.

A coach who's livid at 3-1 and a star defender who refuses to accept a hot start as an accomplishment: that's the profile of a team that believes it should win every week it steps on the field. It is not the profile of a rebuilding team stumbling pleasantly through a transition year.

## FIX ONE: THE INTERIOR OFFENSIVE LINE

Now the checklist, in order of importance, and this one isn't close.

Jackson Powers-Johnson missed another practice with the groin injury and didn't play against Kansas City. That forced Caleb Rogers into the first start of his career at guard. First career start, against a Kansas City front that specializes in generating interior pressure and blowing up a run game before it starts.

You cannot replace a starting interior lineman with a player making his first career start and expect the same push, especially not against a divisional opponent with that much continuity on its own roster.

Here's why I rank this first: it is the single most fixable problem on the entire list, because it isn't a scheme problem and it isn't a talent problem. It's a health and continuity problem.

Get Powers-Johnson back on the practice field this week and evaluate honestly whether he's ready to start. If he isn't, then Rogers needs a full week of reps with the starting unit instead of being dropped cold into a start against one of the better fronts in football. And Cesar Ruiz, who's now in the building, needs those same reps, because an interior lineman's value is almost entirely about the five men beside him knowing exactly what he's going to do.

Cousins gave the best technical explanation of this at the podium. He talked about getting the combo blocks right, about getting better at them as the group works together longer, and about this being a constant build that happens with more time on task. A combo block is two linemen working in tandem on a defensive tackle before one of them climbs to a linebacker. It is pure timing, and timing is built in practice, not in a meeting.

Fix the interior and everything downstream improves: the run game numbers, the play-action game, the deep shots, and the health of a 38-year-old quarterback who took a beating on Sunday.

## FIX TWO: THIRD DOWN ON DEFENSE

This is the highest-leverage adjustment available to this coaching staff, and here's why.

This defense has been genuinely impressive. It ranks near the top of the league in pressure rate, and it's doing it without blitzing often, which tells you the front is winning one-on-one matchups on a down-to-down basis. That's real talent, not scheme trickery. This is also a top-ten scoring defense allowing right around 18 points a game.

But against Kansas City on third down specifically, that pressure didn't translate into enough stops. The Chiefs found ways to extend drives, and extended drives against Patrick Mahomes are close to a death sentence for any defense in this league.

So the diagnostic questions this staff has to answer in the film room this week are specific, and I'd love to hear the answers:

Was it coverage on the back end, with somebody losing a route in zone? Was it Mahomes extending plays longer than any quarterback on the schedule so far, which is a real possibility given who he is? Or did Kansas City's offensive line simply solve the pressure package better than Miami, the Chargers and New Orleans did?

Those three answers lead to three completely different fixes. The first is a communication issue, and Trayden Stokes being back from the concussion protocol helps it immediately. The second is a containment and rush-lane discipline issue. The third is a scheme issue that means Rob Leonard needs new wrinkles.

Whatever the answer, the structural strength is still there. Small improvements on third down turn a top-ten defense into a genuinely elite one, and this unit is close enough that it's worth obsessing over.

## FIX THREE: SETTLE THE RUN GAME ROTATION

Let's be blunt about the backfield, because the head coach already was.

Ashton Jeanty has been getting his ankles taped up during games, which is a small red flag worth watching, and he's averaging right around three yards a carry on the season. That isn't good enough from a top-ten pick, and I say that as somebody who still believes in the player.

Meanwhile Mike Washington Jr. is averaging close to seven yards a carry on limited touches, including the 36-yard touchdown run against New Orleans. And Kubiak himself said after that game that Washington had earned more opportunities.

When your head coach is on record saying a backup running back deserves more snaps, and your lead back is still averaging under three and a half yards an attempt through four games, that isn't fan speculation anymore. That's a coaching staff being handed a decision.

So here's what I expect, and what I want: Washington's touches climb, Jeanty's workload gets managed more carefully both for performance reasons and because of whatever is going on with those ankles, and this backfield becomes a rotation instead of a hierarchy. Fifteen or sixteen carries for Jeanty, eight to ten for Washington, and let the fresher legs find the crease.

That isn't a demotion. That's how you keep a 2025 top-ten pick healthy through December while getting more explosive plays out of the position.

## FIX FOUR: RED ZONE EFFICIENCY

We haven't talked about this enough, and it's the quietest reason this loss happened.

Cousins' own summary of the game was nine words: "We kicked too many field goals, didn't score enough points." That's it. That's the whole thing.

Any offense leaning this heavily on Brock Bowers and on explosive plays from a thin receiver room has to be nearly perfect once it crosses the twenty. Cousins' remarkably clean start gives this offense margin for error everywhere else on the field, and that margin disappears in the red zone when drives stall into field goals instead of touchdowns.

Against Miami, the Chargers and New Orleans, three points instead of seven was survivable. Against Kansas City it was fatal, and against Buffalo and San Francisco later on this schedule it'll be fatal too.

This is also where the two-tight-end identity has to pay off. Bowers and Michael Mayer on the field together, in a 20-yard space, against a defense that can't put enough big bodies out there: that's the matchup this front office paid for when it gave Mayer three years and $45 million. Use it.

## FIX FIVE: PENALTY DISCIPLINE

Remember the twelve-men-on-the-field penalty against the Chargers earlier this season? The Raiders survived that one only because Nakobe Dean forced a fumble on the very next play.

You will not get that lucky every time.

Sloppy, correctable penalties are exactly the kind of self-inflicted wound that turns a close loss into a blowout against a team like New England or Buffalo. And this matters more for this specific team than most, because discipline has genuinely been one of its strengths: the Raiders have been among the cleanest teams in the league in penalty yardage, which is a cultural flex in year one under a new staff.

Protect that identity. It's one of the few areas where this roster already has an edge on better opponents.

## THE TWO THINGS THAT WILL REGRESS, AND WHY THAT'S OKAY

Now let me name two things that showed up in this loss that I don't think belong on a fix list, because they're math rather than mistakes.

**The fourth-quarter streak.** This team had not allowed a single fourth-quarter point through three straight games to start the season, the longest such streak in franchise history. For a roster that national projections pegged at six or seven wins, three straight games of fourth-quarter lockdown defense isn't something cheap teams do. It's something disciplined, well-coached defenses do.

That streak ended against Kansas City. And it ended against arguably the best fourth-quarter quarterback of this entire era. If you're going to lose that streak to somebody, losing it to Mahomes in a shootout is not remotely the same thing as losing it to a bad team because your defense got gassed.

**The takeaway machine.** Nine takeaways through three games led the entire NFL. Compare that to last season's full total of 16 across 17 games. This defense was on pace to obliterate that number.

Against Kansas City, the machine stalled. No team forces turnovers at a historic rate every single week; that's just regression to the mean doing what it does. But it's still worth naming as a factor, because when you're not winning the turnover battle against Mahomes, you're playing from behind in the margins all afternoon.

The lesson isn't "force more fumbles," because you can't scheme luck. The lesson is fix number two: get off the field on third down so you don't need a turnover to survive a drive.

## THE RECEIVER ROOM: REINFORCEMENTS ARE ELIGIBLE RIGHT NOW

Here's the problem that's been building for weeks, and the reason I keep circling back to it.

Jack Bech is on injured reserve with a fractured forearm. Jalen Nailor is in the concussion protocol. And before this loss, no Raiders wide receiver was averaging even four catches per game. Four.

Tre Tucker had that huge 119-yard afternoon at SoFi. Cody White has three touchdown catches on three total receptions, which is both unsustainable and genuinely fun. But outside of a couple of explosive plays, this receiver room has not been deep enough to consistently move the chains.

And the clearest evidence of that is where this offense's center of gravity sits: Brock Bowers was named AFC Offensive Player of the Week heading into the Chiefs game. The engine of this passing attack is a tight end, a running back, and whatever Kirk Cousins can create off schedule. It is not a receiver corps imposing itself on anybody's secondary.

Here's the optimism, and it isn't speculation, it's roster mechanics: Carter Renan and Dante Thornton Jr. are both eligible to return from short-term injured reserve right around Week Five. That's now. If either one comes back healthy and productive, the math on this room changes immediately, and Thornton is a 6-foot-5 rookie with a 4.35 forty who nobody on this schedule has had to prepare for yet.

Beyond that, there's growing chatter about the Raiders looking at receiver help before the November 10th trade deadline, with names like Jerry Jeudy, Xavier Legette and Marvin Harrison Jr. coming up. I want to be clear, as always: none of that has been reported as close or likely. It's speculation. But the fact that it keeps circulating tells you the league sees the same hole we see, and this front office has about a month to decide whether to address it.

My position hasn't moved: at a conditional late-round pick, you make the call. At a premium price, you spend that capital on the interior offensive line instead.

## DON'T LOSE SIGHT OF WHAT COUSINS HAS DONE

It would be easy to pile on this offense after a loss, and I'm not going to, because what Kirk Cousins has done here deserves to be said plainly.

Nine touchdown passes through his first three games with a brand new franchise, with a start so clean that one accounting had him turnover-free across that stretch. By multiple measures, that opening was an NFL first for a quarterback with a new team.

That is not a system quarterback managing a conservative game plan. That's genuine high-level quarterback play from a man plenty of outside voices had already written off. And then in the Chiefs game he threw for 365 yards on 52 attempts against Steve Spagnuolo while taking a beating behind a patched-up interior line.

Remember who was saying what about this team before Sunday. Stephen A. Smith predicted the hot start was eventually going to fall apart. Fox Sports had the Raiders ranked 20th while they were sitting undefeated. National preseason projections had this roster pegged for six or seven wins.

A three-point loss to the Chiefs does not validate any of that. If anything, a team that was supposedly a bottom-ten roster just went toe to toe with the standard of the conference in a winnable football game. The honest revision to those projections after this result is upward, not downward.

## THE CONTEXT THIS TEAM IS PLAYING FROM

Let me re-anchor everybody, because a 3-1 fan base arguing about a fourth-down call has forgotten where this started.

Last season: 3-14. A head coach fired after one year in the middle of a disaster. The franchise quarterback traded away. The first overall pick in the draft as the consolation prize. It was a complete teardown.

Four games into year one of the rebuild, this team is 3-1 with a top-ten scoring defense allowing around 18 points a game, the best tight end in football, a rookie corner with three interceptions and a Defensive Player of the Week award at pick 175, a linebacker in the Fred Warner conversation, and a head coach who is livid after losing to the defending division champs.

That is not a transition year. That's a team that arrived early.

## WHERE REAL CONCERN WOULD START

Now let me be honest about the other side, because not every mistake deserves panic and not every mistake deserves a shrug.

Here's exactly what would worry me, and I'm writing it down so we can check it together next Sunday.

If this team comes out flat against New England. If there's any hint of finger-pointing in that locker room. If the interior offensive line communication problem with Rogers and Powers-Johnson turns into a recurring issue instead of a one-game anomaly. If players start looking like they've lost confidence in the game plan, or if the young defensive backs start playing scared instead of aggressive.

Those are the symptoms of the team we watched last season, and I'd tell you immediately if I saw them.

None of that has happened. One frustrated head coach after a tough loss to a championship-level opponent isn't a crisis. It's a data point.

## THE ROAD AHEAD, AND THE HONEST MATH

Let's finish with the schedule, because it's brutal and it's also not impossible.

New England is next, on the road. Then Buffalo, the Rams, the Jets, San Francisco, Seattle and Denver. Nobody on that list is going to feel sorry for a 3-1 team with a thin receiver room.

But here's the math I keep coming back to. A stretch finishing anywhere from 4-4 to 5-3 over those next eight games would represent a genuinely successful season for a franchise that won three total games a year ago. Four and four puts this team at 7-5 with the finish in front of it. Five and three makes it a December contender in a wide-open AFC.

And one more date for your calendar: these two teams meet again in Week 18, the final week of the regular season. Depending on how things shake out, that rematch could carry enormous playoff weight. After a game decided by special teams mistakes rather than a talent gap, you have every reason to believe this team can settle that score.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's where I land, Raider Nation.

The anger Kubiak showed after this loss isn't a sign this team is falling apart. It's a sign this team has stopped accepting mediocrity as the baseline. The same head coach who called his own mistake out loud in front of the city is the one who told his team at halftime that they were going to win the game, because that's what he actually believed.

Fix the interior line. Tighten up third down. Settle the run game rotation. Clean up the red zone. Cut out the dumb penalties.

Do those five things, and this loss in Kansas City ends up looking like exactly what I believe it is: the week a young team found out precisely how far away the standard is, and then went and closed the distance.

New England on Sunday. That's the whole story now.
