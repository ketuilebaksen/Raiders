OFFENSE — THE WINNERS

Winner: Mike Washington Jr. Start with the one that might surprise you, because he's not the man with the gaudy numbers: seven carries, 41 yards — just under six every time he touched it. And what that did goes way beyond the stat sheet: it kept Jeanty FRESH, and it gave this staff a legitimate one-two backfield punch from the season's first drive. Now marry it to the identity stat: per the NextGen data, the Raiders ran 21 personnel — two backs, a tight end, two receivers — on 24 of 67 snaps, just under 36 percent. Out of that grouping ALONE: 154 yards, 6.4 per play, and BOTH of Jeanty's touchdowns. That's not a wrinkle. That's scheme flexibility that wins November and December games, installed in September.

Winner: Jack Bech. Three catches, 23 yards — numbers that don't jump off the page — and the MOMENT that does: a 15-yard touchdown from Cousins on the season's very first scoring drive. This is the second-rounder we called the X-factor, the one who needed to show up in the red zone, and on drive one of the year he did precisely that — then spent the rest of the day throwing the early-down blocks this offense is built on. Complementary piece, delivered. First deposit, banked.

Winner: Michael Mayer. Now the full contract details are out — THREE years, FORTY-FIVE million, signed on the eve of the opener — and the pressure walking into Sunday was enormous. Six catches for 32 yards won't dazzle you, so here's the context that should: across the five games this franchise has now played without Brock Bowers — four last season plus this one — Mayer has caught 24 of 31 targets for 228 yards and a touchdown. That's not flashy. That's DEPENDABLE — and dependable is what let this staff run its actual game plan, keep the two-tight-end and heavy packages alive, instead of scrambling to replace a superstar with a committee. Worth every dollar, proven within 24 hours of signing. You love to see it.

Winner: the offensive line — all five, plus the professor. Raider Nation, sit with this one, because it's been YEARS: Rick Dennison's group — Kolton Miller, Spencer Burford, Tyler Linderbaum, Jackson Powers-Johnson, DJ Glaze — gave this team a competent, physical, honest-to-goodness NFL offensive line. The advanced numbers are beautiful: a pressure rate allowed of just 29 percent, THIRD lowest in the entire league in Week One — against a Miami defense that blitzed at the eighth-highest rate in football. Read those two numbers together: they threw extra rushers, and it didn't matter. Miller and Glaze were outstanding bookends on tape. Zero sacks allowed. The fault line we feared most is, for one week at least, a foundation.

Biggest offensive winner: Ashton Jeanty. It genuinely came down to a coin flip with the line, but the kid gets the nod for what he did through the AIR: six catches, 45 yards, two receiving touchdowns stacked on 102 rushing yards. And here's the stat that deserves national attention: this was the second time in his young career Jeanty has posted 100 rushing yards with multiple receiving touchdowns in the same game — putting him in company with CHRISTIAN McCAFFREY and BRIAN WESTBROOK among the most such games in NFL history. Barely a season into his career, and he's keeping company with the most dynamic dual-threat backs the sport has produced. The real deal isn't a debate anymore. It's a record book.

OFFENSE — THE LOSERS

Loser: Kirk Cousins. I don't love writing it after a three-touchdown win, but the ledger doesn't lie: 21 of 30, 160 yards, and two genuinely bad interceptions in a game scripted perfectly for him — big early lead, dominant ground game, a defense there for the taking. No, this is not a benching conversation — I'm not making that argument and neither should you. But free possessions are a luxury tax this offense can only afford against rebuilding teams, and the schedule stops being charitable THIS week.

Loser: the receiver room — by design. Here's the uncomfortable usage chart: not one Raiders wideout saw six targets. Tucker: two catches, 27 yards. Nailor: three for 27. Benson: one for six, plus an end-around stuffed for minus-eight. The staff built the entire attack through the backs and tight ends, using receivers as motion decoys more than weapons — and honestly? That's a diagnosis, not an accident. This coaching staff is funneling the passing game away from this room until it EARNS a bigger role. Bech's touchdown was a start. It needs successors.

Biggest offensive loser: the deep ball. You know the split by heart now: over 90 percent completions at ten yards and under... one of SEVEN beyond. Every coordinator in the league just downloaded the same free scouting report: dare Kirk Cousins downfield and squat on everything short. Until somebody makes a defense pay over the top, every underneath window this offense lives in gets smaller by the week. Fix it with scheme, fix it with Bowers' return, fix it with a certain trade board — but FIX it.

DEFENSE — THE WINNERS

Winner: Foley Fatukasi. The name casual fans slept on: roughly 18 or 19 rotational snaps, and in that sliver — half a sack, four tackles, a quarterback hit, and a batted pass he nearly intercepted himself. Remember when the depth chart listed him behind Booker and we said don't overread DT labels, it's a rotation? This is what we meant: the room's best player might be "buried" third on paper and first on impact. By December, don't be shocked if Fatukasi is the name this fan base says most about the interior.

Winner: Trayden Stokes — into the record book. The rookie's third-quarter interception of Malik Willis wasn't just a great play; per Raiders PR, it made him the first Raiders rookie with a defensive interception in his NFL DEBUT since Vann McElroy in NINETEEN EIGHTY-TWO — just the sixth rookie in franchise history to do it. Sit with that list for a second: this franchise has existed since 1960, has employed some of the greatest defensive backs the sport has ever seen, and only five rookies before him ever debuted with a pick. And remember the draft-day discourse: plenty of analysts wanted a receiver in that spot. Instead, Vegas took the rangy ballhawk — and after one week, the free safety job, the trap-coverage interception we diagrammed yesterday, and a 44-year-old record all belong to him. The pop list said watch T.Stokes. The record book said welcome.

Winner: Nakobe Dean — the green dot. Confirmed: Dean wears the GREEN DOT, the defense's on-field quarterback — relaying calls, setting fronts, orchestrating everything we broke down in the film room. The stat line held up its end: six tackles, two for loss, a sack, a quarterback hit. Now zoom out on the degree of difficulty: new team, new scheme, new coordinator, new position coach — all at once — while commanding a unit young enough to wobble. It didn't wobble. It allowed 259 total yards and played four clean quarters. Leadership showed up in the box score AND between snaps.

Winner: Maxx Crosby — with the asterisk that should thrill you. Sunday may have been his lowest snap count in years — by DESIGN, exactly the managed-workload plan the staff promised — and he still played like a wrecking ball: 1.5 sacks that easily could've been two and a half plus a safety with friendlier bounces. Fewer snaps, same terror. That's not decline management, that's January preservation — and it only works because the depth behind him is finally real.

Biggest defensive winner: Kwity Paye. The full stat line: six tackles, two sacks, two tackles for loss, four quarterback hits. And now the number that made my jaw drop: Crosby and Paye COMBINED for 16 pressures on Miami's 37 dropbacks — out of 27 total team pressures. Two men. More than half the entire defense's heat. When this front office wrote Paye that $48 million check back in March, plenty of people squinted at it. Rob Leonard and defensive line coach Travis Smith just deployed him exactly as designed — and Sunday, the check cashed itself. The stock report told you: Paye was the quiet winner of the offseason. He's not quiet anymore.

Honorable mentions, because this defense was too deep for five names: Chinn's forced fumble — scooped up by Quay Walker — buried Miami's last real comeback hope. Rookie Hezekiah Masses stepped in for the injured Darien Porter and held his own in extended action — August's fourth corner, September's answer. And Thomas Booker flashed in his role up front. When your depth chart's third and fourth names make winning plays in Week One, that's not luck. That's roster construction.

DEFENSE — THE LOSERS

Fairness first: a defense that allowed 13 points and forced two takeaways has earned gentle criticism. But honestly: the interior line depth behind the top rotation still needs more consistent push — a young room finding its identity, exactly as our warning light said. And Porter's injury exposed how quickly the corner depth gets tested; Masses passed audition one, but Herbert is a different examination board entirely. Coaching points, not alarms — noted before a much tougher test.

SPECIAL TEAMS — QUIETLY THE CLEANEST PHASE

Matt Gay: PERFECT — every field goal, every extra point. Dylan Laube: over 47 yards per kickoff return on his two chances, the 50-yarder his longest since his very first career return in 2024 — a legitimate weapon under the new rules. And then the game ball nobody saw coming: CODY WHITE. When Miami ripped off a 79-yard kickoff return that had Allegiant holding its breath, White chased the returner down from BEHIND and shoved him out at the 18. Miami missed the ensuing field goal — and never threatened again. If that man scores, it's a one-possession game and a different afternoon. Hustle, on special teams, from a practice-squad elevation: that's culture you can screenshot.

And AJ COLE, Raider Nation — appreciate this man properly. Vegas didn't punt until the FOURTH quarter; when they finally did, it sailed 60-plus, and later he pinned Miami inside their own five to effectively seal the game. Two-time first-team All-Pro, holder of the NFL's career punting average record among anyone with 250-plus punts — statistically, maybe the most efficient punter this league has ever seen, flipping the field exactly when asked. Punters are people too. Elite ones are WEAPONS.

THE FULL PICTURE

So there's your ledger: Jeanty and the rebuilt line headlining the offense, with Washington, Bech, and the freshly-paid Mayer patching the Bowers hole; Cousins' giveaways, an underfed receiver room, and a missing deep ball on the debit side. On defense: a record-setting rookie, a green-dot general, a managed superstar, and a $48 million edge duo that produced over half the team's pressure — with depth notes filed honestly. And on special teams: perfection, weapons, and a game-saving chase. Not a perfect game. Never needed to be. For the first time in years, across all three phases, this was a DISCIPLINED, well-coached, genuinely dangerous football team — and Sunday in Los Angeles, against a desperate Chargers side, we find out if the ledger travels.
