THE HEADLINE: TRAYDEN STOKES WINS FREE SAFETY

To me, the biggest development on the entire chart is Trayden Stokes — OFFICIALLY the starting free safety, over the veteran Isaiah Pola-Mao. We saw this coming over the last couple of weeks, but there's a difference between a rookie getting first-team reps and a coaching staff printing his name at the top, and I love what this placement tells us about the vision. Remember the journey: early in the offseason there was real experimentation — slot work, off-ball alignments like that Arizona usage, debates about his long-term home. But watch his tape and one trait screams: RANGE. Chinn and Pola-Mao are valuable players, but neither is a true rangy post safety — the center fielder who erases deep mistakes. Stokes is. Putting him on the back end is simply the cleanest fit for everyone, and now it's official.

So what about Pola-Mao? Don't read "backup" as "buried" — injuries and matchups will get him real snaps. There's even a rumor floating that Pittsburgh has trade interest, and I'll give you my honest position: I'd keep the depth. This secondary is too young to be selling its experienced insurance in September for a late pick. And zoom out: after all the moving pieces this staff experimented with back there — Stokes, Masses, Pola-Mao, Chinn rotating through different looks — it finally feels like they've settled on a combination they genuinely believe in. That's what good coaching staffs do in August: try everything, then commit.

And understand the scheme ripple, because a true rangy free safety isn't just one position upgraded — he's a PERMISSION SLIP for the whole defense. When Rob Leonard trusts his post safety to erase the deep middle alone, everything else gets more aggressive: Chinn can live closer to the box where his violence matters, the corners can press knowing there's a safety net behind them, and the blitz menu — the heartbeat of this 3-4 — expands, because single-high coverage stops being a gamble and starts being a lifestyle. That's why this "rookie wins a job" story is bigger than one name on a chart. Trayden Stokes at free safety is the key that unlocks the version of this defense Leonard has been drawing on the whiteboard since January.

THE SURPRISE: DARIEN PORTER GETS FIRST CRACK

The starter that surprised me most? Darien Porter, printed at the outside corner spot across from Eric Stokes. Stokes was a lock. Taran Johnson had the slot secured. But Porter had to FIGHT for this — battling Jermod McCoy, Hezekiah Masses, and the room all summer — and the staff just handed him the first crack. Real credit to him.

Now the honest part: this is a pencil job, not permanent marker. The corner group beyond Eric Stokes still carries real questions, and this spot will be watched from the first defensive series of the season. If Porter plays well — fantastic, the Raiders found something with his length and speed profile. If he wobbles, the line behind him is genuinely hungry: McCoy could push by October, Masses keeps stealing practice reps, and don't forget Darrell Luter Jr. — the waiver claim FIVE teams wanted — getting more comfortable in Leonard's scheme every week. That's why I love this first depth chart: it gives us the first answer, never the final one. Competition doesn't end when the chart prints. It just gets scoreboard consequences.

Put the two decisions together and here's my honest feeling: this defensive backfield looks BETTER settled than I feared in June. Eric Stokes and Porter outside, Taran Johnson in the slot, Trayden Stokes patrolling deep, Chinn as the movable chess piece — that's close to the best-case version of how these pieces could have landed, with enough options behind the one wild card that nobody's stuck if Plan A stumbles. On paper, deeper AND more flexible. Now paper meets Sundays.

THE FRONT: LABELS, WRINKLES, AND ONE EYEBROW-RAISER

Up front, Tonka Hemingway being listed as a starting defensive tackle next to Adam Butler makes it official — the young interior piece won real trust this summer. And a detail for my scheme nerds: the chart labels Maxx Crosby a defensive END while Kwity Paye carries the EDGE tag. Maybe that's just paperwork; maybe it hints at how Leonard defines their roles — Crosby heavier-handed at the point, Paye more movable. Either way, it matches the film we watched all August. And of course, hovering over this whole front is the Smith arrival we opened with — when he lands, these labels get a whole lot scarier.

Here's the eyebrow-raiser, though: Thomas Booker IV is listed AHEAD of Foley Fatukasi in the interior pecking order, with Jonah Laulu rounding out the depth. Now, before anyone panics about the summer's best defensive surprise getting "demoted" — don't overread defensive tackle labels. That position is situations, not slots: early downs, goal line, passing downs, nose looks, one-tech, three-tech, endless combinations. Fatukasi will play plenty. But I'm NOTING the placement, because it suggests what we've hoped: a real rotation is forming inside instead of two men carrying the whole load — with JJ Pegues still developing further down. After all our warnings about this room's thinness, a genuine rotation is the best-case outcome.

Linebacker brought zero drama: Nakobe Dean and Quay Walker starting — the Georgia core, exactly as expected — with Cody Lindenberg possibly nosing ahead of Tommy Eichenberg for the third spot. The name I'm tracking there is Walker, because his best football is still AHEAD of him: the size, strength, and violence arrived years ago, and the processing keeps climbing. In this scheme, with this front absorbing blockers — and possibly a new Georgia brother arriving off the edge — Walker's ceiling season might be this one.

Now the consistency standard we talked about this week applies weekly.
