## THE OFFER: "I'M AVAILABLE"

Here's what happened.

**Former Raiders running back Raheem Mostert has publicly offered to return to Las Vegas.**

During an appearance on the show *Just Football*, Mostert was asked about the possibility of Jeanty taking a week off, and he made his stance completely clear.

**"I'm available," he said — adding that Jeanty needs some rest, because he's going to be a key piece of this offense.**

Raider Nation, stop and appreciate the layers in that answer, because there are two things happening at once.

The first is a veteran running back telling the entire league he's ready to work. That's a free agent raising his hand in public, which is unusual and takes a little humility.

The second is more interesting to me. **A professional running back — a man who has made a living carrying a football — looked at Ashton Jeanty's situation and said out loud that the kid needs rest.** That's not a fan on Twitter. That's not a YouTube channel. That's somebody who knows exactly what a bad ankle and a bad foot feel like in week six, endorsing the exact position we've argued on this channel for two weeks.

## WHY THE FIT IS ALMOST PERFECT

Now the football, because this is where it gets genuinely compelling.

**Mostert played for the Raiders last season.** So he already has experience inside this organization, he knows the building, and he is familiar with Jeanty personally.

And here's the detail that matters most: **he also spent years working within Kyle Shanahan's offensive system, which shares concepts with the scheme Klint Kubiak uses.**

Raider Nation, that is the single most important sentence in this story.

Think about why. The reason you can't just sign a running back off the street in October is the learning curve — the protection calls, the footwork on wide zone, the aiming points, the way you read the second-level defender. A back who doesn't know those things is a liability no matter how fast he is. We just spent two days listening to Kubiak explain that Dante Thornton Jr. can't be activated because being "assignment sound" takes time.

**Mostert wouldn't need that time.** The Shanahan tree and the Kubiak offense use overlapping language and overlapping run concepts. He has run these plays, in this terminology, for years — including last season in this exact building.

That background makes him a genuinely practical short-term option if this staff decides they need extra help for a game or two.

## AND IT COSTS NOTHING

Here's the part nobody is emphasizing enough.

Every "big move" we've discussed for a month comes with a price tag attached. A second-round pick for Brian Thomas Jr. A fourth for Jerry Jeudy. A sixth for Calvin Ridley. A future third plus a sixth for Khalil Mack.

**A Mostert signing costs zero draft capital.** He's a free agent. You're spending money, not picks — and money is the one resource this team has used sparingly all season.

For a front office that I've described as patching every hole at the cheapest available price, this is the cheapest possible patch at the position that has hurt this offense most. That's why it belongs in the "one big move" conversation even though it isn't a trade.

## THE HONEST OTHER SIDE

But I'm not going to sell you a reunion without the counterargument.

First, he isn't a long-term answer and nobody should pretend he is. This would be a one-or-two-game bridge while a 22-year-old heals, full stop.

Second — and this is the real objection — **the Raiders already have young backs who deserve those carries.** Washington Jr. is averaging five yards a carry. Roman Hemby was just promoted off the practice squad with a 5.3-yards-per-carry college season behind him. If you sign a veteran, those two get fewer snaps, and this team is supposed to be finding out what its young players are.

So the question becomes: do you want to *win* on Sunday, or do you want to *learn* on Sunday? A veteran who knows the system helps you win now. The rookies teach you something about next year. There's a real argument on both sides, and the answer depends on whether you think a 3-2 team is chasing the playoffs or building toward them.

## BUT HERE'S THE DEVELOPMENT THAT CHANGES EVERYTHING

And now the update that I have to be honest about, because it cuts against the position I've been arguing.

**Ashton Jeanty participated FULLY in Wednesday's practice ahead of the Buffalo matchup.**

Full participation. Not limited. Not a maintenance day. Which strongly suggests the Raiders are not necessarily preparing to sideline him at all.

And Kubiak shared genuinely encouraging news, telling reporters that **Jeanty was beginning to feel much better physically, and that the staff was excited to give him more reps during full-intensity practice sessions.**

Raider Nation, I owe you consistency, so let me be clear about where this leaves me. For two weeks I said: rest him, protect the asset, let the young backs carry it. The head coach answered with two words — "he's playing" — and now the injury report says full participation and the coach says the player feels much better.

**A full practice is real evidence, and it's better evidence than anything I was working from.** If a player with a foot and ankle issue takes a complete Wednesday at full intensity, the recovery is going in the right direction. I still think the workload has to be managed — I'd take 15 touches over 22 — but I'm not going to keep demanding a healthy scratch for a man who just practiced in full.

That's what updating a take looks like. The context, for the record: Austin Boyd wrote on October 15th that this ground game is still struggling despite a top-10 first-round investment, with Jeanty down to **3.3 yards per carry** — a number that puts real pressure on Kirk Cousins — and ESPN reported the Raiders had considered whether resting him would help his recovery.

## COUSINS ON THE ROOKIE

And the quarterback added the human layer.

**Cousins praised Jeanty's attitude, explaining that his hard work and positivity have helped motivate the entire team.**

Think about the context of that compliment. The rookie is on 3.3 yards a carry with two injured lower limbs, his production is the biggest disappointment of this season, and the 38-year-old quarterback's unprompted assessment is that the kid's attitude is *lifting other people.*

That tells you why nobody in that building wants to bench him. And it tells you why Mostert — who knows him — framed his own availability as help *for* Jeanty rather than a shot at his job.

## NOW THE GAME PLAN, AND KUBIAK'S HARDEST ADMISSION YET

Let's move to the second half of today, because this is the part that decides Sunday.

Kubiak knows this team has to respond after the 45-17 loss, and he is not hiding from it. In a piece by Jared Fineberg for Raiders on SI on October 15th, the coach's postgame comments became the focal point of the entire conversation about how Las Vegas improves.

Five games in, the Raiders have shown real signs of progress compared to last year. But New England exposed serious weaknesses: they couldn't establish a ground game, they fell behind early, and they failed to respond effectively to the Patriots' aggressive defensive pressure.

And here is the admission.

**Kubiak gave credit to New England while acknowledging that he had not prepared an adequate plan for his players.** He explained that the team failed in its execution, that the running game never got going, and that the responsibility ultimately rested with him.

Raider Nation, that is the furthest he has gone. "We weren't ready to go" was one thing. **"I did not have an adequate plan" is a head coach saying the game was lost on the whiteboard before anybody put on a helmet.**

I've criticized this staff hard over two weeks. I'm also not going to pretend that level of honesty is common. Most coaches in this league would have spent that press conference talking about "executing better." He indicted his own preparation.

But — and we keep arriving at this same but — admissions are not adjustments. The scoreboard doesn't care what you confessed on Monday.

## WHAT HAS WORKED, AND WHAT GOT EXPOSED

To be fair to him, Kubiak has already demonstrated an ability to build an offense around his actual roster. The clearest example is his use of **Michael Mayer and Brock Bowers** — two tight ends deployed to create opportunities in a variety of situations, which is exactly why this offense averaged over 28 points a game with a receiver room nobody respected.

But the Patriots exposed the places where this offense needs more creativity and better in-game adjustments. And Kubiak himself highlighted two specific failures, both of which are more interesting than the generic "we have to be better" answer.

## FAILURE ONE: THE WASHINGTON JR. PROBLEM — AND THE SCHEME ANSWER

**Washington Jr. recorded only one carry for five yards against New England.**

One carry. Five yards. From a back averaging five yards a carry on the season.

I have hammered this for days as the least defensible decision of that afternoon. And the coach acknowledged it directly — he said Washington deserved more opportunities, and that the Buffalo game could be the chance to correct the oversight.

But here's the detail that finally makes the whole thing make sense, and it's a piece of real football information: **Washington's running style could be particularly effective within gap blocking schemes.**

Let me explain that, because it's the key to this entire problem.

There are broadly two ways to block a run. **Zone blocking** — the foundation of the Shanahan and Kubiak systems — asks linemen to move laterally in unison and block an area rather than a man, while the running back reads the flow and picks a crease that develops. It rewards patience and vision.

**Gap blocking** is the opposite. Linemen pull and down-block to create one specific hole at one specific spot, and the back's job is to hit it immediately and violently. It rewards decisiveness and contact balance rather than patience.

So when the staff says Washington fits gap schemes, they're telling you something important: **he's a one-cut, hit-it-now runner, and this offense's base identity asks for something different.** That's not an excuse for one carry — a staff that knows a player's strength should design four plays a game to that strength — but it explains why he isn't simply interchangeable with Jeanty.

And here's why it matters this week specifically. We've established that the Raiders face the second-most stacked boxes in football. Against a stacked front, zone runs die because there are more defenders than blockers. **Gap runs — with a puller creating one hole by force — are a legitimate answer to a crowded box.** So giving Washington Jr. a handful of gap-scheme carries isn't just fair to the player. It's a counter to the exact thing that has strangled this run game all season.

That's a real adjustment, and the coach just told us it's on the table.

## FAILURE TWO: THE HIDDEN REASON MAYER DISAPPEARED

Now the one that nobody is talking about, and I think it's the most revealing detail of the week.

Michael Mayer's role deserves attention. The tight end can obviously contribute as a receiver — he caught eight passes for 82 yards against Kansas City. But against New England, **the Raiders often needed him to help with protection as the Patriots ramped up the pressure with blitzes.**

**Kubiak admitted Mayer wasn't utilized enough in the passing game, explaining that the team's protection needs limited his opportunities.** And he made clear that finding ways to get everybody involved is ultimately his responsibility.

Raider Nation, this is the hidden cost of a protection problem, and almost nobody accounts for it.

When we talk about a struggling offensive line, we count sacks and pressures. But there's a second bill that never shows up in the box score: **your receiving weapons stop being weapons.** If Mayer has to stay in and block a blitzing linebacker, he isn't running a route. If he isn't running a route, that's one fewer target for a quarterback who already had three healthy receivers.

So the chain goes like this: bad protection means the tight end blocks, which means fewer available targets, which means the defense can cover everybody, which means more pressure. **The line problem and the receiver problem are the same problem.** And that's why I keep saying a trade for a receiver doesn't fix an offense surrendering pressure on 44.8 percent of dropbacks.

The fix Kubiak described is the right one: against Buffalo, Las Vegas needs a better balance between protecting Cousins and giving its playmakers real chances. That means establishing the run, varying the plan, and keeping the defense from predicting the next snap.

He has acknowledged the issue. Now the adjustments have to produce points.

## THE BUFFALO OPPORTUNITY: TWO NAMES

And fortunately for Las Vegas, the Bills may be dealing with significant injury concerns of their own — which brings us to the part of this video that should make you optimistic.

According to a report by Jal Granberry published October 15th, **wide receiver DJ Moore and defensive tackle Ed Oliver were the only Bills players — out of 15 listed on the initial injury report — who did not participate in Wednesday's practice.**

And both of them already missed Buffalo's Week Five victory over the Los Angeles Rams. Moore has been sidelined with a shoulder injury; Oliver has been out with a knee issue.

**DJ Moore** joined Buffalo in the offseason to bolster their passing game, and he's recorded **12 receptions for 178 yards and a touchdown over the first four games.** His absence would be a significant blow to Buffalo's quarterback — it removes an experienced option for moving the ball and generating yards after the catch, which is exactly the kind of player who converts a third-and-seven into a first down.

**Ed Oliver** is the one I care about more. He's in his eighth season with Buffalo and has tallied **nine tackles and half a sack across three games.**

Here's why that matters so much to us. Oliver is an interior defensive tackle — a disruptive one. And the Raiders' entire offensive problem is interior run blocking against loaded fronts. **Remove a quality defensive tackle from the middle of that front and the math in the box changes in our favor.** If Oliver can't play, and if Jeanty is genuinely healthier, and if Kubiak mixes in the gap-scheme carries we just discussed, this is the best chance this run game has had since Week One against Miami.

That's three ifs. But they're all live.

## OUR OWN INJURY REPORT — AND A NEW WORRY

Now the honest accounting on our side, because Las Vegas listed **12 players on its initial report.**

**Jeanty participated fully** — we covered that, and it's the best news on the sheet.

**Brock Bowers was a LIMITED participant.** Raider Nation, that one got my attention. Bowers is the engine of this entire offense — eight catches for 110 yards and a touchdown in a 28-point loss — and he's coming off a procedure on that knee before the season. "Limited" on a Wednesday is usually maintenance and nothing more. But if that word becomes "did not participate" on Thursday or Friday, it becomes the biggest story of the week, and I'll be watching it closely.

**Defensive tackle Tonka Hemingway and wide receiver Jalen Nailor did not practice, both for concussion-related reasons.** Hemingway sustained his injury in Week Five and will require medical clearance to return against Buffalo. Nailor suffered his concussion in Week Four and remains subject to the NFL's concussion protocol — Kubiak hopes he'll return to practice and get cleared in time to play Sunday.

And I'll repeat what I said about protocol a week ago: those steps exist for reasons that have nothing to do with Sunday's game plan, and nobody should be rooting against the process. If Nailor clears, the $35 million receiver gets a chance to justify the investment. If he doesn't, this receiver room is thin again.
