FIRST, THE CREDIT — BECAUSE THE FORMULA WORKED

Before the red flag, the full picture, because Week One's success was real and it was COLLECTIVE. Give Kirk Cousins his credit: 21 of 30, three touchdowns, and a first half that built the lead which controlled the entire game. Give the backfield its credit: Jeanty's 147-yard masterpiece, Mike Washington Jr. contributing, even Dylan Laube — the third back — getting touches. And here's the identity stat of the day: the Raiders ran 21 personnel — two backs on the field — on 35.8 percent of their 67 snaps. That is the franchise's HIGHEST two-back rate since 2016. Read that again: since 2016. Kubiak didn't just talk about a physical identity all summer; he installed it, snap by snap, in one week — and suddenly every "sentimental" roster move makes sense in reverse: the fullback battle, Heyward's role, Laird stashed, three backs kept. That was this plan, hiding in plain sight all summer, forcing defenses to defend looks the modern NFL barely rehearses.

And the five men up front deserve a standing ovation: Kolton Miller, Spencer Burford, Tyler Linderbaum, Jackson Powers-Johnson, DJ Glaze — Cousins was NOT SACKED ONCE. Miller and Glaze posted two of the highest pass-block win rates among all tackles in Week One. Remember when the interior line was our top-three fault line? Week One said the renovation is working. So no, this isn't a panic video. The formula — run it, protect it, quick game, tight ends, control the line — worked beautifully. The question is what happens when it doesn't get to.

THE RED FLAG: ONE OF SEVEN

Now the number. On passes of TEN OR FEWER air yards, Kirk Cousins was surgical: 20 of 22 for 139 yards and all three touchdowns. That's elite efficiency — the distributor role executed to perfection, exactly as we described it after the game. But when the Raiders tried to push the ball beyond ten air yards? One. Of. Seven. Twenty-one yards. And BOTH interceptions came on those deeper attempts.

That's not a quarterback having a bad day. That's a SPLIT — a clean, coherent pattern that every defensive coordinator on our schedule has already highlighted in yellow. And you know we saw this coming, because it's the exact warning we wrote into the win recap: an offense living on-schedule and underneath eventually meets a defense that refuses to allow it. Here's how the adjustment goes: safeties creep down, corners squat on the quick game, linebackers crowd the boxes against Jeanty — and suddenly the easy completions disappear, and the offense's survival depends on the ONE throw the Week One tape says isn't there. Against Miami, the formula never got stress-tested. Against what's coming? It will be, weekly.

Now, in fairness — and this channel always does the fairness — the one-of-seven isn't a solo project. Who was he throwing DEEP to? A room whose only true vertical threat is on injured reserve: Tucker's speed lives on motion and crossers, Nailor wins intermediate, and several of those seven attempts were contested balls because nobody actually UNCOVERED downfield. A deep game is a partnership — someone has to win the footrace first. That's why this flag has three fixes, not one: Cousins throwing it better, the scheme manufacturing separation, or the roster importing someone who creates it naturally. Which, again... the board. But the quarterback doesn't walk free either: both picks were avoidable by his own coach's description. The honest verdict is shared custody — sixty percent personnel, forty percent execution, one hundred percent a PROBLEM.

Which brings us — carefully, fairly — to Fernando Mendoza, because you cannot discuss this quarterback situation without acknowledging the shadow at the edge of it. Let me be crystal clear about what I am NOT saying: you do not bench a veteran who just threw three touchdowns in a 27-13 win. Full stop. That conversation does not exist today.

But let's re-read the rookie's file with adult eyes, because the "he threw preseason picks" narrative deserves the same honesty we just gave Cousins. Interception one? A route miscommunication with Mike Washington Jr. — two young players disagreeing about a landing spot. Interception two? Off Jalen Nailor's HANDS — and Kubiak himself publicly said Nailor needed to make that catch rather than hanging it on the rookie. Context matters when you're evaluating a first overall pick.

And Mendoza just got something priceless: a front-row seat to a real NFL Sunday.
