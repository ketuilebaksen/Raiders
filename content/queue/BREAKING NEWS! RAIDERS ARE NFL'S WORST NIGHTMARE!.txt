BOWERS GOOD SIGN, SAME ANSWER

Start with the biggest question around this team. Brock Bowers returned to practice on Friday in a limited capacity. That's genuinely encouraging, and after the weeks of meniscus updates we've followed together, seeing him back on the practice field at all is progress. But the official designation didn't change. He's listed as doubtful for Sunday, and the Raiders will work him out before the game in Los Angeles to make the final call.

So technically we wait. But based on everything we know, I'd prepare for Bowers not to play. And honestly, I think that's the smarter decision. This is Week Two. If he needs one more week, targeting Week Three against the Saints makes a lot of sense, and it still keeps him well ahead of the Kansas City date that Dan Graziano described as the worst case. You don't push your best offensive weapon back onto a surgically repaired knee because it's the second game of an eighteen-week season. Friday's practice tells you the knee is responding. The doubtful tag tells you the staff is choosing patience. Both of those are good news.

THE REPLACEMENT PLAN WHO TAKES ON DERWIN JAMES

If Bowers can't go, the Raiders have to figure out how to replace what he gives the offense. And that's where one of the most interesting matchups of the game shows up Derwin James Jr.

Look at what happened to James against Arizona. He was targeted on twenty percent of his coverage snaps, which tied for the sixth-highest single-game rate of his career. He allowed five receptions, which tied for the third-most he's ever given up in a game. Four of those catches went to Trey McBride, including a touchdown. And McBride did most of that damage from the slot, where he ran sixty percent of his routes.

The obvious takeaway would be attack Derwin James with the tight end. But the Raiders don't have another Trey McBride on the roster, and they shouldn't pretend they do. Michael Mayer is a different kind of player, and Kubiak has to use him for what he does well instead of copying Arizona's plan.

Here's what the numbers from Miami tell us. Mayer ran only 8.3 percent of his routes from the slot. He played 54 snaps, ran 24 routes, caught six of seven targets, and finished with 32 yards. He's an inline tight end who wins over the middle and can be moved around the formation. Jack Bech is the opposite he ran 69.2 percent of his routes from the slot. In 27 snaps and 13 routes, he caught three of four targets for 23 yards and that opening-drive touchdown.

So instead of asking one player to do everything Bowers does, the Raiders can combine two skill sets and make the Chargers defend different problems on the same play. Bech is a physical, power-slot receiver who can work in-breaking routes against a safety. Mayer can work the middle of the field, or split out wide and force the Chargers to decide who covers him. Put both on the field and you're making Derwin James deal with size and physicality from two different directions. I call it basketball at the catch point big bodies boxing out defenders and winning contested balls.

The point isn't that the Raiders can replace Bowers one for one. They can't. The point is that they have pieces they can combine to make the Chargers account for more than one threat. If Bowers is ruled out, watch how creative Kubiak gets with those personnel packages. That will tell us a lot about his offensive plan when the best weapon isn't available.

And connect this to the bigger problem we've been tracking all week the deep ball. Cousins went one for seven beyond ten yards against Miami. If the Chargers load up to stop Jeanty and sit on the short routes, the Mayer and Bech combination in the intermediate middle of the field becomes even more important. That's where Cousins is most comfortable, and that's where Derwin James just had one of the roughest days of his career.

There's also a second layer to the Mayer plan blocking. Remember what this offense did against Miami, running two-back personnel on over a third of its snaps, the highest rate in a decade, and averaging 6.4 yards a play out of it. Mayer is a big part of why that works, because he blocks like an extra lineman on the edge. If the Chargers bring Derwin James down into the box to help stop Jeanty, that's when play action to Mayer or Bech behind him becomes the best call on the board. Kubiak doesn't need Bowers to create that conflict. He just needs James to guess wrong a couple of times.

THE CORNER NOBODY HAS NAMED

Now the defense. Darien Porter is out, and the Raiders still haven't said who will start opposite Eric Stokes. There's genuine uncertainty about who gets the first snap.

Here's what we know. When Porter left the Miami game, Hezekiah Masses came in and played eight coverage snaps. He was targeted once and broke up the pass. He also drew a penalty, which you'd like to clean up. But beyond the flag, there was a lot to like in those snaps. He looked athletic, he stayed on top of routes, and he held his position instead of getting caught out of the play. For a young corner thrown into a game without warning, that's exactly what you want to see.

Then there's Jermod McCoy, who's also in the mix. Kubiak said earlier this week that both players are ready and that the team likes what each one brings. So this isn't a case where one player was handed the job and everybody else is waiting.

My expectation Masses gets the first opportunity, and the Raiders ride the hot hand. If he plays well, he stays out there. If he struggles, McCoy is ready to step in. And you'd better believe the Chargers will test whoever lines up opposite Stokes. We already know the secondary gave up three completions of twenty-plus yards to Miami. Justin Herbert will be looking for the same openings, and he's a lot better at finding them.

The safety help matters here too. Trayden Stokes, the rookie who grabbed an interception in his debut, and Jeremy Chinn will both be aware that the young corner is the target. Expect Leonard to rotate help toward that side on key downs and trust Eric Stokes on an island on the other. That's the whole point of the disguise package we broke down in the film room make Herbert think the young corner is alone, then close the window after the snap.

McCONKEY THE CHARGERS' X-FACTOR

On the Chargers' side, the name to watch is Ladd McConkey. He missed the first two practices this week with the rib injury he suffered against Arizona, returned on Friday, and is officially questionable.

Here's why that matters so much. When McConkey left the Arizona game in the third quarter, the Chargers' yards per pass attempt dropped by five yards. Five. That's how much their passing game depends on him, especially with a receiver room that's already lost KeAndre Lambert-Smith to injured reserve.

I'd expect him to have a chance to play. But rib injuries affect receivers in ways the injury report doesn't show. How comfortable is he running routes Will he go up for contested catches Is he willing to take hits over the middle If he's limited, the Raiders' secondary gets opportunities it wouldn't otherwise have. And if he can't go, a young corner room gets a much easier afternoon.

THE MATCHUP I'M WATCHING MOST THE MIDDLE OF THE LINE

But the matchup I'm watching most closely is happening in the middle of the field, and it's the Chargers' interior offensive line, led by rookie center Jake Slaughter.

The Chargers want to run the football. Their scheme is built on a Shanahan-style wide zone foundation, now with Mike McDaniel's concepts influencing how they create movement and space. That's an interesting wrinkle, because Kubiak and McDaniel came up in the same coaching family and are friends going back to their Denver days. They know each other's playbooks. The Raiders can't sit back and wait for obvious passing downs. They have to control the line of scrimmage and stop the run early.

That puts a lot on the interior defensive line, the room we've worried about since August. Tonka Hemingway had three pressures against Miami, early signs that he can create disruption from the inside. Foley Fatukasi had half a sack, a pressure, two run stops, and appeared to draw back-to-back holding penalties in his limited snaps. Adam Butler didn't register a pressure or a run stop according to Sumer Sports, but I liked some of what I saw on tape. His first step and ability to get into the backfield can blow up plays even when the stat sheet doesn't give him credit.

And here's where Jake Slaughter becomes central to this game. He's a rookie center making his second NFL start, and Rob Leonard has already shown us he'll bring pressure. In Week One, the Raiders ranked first in the league in havoc rate, which counts tackles for loss, sacks, pressures, and other disruptive plays. I expect Leonard to make life very uncomfortable for that rookie stunts, line movement, pressure from different angles, overloads that force the center to make the right protection call over and over again. If the Raiders create confusion inside, they disrupt both the run game and the pass protection at the same time.

There's more evidence for that approach. Herbert had trouble adjusting to pressure and protection looks against Arizona. That doesn't mean he'll make the same mistakes again, but it shows why disguising things before the snap is so valuable against this team. Leonard comes from the Brian Flores coaching tree, and Flores showed how aggressive that philosophy can get by blitzing on seventy-eight percent of his defensive snaps against Green Bay. The Raiders have their own system and their own personnel, but the willingness to attack protections is part of the same DNA. We saw it all over the Miami tape the line games, the corner blitzes, the disguised rotations.

THE ONE REQUIREMENT WIN THE LINE OF SCRIMMAGE

For me, all of this comes down to one requirement. The Raiders have to win the line of scrimmage. They cannot let the Chargers establish the run with rookie running back Omarion Hampton and force the defense into a series of second-and-short situations.

If Los Angeles runs the ball efficiently, it opens the play-action game and gives Herbert all the time and options he wants. That's the version of this game where the Chargers' desperation turns into confidence and the Raiders' five-game losing streak at SoFi continues.

But if the Raiders stop the run, create negative plays, and force the Chargers into long down-and-distance situations, that's when Leonard's pressure packages become truly dangerous. Now Crosby and Paye are rushing on third-and-eight instead of third-and-two. Now a rookie center has to sort out stunts while the crowd noise from the silver-and-black section drowns out his calls. Now the Raiders are the ones dictating the protections instead of reacting to them.

That's why these injury updates matter so much. It's not about scanning a list for green lights and red lights. Bowers being doubtful points us to Mayer, Bech, and Derwin James. Porter being out points us to Masses and the hot hand. McConkey's ribs point us to the Chargers' passing ceiling. And a rookie center points us to the exact spot where Rob Leonard will try to win this game.

My prediction hasn't changed Raiders 24, Chargers 17. Win the line of scrimmage, and the curse dies today.
