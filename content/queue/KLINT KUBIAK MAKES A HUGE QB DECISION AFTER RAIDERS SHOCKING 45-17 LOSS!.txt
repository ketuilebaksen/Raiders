## WHAT THE PATRIOTS DID THAT ALMOST NOBODY DOES

The Patriots accomplished something very few opponents have managed this season: they prevented Maxx Crosby from controlling a football game.

Las Vegas walked into Gillette Stadium on October 11th hoping to recover from its first defeat and instead suffered a 45-17 loss, falling to 3-2 after starting the season undefeated.

And here are the two numbers that define the defensive half of this disaster.

**Crosby finished with one tackle and zero sacks. And the Raiders recorded just one sack against Drake Maye in the entire game.**

One tackle, Raider Nation. For a player who has been double-teamed more than anybody in football and still produced. One sack as a team against a quarterback who had all afternoon.

That lack of pressure allowed New England to keep the middle of the pocket clean, extend passing concepts, and attack downfield without ever forcing Maye to operate under disruption. A quarterback who is never disturbed will beat any secondary in football, let alone a young one playing without safety help.

## VRABEL EXPLAINED THE HOW — AND IT'S A PROBLEM

Now here's why this story is bigger than one Sunday.

After the game, Vrabel publicly recognized his offensive line for understanding the protection plan and preventing Crosby from becoming a decisive factor. And then he said the part that should concern every one of you.

**He explained that the Patriots knew how Las Vegas intended to pressure them — including the situations in which the Raiders used zero blitzes and committed every available defender to attacking the quarterback.**

They knew. Not guessed. Knew.

Raider Nation, a zero blitz is the most aggressive call in football: you send everybody and you leave nobody deep. It's a dare. When it works you get a sack or a strip. When the offense recognizes it pre-snap, you get a touchdown, because there is nothing behind it.

New England blocked those pressures successfully and converted the opportunities downfield. Which means our most aggressive call wasn't just unsuccessful — it was *anticipated.*

And that is a self-scouting problem, which lands on Rob Leonard's desk, not on the players. If an opposing staff can identify when you're going to send everybody, your pressure package has a tell. Maybe it's a personnel grouping. Maybe it's an alignment, or a down-and-distance habit, or a safety rotation that tips a half-second early. Whatever it is, it's on tape now, and Vrabel just told the entire league it exists.

## THE CROSBY PART IS EVEN MORE IMPORTANT

And then Vrabel described his philosophy for handling number 98, which is the most instructive coaching quote of this entire week.

**He emphasized that protecting Maye required every available offensive player, rather than one blocker attempting to handle Crosby alone.**

He also noted that the Patriots needed reserve players to contribute — including Caleb Lomu — and credited them for it.

Think about what that means in practice. Not a chip from a tight end. Not a slide to his side. **An entire offensive operation organized around the principle that no single human being gets left alone with Maxx Crosby.** Backup linemen included.

And that approach matters because of exactly who Crosby is. He has built his reputation on defeating isolated matchups, creating pressure even when opponents know precisely where he's aligned, and changing games through effort that lasts until the whistle. His superpower isn't surprise — it's that he beats you when you're ready for him.

So New England took away the only thing that can actually stop him: the one-on-one. By supporting the line through protection design and recognizing our pressure packages, they refused to give him favorable situations and refused to let our front create the negative plays this defense depends on.

Raider Nation, I want to be honest with you about something. For a month I've been telling you that the respect Crosby commands is a structural advantage, because sliding protection toward him creates a one-on-one somewhere else for Kwity Paye, Malcolm Koonce or Nolan Smith. That's been true all season.

Sunday was the counterargument. New England didn't slide help to Crosby and create a weakness elsewhere — they protected everywhere, with everybody, and nobody won. Which means the theory only holds if the supporting rushers actually cash in. One sack says they didn't.

## VRABEL'S FULL ACCOUNTING

He also credited his players and staff for overcoming injuries while limiting Las Vegas to **17 points and 38 rushing yards**, while recording **three sacks**.

He specifically recognized Josh McDaniels for the offense and Jeremy Springer for the special teams units, along with his defensive staff — but insisted the players deserved the greatest credit for executing through sudden changes, short-yardage situations and personnel adjustments.

And notice that Springer's name is in there. The former DeCamillis assistant we talked about last week. The chess match we flagged, and the student beat the teacher.

## THE CARRY COUNT THAT SHOULD INFURIATE YOU

Now the part of this I find hardest to defend, and this is where my criticism is sharpest.

Klint Kubiak accepted responsibility for one of the most confusing decisions of this loss: abandoning the running game early while giving rookie Washington Jr. almost no opportunity to help an injured and ineffective Ashton Jeanty.

Here is the full accounting, and I want you to look at it carefully.

**Las Vegas rushed for 38 yards at 2.1 yards per attempt. Jeanty carried 10 times for 27 yards. Washington Jr. received exactly ONE carry — despite averaging 5.0 yards per attempt this season. And Dylan Laube, who contributes primarily on special teams and as a receiving option out of the backfield, finished with FOUR carries.**

Read that again.

The back averaging five yards a carry got one attempt. The special teams back got four. The starter with a foot injury and an ankle injury got ten for twenty-seven.

Kubiak explained afterward that falling behind pushed the Raiders toward a pass-heavy approach as they tried to erase the deficit, and he admitted he moved away from the run too quickly, particularly early.

I respect the admission. I said so yesterday. But here's the thing, Raider Nation: **that admission explains the total number of carries. It does not explain the distribution.**

If you only have fifteen rushing attempts to give, the question of who gets them is a completely separate decision from how many there are. And in a game where your starter was visibly compromised, your most efficient runner this season received one of them while your special teams back received four.

## WHAT MIGHT EXPLAIN IT — AND WHAT DOESN'T

Let me be fair and give you the most likely football explanation.

Washington's limited role may reflect concerns about pass protection, or other responsibilities running backs have to handle away from the football. In a game where New England's pressure package was getting home, you might trust a specific back in protection more than another. Laube's role as a receiving threat out of the backfield fits a two-minute, pass-heavy script, which is exactly what the fourth quarter became.

And I want to be clear: there's no confirmation that blocking assignments drove the decision. That's my inference, not a report.

But here's why I don't think it's a complete answer. The rookie has produced every time he's been given the football. He appears capable of creating missed tackles, contributing as a receiver, and giving this offense a different source of energy. If pass protection is genuinely the barrier, that's a coaching assignment to solve, not a permanent sentence — you build the three or four runs where he doesn't have to block and you call them.

**Washington does not need to replace Jeanty as the long-term foundation of this backfield to deserve a bigger immediate role. He only needs to provide more production while the starter recovers.** That's it. That's the whole argument.

## THE JEANTY PROBLEM, STATED WITH RESPECT

And this is where it gets genuinely difficult, because it involves a young man's health and his character.

Jeanty hurt his ankle before the season, played through it in Week One, and is now also dealing with a foot injury. Kubiak described him as beat up and praised the toughness required to stay on the field for his teammates.

And Jeanty reinforced that mentality himself, declaring that he intends to play unless he becomes physically unable to run or jump. He acknowledged that football rarely allows a player to feel completely healthy, and said his priority is doing everything possible to help the team.

Raider Nation, that commitment deserves respect. Full respect. That's exactly the mentality you want from the sixth overall pick, and it's the reason his teammates clearly love him.

**But it also creates a difficult responsibility for the coaching staff, and I'm going to say it plainly: a competitive player may refuse to remove himself even when his injuries prevent him from performing effectively.** That's not a criticism of Jeanty. It's a description of every great competitor who ever played this sport.

Which means the decision cannot be left to him. The Raiders have to determine whether continuing to play him is helping this offense or extending a problem that could become more serious. Ten carries for twenty-seven yards is not helping the offense. And a foot injury that a player runs through for six weeks is how a four-week problem becomes a four-month problem.

Increasing Washington's carries would do three things at once: protect Jeanty, introduce more explosiveness, and let this staff finally evaluate whether the rookie can handle a broader assignment. There is no version of that where the Raiders lose.

## AND THEY CANNOT KEEP LIVING THIS WAY

Here's the structural lesson of this loss, and it's the one I'd put on the wall.

The Raiders cannot depend on the passing game to overcome every failed rushing performance.

Cousins and the receivers carried this offense for four weeks, and it worked — 28 points a game, a 3-1 record, no functioning run game. But New England demonstrated precisely what happens when an opponent generates pressure, controls the line of scrimmage, and forces you into predictable passing situations.

The entire act collapses at once. That's what 45-17 looks like.

So Buffalo will create another demanding test, and Kubiak has a decision to make: remain committed to an injured starter, expand Washington's role, or distribute the workload more evenly. His postgame admission showed accountability. **The next step has to be a tangible adjustment** — because if it isn't, the running game becomes one of the primary reasons a promising start keeps collapsing.

## MENDOZA'S DEBUT: THE NUMBERS

Now the story everybody's talking about, and I want to handle it with precision instead of emotion.

Fernando Mendoza's long-awaited NFL debut arrived under circumstances nobody in Las Vegas wanted. But the rookie turned one late possession into a valuable first experience.

Remember the context. This organization selected him with the number one overall pick to be the future of the franchise, and Cousins' excellent start allowed them to stay patient and keep him in a developmental role. That changed when New England built a 45-17 advantage and removed any realistic possibility of a comeback. Las Vegas replaced Cousins and gave Mendoza the final drive — letting him experience professional game speed without asking him to rescue an impossible situation.

**He completed six of eight passes for 48 yards, released the football quickly, and appeared comfortable moving within the pocket.**

Six of eight. Quick release. Comfortable feet.

## THE HONEST CONTEXT

Now let me do what a lot of channels won't and immediately tell you what that does and doesn't mean.

The opportunity was extremely limited and came against Patriots reserves late in a blowout. It cannot provide a reliable prediction of how he'd perform as a starter against a complete first-team defense. Backup defenders in a 28-point game play soft coverage, don't blitz much, and are trying not to get anybody hurt.

But there is real encouragement available here, and it isn't the stat line. It's that their young quarterback remained composed and did not allow an ugly team performance to overwhelm him. Plenty of rookies walk into a blowout and start pressing, trying to prove something on every snap. He didn't.

## AND LISTEN TO WHAT HE SAID

Mendoza described the appearance as a good experience and recognized that nearly every rookie eventually faces a moment when he's asked to enter under difficult circumstances.

And then — this is the part I loved — **he focused on the offense's effort instead of his individual statistics, saying he was proud of how the unit continued competing late in the game.**

Raider Nation, that's a number one overall pick who just got his first NFL completions in a 45-17 loss, and his instinct was to praise his teammates for still competing.

That response reflects the patient, positive approach he's maintained the entire time he's been waiting behind Cousins. We've talked all season about whether sitting him was the right call. Whatever you think of the football decision, the human being has handled it perfectly.

## THE QUARTERBACK TIMELINE, WITH REAL DATES

Now the larger question: how long can Las Vegas continue treating him only as a developmental quarterback if the losses continue?

Let me give you the actual schedule, because this is where the conversation gets concrete.

The Raiders have dropped two straight and now face **Buffalo in Week Six**, then the **Los Angeles Rams**. If this team continues performing the way it did in New England, 3-4 becomes a realistic possibility.

From there, the **Week Eight matchup against the New York Jets** could provide a logical opening for Mendoza's first start. The organization could also remain patient until after the **Week Ten game against Seattle** — the schedule becomes less demanding after that point, and a record of 3-7 or 4-6 would create a much stronger argument for beginning the rookie's evaluation.

So that's your map. Two soft landing spots, Week Eight and Week Eleven, and a record threshold that would justify either one.

## BUT NONE OF THAT MEANS COUSINS CAUSED THIS

And here's where I'm going to defend the veteran, because the facts demand it.

**New England pressured Cousins on nearly 40 percent of his dropbacks and recorded three sacks.** Forty percent. That makes consistent execution extremely difficult for any quarterback, and especially for a 38-year-old who has never depended on mobility to escape collapsing pockets.

Kubiak praised New England's blitz plan and acknowledged that throwing as frequently as Las Vegas did naturally created more opportunities for the pass rush — which is the circular trap we discussed yesterday.

And Cousins credited the Patriots for their pressure design but refused to use protection as a complete excuse. His argument: NFL games change through one or two critical plays, and the Raiders failed to produce those moments.

That's a man who was hit on two of every five dropbacks declining to blame his blockers. Through the first four games he'd performed at a level approaching early MVP consideration and had given this staff no football reason to rush the rookie onto the field. Even in this one, he played reasonably under terrible conditions and avoided turnovers.

**Mendoza's debut should not be interpreted as evidence that an immediate quarterback change is underway.** It was the first meaningful step in a process Las Vegas always knew would eventually begin.

But every future loss will intensify the conversation. And his composed performance guarantees that the supporters calling for the rookie now have a real game sample to point at. That's just reality.

## COUSINS' RESPONSE, AND WHY IT MATTERS

And then the veteran did something that tells you everything about this locker room.

Cousins responded to the debut without defensiveness or hesitation. He told reporters the rookie competed well and performed effectively — offering public support rather than treating the appearance as a threat. Kubiak was similarly direct, saying he wanted Mendoza to get exposure and confirming he thought the rookie did a good job.

Raider Nation, understand what Cousins is actually doing. He is 38 years old, playing well, and publicly complimenting the 22-year-old drafted to take his job, four days after the worst loss of the season.

A lot of quarterbacks in that position get short with reporters. A lot of them say "that's a question for coach." He didn't. And remember what he said earlier this year about Mendoza — he's been mentoring him from the sideline all season, pointing out coverages between drives.

The small sample doesn't change the immediate expectation that Cousins starts against Buffalo. But the way he handled this week is the reason this locker room hasn't fractured after a 28-point loss.

The problem is that quarterback debates rarely stay centered on individual responsibility once a team starts losing. Every defeat increases the demand to see the number one selection, particularly if the Raiders fall out of contention and the season shifts from competing now toward evaluating the future.

## MY HONEST BIG-PICTURE VERDICT

Las Vegas does not need to make that decision after one blowout, and I want to give you the measured version here.

The 3-0 start may have created expectations that exceeded this roster's actual stage of development. That's a fair thing to say now. But two consecutive defeats do not prove the entire reconstruction has failed.

What the loss demonstrated is the distance that remains between this team and opponents capable of exploiting weaknesses on both sides of the ball. New England controlled the running game, pressured Cousins, neutralized Crosby, and converted our defensive aggression into opportunities downfield.

**And here's the crucial point: none of those failures are solved by changing quarterbacks.** Not one. A rookie passer does not fix a 2.1-yard rushing average, an anticipated zero blitz, or a pass rush that generated one sack.

This team may still need another draft class and another free agent period before it can consistently compete with the strongest teams in this conference. That's not pessimism. That's the honest timeline of a rebuild that got ahead of schedule for three weeks and then met the reigning AFC champion.

## WEEK SIX IS THE REAL TEST

So now Buffalo comes to Allegiant Stadium on October 18th, and Week Six will reveal how Kubiak's team responds to its first genuine crisis.

Here's the interesting part. Buffalo had already lost in Week Four and was scheduled to face the Los Angeles Rams on Monday Night Football — which creates the real possibility that **both teams enter this game carrying two-game losing streaks.**

The Bills will arrive on a short week. Home-field support should help. This is a superior but potentially vulnerable opponent, and that's about as favorable a setup as a reeling team can ask for.

And let me frame the stakes precisely, because I don't think the Raiders necessarily need to beat Buffalo to prove this organization is still headed in the right direction.

**But they cannot repeat another uncompetitive collapse.** That's the line.

Another defeat moves them to 3-3 after a 3-0 start. Another performance resembling New England raises far more serious questions about whether the early success was ever sustainable at all. And if Cousins gets protection and this offense returns to its early-season form, Kubiak can keep developing Mendoza patiently. If the line fails again, the streak reaches three, and this team drifts toward midseason without answers, the pressure surrounding the rookie becomes impossible to ignore.

## THE FIRST ADJUSTMENT

And the very first adjustment has to begin with the thing Vrabel just put on tape for everybody.

**Find ways to free Maxx Crosby.**

Because every coordinator left on this schedule just watched a head coach explain, in public, that the answer is total-unit protection plus recognizing our pressure tells. Move him inside on third down. Use him as a decoy and bring pressure from the opposite side. Change the picture pre-snap so the zero blitz isn't readable. Do something, because leaving the most dangerous player on this defense in a position to be neutralized by scheme is now a known vulnerability, not a bad day.
