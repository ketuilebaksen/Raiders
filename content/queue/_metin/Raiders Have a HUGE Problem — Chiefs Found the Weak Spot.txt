WHERE THE PLAN BROKE: Mahomes Is Now 14-2 Against Us, We Had Four Real Edges, and Not One of Them Converted 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, I want to do something uncomfortable today.

Before Sunday, I sat here and told you this Chiefs team had cracks in the armor. I was not making it up. The numbers were real, the injuries were real, and the matchup edges were real. Kansas City walked into Allegiant Stadium with an undrafted rookie protecting Patrick Mahomes' blind side, a kicker missing field goals, a pass rush ranked 26th in the league in sacks, and a secondary missing pieces.

We had a flashlight and a crowbar, and the house had loose bricks.

And we lost anyway. The Raiders are 3-1, Kansas City owns sole possession of first place in the AFC West, and Patrick Mahomes is now 14-2 against this franchise. Fourteen and two. If that number doesn't make you want to put a remote through a television, check your pulse.

So today isn't a highlight show and it isn't a pity party. Today we go through every single edge we identified, and I tell you honestly why each one didn't convert. Because that's how you learn something from a loss, and because the next seven weeks of this season depend on fixing exactly these things.

I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. If you're here after a loss, you're the real ones. Hit that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we push toward 6,000 believers. Let's do the autopsy.

FIRST, THE CONTEXT NOBODY SHOULD FORGET

By most accounts, this was the first time in the entire history of this rivalry that these two franchises met with matching 3-0 records. Decades of hatred and heartbreak, and never a collision quite like this one.

The Chiefs opened as five and a half point road favorites and the line slid to four and a half by kickoff, even while the betting public piled onto Kansas City. Las Vegas was 3-0 against the spread coming in. The market had this as a close game, and the market was not wrong about that part.

And here's a quote I want you to hold onto, because it isn't nothing. Mahomes himself said during the week that the Raiders' 3-0 start was no fluke. That's not the kind of respect you hand an opponent you think you can sleepwalk past. The best quarterback of this generation looked at our tape and took us seriously.

He also went out and won his fourteenth game against us.

EDGE ONE: THE BLIND SIDE WE DIDN'T BREAK

This was the big one. This was the edge I was most confident about, and it's the one that hurts most.

Kansas City's starting left tackle, Josh Simmons, has not played a single snap this season. Andy Reid revealed during the week that Simmons is dealing with a bulging disc in his back that's causing weakness in one leg, and the earliest anybody expects him back is Week Six against the Chargers. That is not a tweak. That's a serious problem for an offensive line.

So Mahomes' blind side belonged to Kahlil Benson, an undrafted rookie.

And I said it then and I'll repeat it now: credit to that kid, because he had held up better than anybody expected and Mahomes wasn't sacked a single time in Miami the week before. But there is an enormous difference between facing a Dolphins front and facing Maxx Crosby, Nakobe Dean, Malcolm Koonce and a defense that had ten sacks through three games, a top-four mark in the NFL, generated without blitzing like maniacs.

The plan was simple. Red Rover, Red Rover, send Max right over. Turn that left side into a revolving door, make Mahomes wait for routes to develop, and when Mahomes has to wait, he gets uncomfortable.

We didn't do it. And here's the honest football explanation for why, because "Crosby should have had four sacks" isn't analysis.

When you want to attack one specific protection weakness, you have to earn the right to isolate it. That means winning early downs so the opponent is throwing on obvious passing situations, and it means forcing them into empty or five-man protections where the rookie is genuinely alone. Reid's answer was the same answer every good coach has: chip help, a back in protection, quick throws that get the ball out before any rush matters, and a run game that keeps the down-and-distance friendly.

That's the thing about the blueprint for beating Mahomes. It isn't a secret. The two Super Bowl losses against Tampa Bay and Philadelphia weren't Mahomes getting outplayed by a better quarterback. They were Mahomes getting hit, hurried and harassed until the magic ran out. That blueprint has been on a whiteboard in every defensive meeting room in this league for years.

The hard part was never knowing it. The hard part is executing it for sixty minutes against a staff designed to prevent it, and we executed it for stretches instead of for a game.

EDGE TWO: THE EARLY-DOWN BATTLE, AGAIN

Which brings me straight back to the thing I've been hammering for a week, because everything above depends on it.

Kenneth Walker III is the reason the Chiefs' offense looks different this season. The Super Bowl MVP from Seattle came in with 360 rushing yards in three games, and here's the number that should have scared all of us more than it did: he had forced twelve or more missed tackles in each of the first three weeks, a feat no other player in the league had matched even once this season.

Read that again. Twelve forced missed tackles in a game is a monster afternoon. He did it three times in a row.

The Raiders were allowing about 116 rushing yards a game coming in, which is respectable and not elite. And the entire defensive plan was built on the premise that you cannot let Walker turn second-and-eight into second-and-three, because the moment Reid has manageable distance, the playbook opens and Mahomes becomes unsolvable.

A back who breaks a dozen tackles a game is precisely how second-and-eight becomes second-and-three. Not with 50-yard runs. With five yards after contact on first down, over and over, until your safeties have to come down and the deep middle opens up.

That's the loop. Walker makes the run game work, the run game makes the play action work, and the play action is where Mahomes lives. Last week against Miami he was 20 of 24 and perfect on play action. Perfect. Through three games he was completing around 68 percent for 812 yards with seven touchdowns, two interceptions and 8.3 yards per attempt, fully healthy after the knee injury that ended his 2025 season.

You don't beat that with talent. You beat it by taking away first down, and we didn't take away first down.

EDGE THREE: THE SPECIAL TEAMS ADVANTAGE THAT NEVER CASHED IN

This is the edge that genuinely frustrates me, because it was free money and we left it on the table.

Look at what Kansas City brought into this game in the third phase. Harrison Butker, a kicker who used to be automatic, had connected on six of eight field goals, 75 percent, well below league average. Their return units ranked 27th in the league. They have been among the most penalized special teams groups in football, with missed tackles mixed in.

Meanwhile we have Joe DeCamillis, one of the most respected special teams coordinators in the business, A.J. Cole punting the ball into the next time zone, Matt Gay coming off a career-long 59-yarder, and a coverage unit that had been playing with discipline all season.

The plan was hidden yardage. Flip the field a couple of times, pin Kansas City deep, and make Mahomes start drives from his own eight instead of the plus-40. In a one-possession game, a field position edge isn't a bonus, it's a heist.

And it didn't materialize into points. Which, by the way, is the second straight week special teams has cost this team something, because Dylan Laube's fumble in New Orleans handed the Saints the lead and we only survived it because the defense took the ball away four times.

Here's the lesson for the next seven weeks: an advantage is only an advantage if you convert it. This team is good enough in the third phase to steal a game. It has to actually steal one.

EDGE FOUR: WE HAD THE PROTECTION ADVANTAGE AND THE GUARD SPOT COST US

Now the offensive side, and this is where the loss really lives.

Look at the matchup on paper. Kansas City came in with four sacks on the season, 26th in the league, with a sack rate around 3.7 percent, bottom third of the NFL. Chris Jones is still Chris Jones and I'd never be foolish enough to say a player like that can't wreck a game, and he practiced fully after a calf note. But the supporting cast had not been getting home at the rate Chiefs fans are used to.

And the Raiders? Only five sacks allowed through three games, tied for seventh-best in the league, inside the top ten in nearly every pass-blocking metric you can find. Kolton Miller and DJ Glaze had been solid on the edges. Rick Dennison's group did not need to be perfect in this game. It just needed to be steady.

Except Jackson Powers-Johnson was ruled out with the groin injury after missing practice all week, which put Caleb Rogers into the starting lineup at right guard. And to be fair to Rogers, he filled in for JPJ in the second half of last season and played well, so this wasn't a hopeless situation.

But here's the reality of interior offensive line play, and it's the thing I keep coming back to: it's the most communication-dependent job on the field. Protection calls, slide rules, who takes the looping defender, who passes off a twist, who climbs to the second level. A guard who has practiced all week with the same center and tackle is a different player from a guard patching into a new combination against Chris Jones and Steve Spagnuolo's pressure looks.

And when the interior leaks, three things happen at once. The run game dies, which it did. The quarterback can't hold the ball long enough for the deep shots we've been asking for. And a 38-year-old quarterback starts taking hits he doesn't need to take.

That's why the Cesar Ruiz addition went from "nice move" to "the most important thing happening on this roster" in one afternoon.

EDGE FIVE: THE SECONDARY WE WERE SUPPOSED TO ATTACK

Here's the one that got away, and it's the one I'd most like to see on tape again.

Kansas City's defense had allowed only 178 passing yards per game coming in, which looks spectacular until you look at who they'd played: Bo Nix in a shaky Week One, Daniel Jones in Week Two, and Malik Willis in Week Three. Those are not Hall of Fame résumés.

I'll be fair, because honesty matters more than a take. That defense has genuinely been strong, giving up the third-fewest points per game in the league at 16.7, and Spagnuolo is one of the best defensive minds in football who always squeezes more out of his group than the names suggest.

But the back end was where the questions lived. They lost a player of Trent McDuffie's caliber from that secondary. Chamarri Conner was making his season debut after missing three games with a knee injury. L'Jarius Sneed was on the injury report with a knee issue, listed as limited.

That is exactly the kind of group a tight end who runs routes like he was drawn up by an engineer is supposed to feast on, and exactly the kind of group a veteran quarterback who has seen every coverage ever invented is supposed to carve up.

Brock Bowers had come off a season debut of ten catches for 116 yards and a touchdown on 14 targets, winning AFC Offensive Player of the Week, and once he was back, Klint Kubiak and Andrew Janocko used two-tight-end sets on a season-high 52 percent of their snaps. That's the chess piece: Bowers and Michael Mayer on the field together, forcing a defense to choose between base personnel that can't cover and nickel personnel that can't tackle.

The problem is the thing we flagged all week. When your only must-cover player is a tight end, a good defense with a full week to prepare takes the middle of the field away from him and dares the rest of your receivers to win. No Raiders wide receiver averages even four catches a game. Jack Bech is on injured reserve with a broken forearm. Tre Tucker has had exactly one enormous afternoon. Cody White has three touchdowns on three catches, which is a wonderful story and a tiny sample.

Somebody besides 89 has to be a problem. That's the single clearest thing this loss said out loud.

EDGE SIX: THE DISCIPLINE EDGE WAS REAL, AND IT WASN'T ENOUGH

One more that I want to acknowledge honestly, because it's the one edge I think we actually kept.

Kansas City came in averaging over 65 penalty yards per game, in the lower half of the league, with a big chunk of those flags on special teams, where they rank near the top in penalties committed. The Raiders, meanwhile, had been one of the cleanest teams in the NFL through three games, giving up a tiny number of penalty yards.

I called that a cultural flex when I previewed this game, and I stand by it. Kubiak has built a team that doesn't beat itself with free yardage, and in a game expected to come down to a couple of plays, that's a built-in advantage.

But here's the hard truth: clean football is necessary and not sufficient. You can win the penalty battle and still lose the game if you can't run the ball, can't get off the field on third down, and can't make an opponent pay for an undrafted rookie at left tackle. Discipline keeps you in games. Execution wins them.

And this is where Kubiak's anger after the game makes even more sense to me. A coach whose team plays clean and still loses is a coach who knows the gap is in execution, and execution is coachable. That's why he was furious instead of resigned.

THE TAKEAWAY WELL, ONE MORE TIME

The last piece of the plan was the thing this defense does better than anybody: taking the football away.

The Raiders came in leading the NFL with nine takeaways, including a league-high five forced fumbles and four interceptions, tied for the league lead. Nakobe Dean had made a habit of ripping the ball loose in the fourth quarter like it owed him money. Hezekiah Masses had three interceptions in three games as a fifth-round rookie. Trayden Stokes was back after clearing the concussion protocol.

I said before the game that if the Raiders forced even two turnovers, the entire complexion of the afternoon changes.

They didn't get there, and that's the whole ballgame. I warned you two weeks ago that nine takeaways in three games was not a sustainable rate, that this team forced only 16 in seventeen games last season, and that the turnovers had been masking a third-down problem. Against a team that protects the football, the mask comes off.

So the honest conclusion is this: this defense is real, but it is currently built to win games by creating chaos rather than by consistently getting off the field. Those are different things, and the second one is what separates good defenses from the ones that beat Kansas City.

WHAT I STILL BELIEVE AFTER WATCHING THIS

Let me be clear about where I stand, because I don't want this to read like a man abandoning a team after one loss.

This is a 3-1 football team that won three games all of last season. Mahomes being 14-2 against this franchise tells you about the last decade, not about the next two months. Our head coach was angrier than anybody in the building afterward, which is the behavior of a man who thinks this gap is closable and has a plan to close it.

Kubiak said during the week that every game is a one-game Super Bowl and this one was no different. I love that mentality because it's calm and it doesn't blink, and it's the right posture for a road trip to New England.

And the roster is still mostly healthy. Bowers appeared on the injury report with a knee note on Friday and was fine. Jeanty's ankle is being managed. Stokes is back. Nolan Smith has joined the edge rotation and Cesar Ruiz is coming through the door. Aidan O'Connell has been away from the team dealing with his father's passing, and football can wait on that one; all of Raider Nation is sending him love.

THE FIVE-ITEM FIX LIST, UPDATED

So here's what I need to see before and during New England, in order.

One: the interior offensive line. Get Powers-Johnson healthy, integrate Ruiz, and give Rogers a full normal week with the same center and tackle beside him. Everything in this offense, especially the run game, starts here.

Two: third down on defense. Stop needing a turnover to get off the field. Rush lane integrity, coverage discipline on third-and-medium, and no free conversions over the middle.

Three: tackle better in space. Walker forced twelve-plus missed tackles three weeks in a row before he got here. Every team we play from now on has watched that. Angles, leverage, and wrapping up.

Four: a second must-cover player. Whether that's Tucker on a bigger target share, Cody White earning a real snap count, or Carter Renan and Dante Thornton Jr. coming off short-term IR around Week Five, somebody has to make defenses stop doubling Bowers.

Five: convert the hidden yardage. Cole and Gay are weapons. Use them to steal points and field position instead of trading them away.

THE PERSPECTIVE, AND WHAT COMES NEXT

New England is next, on the road. Then Buffalo, the Rams, the Jets, San Francisco, Seattle, and Denver. Nobody in this stretch is going to feel sorry for us.

But I'll give you the same math I gave you yesterday, because it still holds. At 3-1, going 4-4 through this stretch puts this team at 7-5 with the finish in front of it. Going 5-3 makes it a December contender. Even 3-5 leaves it at 6-6, which is double last season's win total with weeks to spare.

Four weeks ago, the most optimistic sentence anybody in the national media would say about this franchise was that Fernando Mendoza might inherit a decent situation someday. Now the first overall pick is standing on the sideline in his helmet learning from a quarterback who made history in his first three games here, the defense leads the league in takeaways, a fifth-round corner has three interceptions, and the linebacker in the middle is in the Fred Warner conversation.

Mahomes is 14-2 against us. Our job for the next few years is to make that number stop growing, and you don't do that in one afternoon in October. You do it by fixing an interior line, finding a second receiver, and learning to get off the field on third down.
