# "WE WEREN'T READY TO GO." — KUBIAK'S BRUTAL ADMISSION AFTER NEW ENGLAND 45, RAIDERS 17 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, I'm not going to spin this one. I'm not going to find you a silver lining in the first ninety seconds and tell you everything is fine.

**New England 45. Las Vegas 17.**

That's not a loss. That's a dismantling. And the man in charge said the single most damning sentence a head coach can say at a podium:

**"It's on me to get us ready to go, and we weren't ready to go."**

So today we're doing the press conference format, and we're doing it honestly. Every significant quote from Klint Kubiak and Kirk Cousins, what each one actually admits, and then my own criticism — of the play-calling, of the defensive plan, of the discipline, of the decision to play a hurt running back, and of what this result does to everything we've been building toward for a month.

## THE OPENING STATEMENT

**Kubiak: "Very disappointing performance today from everybody, players and coaches. Obviously, give a lot of credit to the Patriots. They did a great job. We had eight penalties today — far too many. It's a game that we have to move forward from very quickly. We have everything ahead of us as a team. We're not happy with what happened today. We've got to go fix it and move on."**

Everybody. Players and coaches. Eight penalties.

Let me start with the number, because it's the most un-Raiders-like thing about this entire afternoon. This team had been one of the most disciplined in football through four weeks. Discipline was on the short list of things that were genuinely working — I put it in the "preserved advantages" column when I broke down the Kansas City film.

Eight penalties in a 45-17 road loss is not bad luck. That's a team that lost its composure, and composure is the one thing that is supposed to be free.

## "I DON'T THINK ANY OF US PLAYED WELL ENOUGH TO WIN"

**Reporter: "Coach, as a whole, what went wrong today?"**
**Kubiak: "Well, defensively, offensively, special teams — I don't think any of us played well enough to win today. And credit to the Patriots. They did a great job."**

This matters because it's accurate, and accuracy is the first step to a fix.

Raider Nation, through four games this team always had one phase carrying it. Week One the defense traveled. Week Two the secondary produced two interceptions. Week Three the fourth quarter produced thirteen unanswered points. Week Four the tight ends produced 168 yards.

Sunday there was nothing. No phase held. That had not happened once this season, and it's the clearest possible signal that this wasn't a matchup problem — it was a readiness problem.

## THE MOST DAMNING QUOTE OF THE DAY

**Reporter: "Did you pinpoint a reason why the team came out the way it did today?"**
**Kubiak: "You know, it's on me to get us ready to go, and we weren't ready to go."**

I want to sit on this one, because there are two completely different ways to read it and both are worth your time.

The honorable reading: this is the fourth consecutive week we've watched this man absorb blame that he could have distributed. He blew the fourth-down call in Kansas City and said "I blew it." He didn't get the final play in and said so. He took the run game on himself. Now he's taking the entire emotional state of a football team on himself. That is leadership, and it's the reason I believe the locker room will respond.

The uncomfortable reading: **he's right.** A first-year head coach walked a 3-1 team into the home stadium of the reigning AFC champion, on a 10 a.m. body clock, in the elements, and his team was not ready. Preparation is the job. That is the actual job description. And this is now two straight weeks where the staff has been out-prepared — out-coached by Andy Reid in one sequence, and out-prepared by Mike Vrabel for sixty minutes.

One sequence against Reid is tuition. Forty-five points against Vrabel is a bill.

## THE RUN GAME CONFESSION

Here's where my criticism gets sharpest, and the coach handed me the ammunition himself.

**Reporter: "[He] only had one carry. What led to that?"**
**Kubiak: "Well, playing from behind the whole game, and there were plenty of opportunities to get him in later in the game, but it was a passing game. And early in the game especially, I needed to stay with the run. I didn't need to abandon it so fast. So that's on me."**

**"I abandoned the run game too early and didn't give it a chance to get going."**

Raider Nation, listen to me carefully. **This is the third straight week where what this staff says its identity is and what this staff actually calls have gone in opposite directions.**

Four days ago Kubiak stood at a podium and said he does not want Las Vegas becoming a pass-dependent offense. He said the staff was working diligently to recapture the balance from the Miami game. He said it with conviction and I believed him.

Then the first adversity arrived and the run game was gone by the middle of the second quarter.

And I understand the scoreboard logic — when you're down multiple scores you have to throw. But that's exactly the trap, and it's circular: you abandon the run because you're behind, which makes you one-dimensional, which makes it easier to defend you, which keeps you behind. The only way out is to stay committed through the ugly early carries, and he admitted he didn't.

He owned it fully and immediately. I respect that. But owning a mistake is not the same as not making it, and this specific mistake has now cost this offense its stated identity in three consecutive games.

## THE PLAY THAT SWUNG IT

**Reporter: "Down 14-7 in the second quarter — that toss play resulted in an 11-yard loss. How much of a momentum swing was that?"**
**Kubiak: "That was a huge moment. Tried to make an aggressive play, their defensive end made a great play and sniffed it out. You took a big negative loss there. That was a big play in the game."**

And Cousins described the same play: an end-tip reverse with Tre Tucker that lost eleven.

Here's my criticism, and it's a philosophical one.

At 14-7, in a hostile building, with three healthy receivers and a running back on a bad foot, the correct play is the boring one. Trick plays are a luxury purchased with margin, and this offense had no margin. A reverse that loses eleven yards doesn't just cost you a down — it hands the defense second-and-twenty-one, which is the most helpless situation in football.

Which brings us directly to the structural problem Kubiak named himself:

**Reporter: "The negative plays and the penalties put you behind the sticks. From a play caller's standpoint, how difficult is that to overcome?"**
**Kubiak: "Second and long, third and long — that's advantage defense every time."**

Every time. He's right. And eight penalties plus negative plays is a machine for manufacturing exactly that.

## THE DEFENSIVE COLLAPSE — AND THE WORST EXCHANGE OF THE PRESSER

Now the forty-five points, and this is the part that genuinely alarms me.

**Reporter: "There were a handful of situations where your corners were in one-on-one coverage. Did you see safety help? Was that the plan coming in, to be aggressive in that regard? And did you make any adjustments to that?"**
**Kubiak: "Obviously not, you know, obviously not. They kept going after us and they made big plays, and credit to them."**

**"Obviously not."**

Raider Nation, that is a head coach confirming his defense left young cornerbacks on an island, got punished for it repeatedly, and did not fix it during the game.

And on the pass rush:

**Reporter: "It didn't seem like the pass rush was getting home on Drake today. Where do you think they can affect the quarterback better?"**
**Kubiak: "Credit to New England. They protected well and they threw the ball down the field really well."**

So let's connect those two answers, because together they're the entire story of this defense's afternoon. No pressure on the quarterback, no help over the top for the corners. That is the worst possible combination in football. A quarterback with a clean pocket and a receiver with no safety in the picture is a touchdown waiting for somebody to draw it up.

And remember what Mike Vrabel told his own radio audience two weeks ago: his concerns were the Raiders' pass rush and their takeaway production. He named our two weapons publicly, built a protection plan to neutralize the first, and then attacked the coverage the second one depended on. He told us what he was worried about and then made sure neither one happened.

That's a head coach winning a chess match in public, and we have to be honest about it.

## THE IDENTITY THAT VANISHED

Here's the part that should concern you most, and I warned about this specific risk two weeks ago when I listed the things we were leaving to regression.

This defense's entire identity was built on two things: taking the football away, and owning the fourth quarter. Nine takeaways in the first three games led the league. The fourth-quarter streak was historic.

The takeaways dried up against Kansas City. And Sunday the defense surrendered 45 points to a team that threw it down the field at will.

I said at the time that the takeaway rate was unsustainable and that a regression was coming. But I also said this: when the turnovers stop, you find out what your defense actually is without them. We just found out. Without takeaways, this is a young unit with a thin third level that can be attacked vertically — and now that's on tape for every offensive coordinator left on the schedule.

## THE CORNERBACK ROTATION

**Reporter: "What was the reason for subbing Zeke and Darien in and out?"**
**Kubiak: "We just — that was the plan going in. We wanted to get both guys on the field."**

So the Darien Porter conversation we discussed after the Kansas City tape was real, and it produced a rotation with Hezekiah Masses rather than a replacement.

My honest take: I understand the intent and I question the timing. Rotating two young corners in a hostile road environment against an offense throwing deep is a developmental decision, not a winning-this-game decision. Communication in the secondary depends on repetition between the same bodies, and you just introduced a variable into the one area of your defense that was already the thinnest.

Give both of them the reps — absolutely. But in a game where "obviously not" is the answer to whether you adjusted your coverage help, adding rotation on top of it compounded the problem.

## SPECIAL TEAMS, AGAIN

**Reporter: "There were special teams mistakes as well. Did that surprise you a little bit today?"**
**Kubiak: "Yeah, it did. It did. That's been a strength of our team. I see it as a strength moving forward, and we have to go to work to get it fixed."**

Raider Nation, I have to say this plainly: it should not have surprised him.

This is the third consecutive week of special teams failure. Dylan Laube's fumble. Dariq Young's fumble that became a Kansas City touchdown. The fake punt conversion. The unit drifting back on fourth-and-nine.

And Joe DeCamillis told us this was coming. He stood at a podium four days ago and said, in his own words, that New England just activated one of the best special teams players in football, that Vrabel pours time into this phase and loves fakes, and that "we better eliminate" the one bad play.

They didn't eliminate it. The coordinator identified the exact threat, named it publicly, and it happened anyway. That's not an information failure. That's an execution failure, and at some point execution failures in the same phase for three straight weeks become a coaching question.

## THE JEANTY DECISION, AND MY REAL CRITICISM

**Reporter: "Do you feel that Ashton was still hampered by injury at all today?"**
**Kubiak: "I think everyone is beat up. You know, that's the league. And yes, Ashton is beat up, and it's a testament to how tough he is that he's out there playing today for his teammates."**

So he played. A running back with a foot injury and an ankle injury, who was limited Thursday and did not practice at all Friday, played in a game that was out of reach by the second half.

And Raider Nation, I want to be very careful and very clear here, because this is about a 22-year-old's body.

I am not going to question his toughness — the coach is right that playing through that for his teammates says something about him. I'm also not going to pretend I know more than the medical staff that cleared him.

What I will question is the football logic. We spent an entire week discussing whether a week of rest could restore the burst that made him the sixth overall pick. The answer the organization gave was to play him in a road game that ended 45-17, in which the run game was abandoned by the second quarter anyway.

Think about that sequence. You played a hurt back, and then you didn't run the ball. Whatever risk you accepted, you got almost no reward for accepting it. **That's the decision I'd want explained more than any other from this afternoon.**

## MENDOZA'S DEBUT — AND LET'S NOT START THAT NARRATIVE

**Reporter: "What was the decision to have Mendoza come out there for that final drive?"**
**Kubiak: "Yeah, I just wanted to get him some exposure."**
**Reporter: "How did he handle his first drive?"**
**Kubiak: "He did a good job. Yeah, he did a good job."**
**Reporter: "How does game action help his development going forward?"**
**Kubiak: "All reps are valuable. Very, very important for him."**

And when asked directly whether this changes anything at quarterback, Kubiak said it does not.

Cousins on the rookie: **"Did a nice job competing. I thought he played well."**

Let me be the voice of reason here before the internet runs wild. Fernando Mendoza taking a garbage-time drive in a 28-point game is not the beginning of a quarterback change. It's a coach using a lost afternoon to get his first overall pick a few real NFL snaps, which is exactly what a sensible organization does with a blowout.

But I'd be lying if I said it carried no weight at all. When the number one overall pick enters a game in Week Five, the scoreboard has already made a statement — and the fact that a veteran quarterback who'd never been pulled this season watched the last drive from the sideline is its own kind of marker for this season.

The quarterback situation hasn't changed. The *conversation* has gotten one degree louder. Those are different things, and I'm telling you which one actually happened.

## COUSINS DELIVERED THE BEST ANALYSIS IN THE ROOM

And now the man who explained this loss better than anybody.

**Reporter: "How does the margin of error change when the defense knows you're passing?"**
**Cousins: "Anytime you're one-dimensional, it probably does make it a little bit easier on a defense. And playing from a lead, they can afford to be flexible with what they're doing because they've created a bit of a cushion. But you certainly want that run-pass threat at all times if you can have it."**

Raider Nation, that is the thesis of this entire channel for the last week, delivered in reverse.

For seven days we've been talking about stacked boxes — that defenses were loading up because they didn't fear the pass, and the cure was a receiver who forces a safety to stay deep. Sunday was the mirror image. New England got a lead, the run disappeared, and they got to play pass defense for two and a half hours with the luxury of a cushion.

Either way the principle is identical: **the moment a defense knows what you're going to do, you lose.** We've now lost two straight games to that principle from opposite directions.

## COUSINS ON THEIR DEFENSE, AND ON THE REVERSE

**Reporter: "Was there anything this week that hinted to you as a veteran that this was coming?"**
**Cousins: "I don't think you ever expect this, but there was a lot of respect for their defense. I was here last year and didn't play, but preparing for them last season and watching their run to the Super Bowl, you saw an elite defense — a Super Bowl caliber defense — and felt that all week as we were preparing. Just that we were playing a very, very good defense."**

This is worth remembering when you grade this result. That's a reigning AFC champion's defense, in its own building, and this team's veteran quarterback says they felt it all week in preparation.

That's context. It is not an excuse, and notice that Cousins didn't offer it as one.

On the blitz: **"Maybe a different answer, a different issue on each play. But they did a nice job with their pressures getting home quickly and made it hard for us."**

A different problem on every snap is actually the most worrying version of that answer. One repeated breakdown is fixable with one correction. Different failures on different plays means the protection plan as a whole didn't hold — and this is a unit that leads the NFL in pass blocking success rate. That's how good New England's pressure package was.

And on the reverse: **"As you point to certain plays in the game that can lead to a tough outcome, that's certainly one of them. There were others. You feel that tension on every snap, that any play can be the difference, good or bad. So you play with that tension even on the ones that appear insignificant to the eye watching from a distance. So that was one of those plays where you say, man, could the game have been different — but it certainly was more than just that."**

"It certainly was more than just that." A 38-year-old quarterback refusing to let one play carry the blame for a 28-point loss. That's the correct instinct, and it's the right thing for his teammates to hear.

## ON BOWERS, AND THE THING NOBODY SOLVED

**Reporter: "It seemed like you had a mismatch with Brock against some of their secondary players, and that secondary was banged up. Could you have taken advantage of that more?"**
**Kubiak: "I think we did not get it done as a staff, as an offense today."**
**Cousins: "Hard to say until I watch it. We targeted Brock certainly. I don't even know the stats yet, so I don't want to speak from an uneducated place. But at times it felt like we were targeting pretty heavily, and I just would have loved to have been able to make more plays — whether it was Brock or anybody else — just to keep us on the field that first half."**

**"Just to keep us on the field that first half."**

Raider Nation, there's the receiver crisis in one sentence from the quarterback. Four New England corners were reportedly unavailable coming into this game. Their secondary was banged up. That was supposed to be our opening — I told you to watch for a deep shot in the first three snaps.

And with three healthy wide receivers, this offense could not stay on the field.

That is the most direct argument for the trade deadline I have heard all season, and it came from the quarterback's mouth without him trying to make it.

## THE CULTURE ANSWER — AND IT'S THE BEST THING IN HERE

**Reporter: "Nick Saban talks about rat poison when you start doing some things well. Was there an element of that — a young team learning how to become a winner?"**
**Kubiak: "I think it's going to show a lot about who we are, how mature we are, how good our leadership is — myself and our players. And it's an opportunity for us to move forward. We have our whole season ahead of us, and we can't let this game beat us twice."**

**"We can't let this game beat us twice."**

That's the line. That's the one going on the wall of the facility this week, and it should be.

**Reporter: "What's the message to the team going forward?"**
**Kubiak: "What I told the guys is: what makes you a loser is if you are pointing fingers at other people. We're all trying to look interior, look at what we can do better, and move forward."**

**"What makes you a loser is pointing fingers at other people."**

Raider Nation, this franchise has spent the better part of two decades losing exactly that way — leaks, blame, anonymous quotes, somebody else's fault. Four men have now taken public accountability in two weeks: the head coach, the quarterback, the special teams coordinator, and a rookie running back with a hurt foot.

And then: **"I love our guys. I love our guys, and we didn't get it done as a group today. I know that they're going to be ready to compete moving forward."**

The culture is intact. The football was not.

## COUSINS CLOSED IT PERFECTLY

**Reporter: "You've got a tough stretch of games coming up. What's the key to rebound?"**
**Cousins: "Every week is its own entity. You have to come in Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday morning and just — hey, it's a new week, fresh start. You really have to, as best you can, mentally and emotionally create a segmented week. You start 0-0 next week. You don't start behind the eight ball because you had a tough game the previous Sunday. It's the same start as if we had a big win today. The ability to have each week be its own entity, whether winning or losing, is really important for our football team."**

Fourteen years of starts talking. "You start 0-0 next week" is the only sane way to process a 45-17 loss, and it's exactly why this team signed him.

## MY VERDICT: WHAT THIS ACTUALLY CHANGED

So let me give you the honest accounting, because you deserve better than panic and better than denial.

**What didn't change:** this team is 3-2. The pass protection still leads the league in success rate. Matt Gay is still perfect. Bowers is still an elite tight end. Cousins still gets the ball out. The culture, by every piece of evidence available, is genuinely healthy.

**What did change, and this is the part that hurts:** the margin is gone. A 4-1 team with a league-leading takeaway rate was a buyer at the deadline — Jeremy Fowler reported it, and we spent a week pricing Khalil Mack, Jerry Jeudy and Marvin Harrison Jr. A 3-2 team that just lost by 28 is not a buyer. It's a team that has to prove something first.

**That is the real cost of Sunday.** Not the standings. The leverage. Every receiver conversation we've had for a month just got frozen, and the deadline is three weeks away.

And the warning signs I listed are being hit one by one. I said to worry if the penalties came back — eight. I said to worry if the defense couldn't generate pressure without Crosby winning alone — no pressure on Drake Maye. I said to worry if the special teams mistakes repeated — third straight week.

## THE FIX LIST

Five things, and they're all correctable, which is the one genuinely encouraging thing I can tell you.

**One: eight penalties has to become two or three.** Kubiak said it isn't up to their standard, and he's right. This is the cheapest fix available and it's pure focus.

**Two: stay with the run through the ugly part.** He confessed to abandoning it early. The test is whether the confession changes the next call sheet.

**Three: help the young corners.** If your plan is aggressive single coverage and it gets punished in the first quarter, you adjust in the second. "Obviously not" cannot be the answer again.

**Four: the special teams has to stop being the phase that loses games.** One bad play per week, three weeks running, is now a pattern.

**Five: get a receiver.** Cousins said he wanted to keep the offense on the field in the first half and couldn't. With four of New England's corners out. There is no stronger evidence than that.
