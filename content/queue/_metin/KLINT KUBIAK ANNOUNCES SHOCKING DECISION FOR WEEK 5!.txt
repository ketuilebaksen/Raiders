# KLINT KUBIAK ANNOUNCES SHOCKING DECISION FOR WEEK 5! — "Is JPJ Good To Go?" "Yes." "Will He Start?" "We'll See." Plus a New Jeanty Injury Nobody Saw Coming 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, I just went through every word of this week's media availability — Klint Kubiak, Joe DeCamillis, and Kirk Cousins — and there is a decision buried in there that is going to divide this fan base.

Two days ago on this channel I made the case that Jackson Powers-Johnson should walk straight back into the starting lineup the moment he's cleared. Well, he's cleared. A reporter asked Kubiak directly if JPJ is good to go for the weekend, and the answer was one word: "Yes."

Then came the follow-up. Do you expect him to rejoin the starting lineup?

**"Uh, we'll see."**

That's the shocking decision of Week Five, Raider Nation — or more precisely, the decision this head coach is refusing to announce. A healthy second-round interior lineman, on a team whose interior line has been its biggest weakness for three straight weeks, and the honest answer from the man in charge is that he hasn't committed.

And that's not the only surprise in here. There's a new injury on Ashton Jeanty that changes the conversation entirely, Nailor is out, Thornton still isn't activated, and Joe DeCamillis delivered one of the most brutally honest self-assessments I've ever heard from a coordinator.

So we're doing the press conference format today. Quote, then what it actually means, then what it means for Sunday. I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. Hit that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we chase 6,000 believers. Let's get into it.

## THE DECISION: "WE'LL SEE"

Let me put the full exchange in front of you, because the sequence matters.

**Reporter: "Is JPJ good to go for the weekend?"**
**Kubiak: "Yes."**
**Reporter: "And do you expect him to rejoin the starting lineup?"**
**Kubiak: "Uh, we'll see."**

Raider Nation, in coach language, "we'll see" after an unqualified "yes" is not nothing. If the answer were obvious, the answer would have been "yes" twice.

So what's actually happening here? I see three possibilities and I think it's some combination of all of them.

The first is workload. He missed Week Four with a groin injury, and a groin is exactly the kind of injury where a lineman can be cleared to play without being cleared to play 70 snaps in cold, wet weather. Maybe this is a rotation plan rather than a benching.

The second is continuity. This staff has talked openly about wanting the same five men building chemistry together, and Caleb Rogers started against Kansas City. I argued two days ago that continuity stops being a virtue when the unit is struggling, and I stand by it — but I understand the other side of the argument.

The third is competition, and this is the one I'd actually respect. If this staff is telling a talented young player that the starting job is earned in practice rather than returned automatically after an absence, that is a culture statement. It's the same philosophy that produced Hezekiah Masses starting as a rookie and Cody White earning real offensive snaps off the practice squad.

**My position hasn't moved.** If he's healthy, JPJ is one of the five best offensive linemen on this roster, and the interior has not been good enough for us to ignore a possible upgrade. I'd start him. But I'm not going to pretend "we'll see" is an insult to the player — it might just be a coach who refuses to announce a lineup on a Wednesday against a well-coached opponent.

## THE NEW JEANTY INJURY

Now the part of this presser that genuinely surprised me.

The questioning opened on **a foot**. Not the ankle we've been discussing for two weeks. A foot.

**Reporter: "As far as the foot, was that something that happened for Ashton against KC, or was it something in practice since it popped up?"**
**Kubiak: "Yeah. Not going to get into specifics, but, you know, he's going to do his best to go."**

"He's going to do his best to go." That is not the language of a coach describing a healthy running back.

He confirmed Jeanty will travel with the team. And then he gave the most revealing answer of the entire availability when asked about his concern level:

**"You know, it's that time of year. Guys are getting banged up, and if he can't go, then we got to get the next man up."**

Raider Nation, read that again. **"If he can't go."** The head coach is openly preparing for the possibility that his franchise rookie running back does not play in Foxborough.

## AND HE DEFENDED HIM ANYWAY

Then came the question everybody on football Twitter has been asking, and the answer tells you a lot about this coaching staff.

**Reporter: "Do you think it's something that's affected his performance at all the last few weeks?"**
**Kubiak: "I think he's been doing a great job, and again, he's a leader on our team. He's a guy we're counting on, and I couldn't be more happy with Ashton. We got to keep getting him and the entire offense playing better every week. But no, I don't think so."**

So the coach says the injuries have not affected his play, and that the burden is on the offense as a whole to get better — not on the rookie.

Now, I'm going to be honest with you the way I always am: the film says something slightly different. We talked about this when I graded the Kansas City tape. The burst we saw at Boise State hasn't been there, and Jeanty himself said after the loss that his ankle is still in the recovery process.

But understand the position Kubiak is in. He is not going to stand at a podium and tell the league that his starting running back is compromised, and he is not going to let a 22-year-old read a quote from his own head coach suggesting he's been dragging the offense down. That's not spin. That's leadership. The word "leader" showing up in that answer is deliberate.

And notice the pattern, because it is now undeniable: this man took the blame for the fourth-down call, took the blame for the final play not getting in, took the blame for the run game, and has now taken the blame for Jeanty's production. Four separate times in two weeks, the arrow has pointed up instead of down.

## THE WIDE RECEIVER REALITY

The depth news is now official, and it's not good.

**Reporter: "With Jalen out and Dante not activated yet, how does that challenge guys like Malik and Cody to step up?"**
**Kubiak: "Yeah, I know. They've been challenged all year, and that's no different this week."**

So let's be precise about what we know. Jalen Nailor is **out**. I told you two days ago I didn't expect him to clear protocol this week, and that's where it landed. Dante Thornton Jr. is back practicing but has **not been activated** to the 53-man roster yet.

Which means the receiver room on Sunday is Tre Tucker, Cody White, and Malik Benson, with Bech on injured reserve.

That's it. That's the group going into Foxborough against a defense Kubiak described as follows:

**"Very well coached. High effort, take the ball away. Just a very impressive defense, and that's obviously going back two years. I have a ton of respect for what they do schematically and how hard they play."**

"Take the ball away." He said that first, before anything else. New England's defense is built on turnovers, and we're going in there with three healthy receivers in rain and wind. Every single possession is going to matter.

## THE MYSTERY SECONDARY

And here's the flip side that keeps me optimistic, and it's even better than what we discussed two days ago.

**Reporter: "When you're preparing for a team, it's kind of a mystery who's going to be in the secondary for them with four guys missing. How do you prepare when you're not sure who's going to be out there?"**
**Kubiak: "Yeah, you just worry about yourself and worry about our guys getting better. It's the NFL, injuries happen and the next guy steps up."**

**Four guys missing** in that secondary. Not one, not two — four, according to the reporter in that room.

Kubiak's answer is exactly right publicly and tells you nothing, which is the point. But understand what a coaching staff actually does with this information: you don't build a game plan around one specific opponent's weakness when you don't know who's playing. You build concepts that work against *any* coverage, then you attack whatever shows up with your in-game adjustments.

Which is why what Cousins said later in this same availability is the most important thing anybody said all week.

## COUSINS ON STACKED BOXES — THE QUOTE OF THE WEEK

I have been hammering one point on this channel all week: the Raiders have faced the second-most stacked boxes in football, and you cannot fix a numbers problem with effort.

A reporter asked Cousins about exactly that.

**Reporter: "You guys face stacked boxes quite a bit. What challenges do those defensive looks present when trying to establish a consistent run game?"**
**Cousins: "Well, it just becomes a little bit of the math of how many players are down inside the tight end box, and do we have angles, do we have numbers, or don't we? And it changes play to play and even within the play — sometimes they can start with eight in the box and then be bailing out on the snap, or they can start with seven in the box and move down at the snap. So it's kind of a fluid deal, but teams are always going to be in that cat-and-mouse game of how you're trying to defend or how you're trying to attack the defense."**

Raider Nation, that is the starting quarterback of this football team describing the problem as **math**. Angles and numbers. Do we have them or don't we?

That's not me theorizing on a YouTube channel. That's the man taking the snaps confirming that defenses are solving this offense with arithmetic before the ball is snapped.

And look at the detail he added, because it's the part most fans don't appreciate. Defenses aren't just lining up with eight in the box and staying there. They're starting with eight and bailing out at the snap, or starting with seven and dropping a safety down as the ball moves. That means the offense's read isn't made before the play — it's made *during* it. Which is precisely why a quarterback with fourteen years of experience is worth what this team is paying him, and precisely why the answer to stacked boxes isn't a different running back. It's a receiver the defense can't afford to leave uncovered, so the math stops being fluid and starts being honest.

## COUSINS ON CODY WHITE, AND A DETAIL I LOVED

Then came my favorite exchange of the whole week.

**Reporter: "Why do you think it is that pretty much every time Cody White gets an opportunity, he delivers?"**
**Cousins: "It's a good question. If you watch the tape, there's a good two more opportunities that I felt I missed that would have given him a chance to really impact the game, so I was kicking myself for those plays. But the ball just has found him. I don't have a great answer for you... he's attacked the football. I think that's the trait that I would say has jumped off the tape, whether it was the Chargers touchdown or the go-ball last week. Just attacking the football and saying, 'ball's in the air, that's my ball.'"**

First, notice that a 38-year-old quarterback answered a question about someone else's success by naming two throws he missed. That's the third different person in this building this week taking blame that wasn't assigned to him.

Second, "ball's in the air, that's my ball" is the single best description of what this receiver room has been missing. Not speed. Not route polish. **Possessiveness.** The belief that a contested ball belongs to you.

And then the detail that made me grin:

**Reporter: "Is there a moment that sticks out in terms of when he earned your trust?"**
**Cousins: "I think probably going back to watching him at Michigan State a little bit as an alumni. Seeing him make plays there in the number seven. And then that play against the Chargers, where he attacked the football for a touchdown in tight coverage."**

Cousins is a Michigan State man. He was watching Cody White catch passes in East Lansing years before either of them wore silver and black. That's not a coincidence story — that's a quarterback who had already formed an opinion about a player's hands long before he had to throw him a touchdown in tight coverage at SoFi.

## COUSINS ON THORNTON, AND ON THIS TEAM

On Thornton's return, Cousins was warm in a way that tells you something about the locker room.

**"He was able to be out there in OTAs, and it's been hard to get him in the mix as much since. So it's been a while. It'll be good to get him back out there and get him playing and reacclimating. But he's a great teammate. He's a great person. He's worked really hard. He stayed patient, had a great attitude — this is the kind of guy you really pull for."**

Patient. Great attitude. Hasn't practiced since August 31st and nobody's heard a complaint. That's a fourth-round pick handling the worst version of his first real season the right way, and it's why I want him to get those three targets on Sunday.

And then the question about what he's learned in four games, which produced the line I'll remember from this whole press conference:

**"I think it's been a resilient group. I think our defense has played very well. I think guys are making plays. I think it's probably less learning and more confirming what I learned about them back in the spring or even in training camp. These guys, they want to do it the right way. Football means something to them. They're hardworking. Listen well. It makes it a joy to be the quarterback for a group of guys like that. But you've got to go prove it again every week."**

**"Less learning and more confirming."** Raider Nation, that's a veteran telling you the 3-1 start wasn't a fluke that surprised him — it's the thing he already believed showing up on Sundays.

And on the mindset after the loss: "You just go right back to work. Monday we had a good workout, good meetings. Tuesday a lot of guys are in here doing a myriad of things. You just go through your weekly process, your rhythm, your routine."

No speeches. No drama. Process.

## DECAMILLIS: THE MOST HONEST MAN IN THE BUILDING

Now the special teams portion, and I want to warn you: Joe DeCamillis did not protect himself at all in this one.

On the onside kick delay:

**"I don't think it was anything we had getting set up. It was more with the officials, just some things they wanted to make sure they had squared away. Matt has done that onside kick a few times. Would have liked to have gotten a little better execution on that, but he still had a shot at it. Worthy has to come in and dive in and go get it. It's just one of those things with the officials. It's a new play for them, too."**

So the delay was officiating, not disorganization. And asked whether that delay killed the surprise element: "I don't know. I think if we execute the kick better, who knows? But that's something you'd have to ask them."

Fair answer. Gay had a real shot at it, and it took a Kansas City player diving on the football to end it.

## "A TOTAL MISPLAY ON MY PART. I GOT WAY TOO GREEDY."

Then the kickoff-return decision, and this is the quote of the day.

**"Yeah, that was a total misplay on my part. I got way too greedy. That was not for that part of the field. And really I should have been much smarter than that, because the last time we played them in Kansas City, we ran a fake, got a first down on him, and we also got a turnover. So I guarantee he was probably irritated just like I was after this game. It's just how it goes. But it was a great call on their part, and I'll learn from that situation and not get greedy."**

Raider Nation, that is a veteran coordinator standing at a podium and saying "I got greedy, I should have known better, and the other guy out-coached me."

He even explained the scheme honestly:

**"We've done it about — we did it in the first game too against Miami. We did it from a little better position. You're trying to block people downfield. It's something a lot of people do around the league. I saw on some podcast where guys said, 'I've never seen anything like that.' Well, we scored a touchdown against you, dude, so obviously you have. It's just one of those things schematically that you try to work on, and it was obviously a bad play at that point in the game. Defense bailed us out though. They did a heck of a job. I think we ended up getting a touchdown on that drive, so kudos to them."**

That's accountability with a sense of humor and credit handed to the defense in the same breath. I'll take that coordinator every day of the week.

## ON DARIQ YOUNG'S FUMBLE — AND WHY I RESPECT THIS ANSWER

And here's where DeCamillis did something I genuinely admire.

**"Was a great punch by that kid. It was very similar to Dylan's. We got whipped right at the point of attack when they were coming down field. And in my opinion, that's more of a team look, not Dariq's look. Dariq feels horrible. All of us feel horrible. But we have got to quit the one bad play where we're letting guys get to the returner that quickly. We've got to eliminate that, and that's been a focus this week."**

A reporter then floated that Young was maybe "dancing a little" and getting cute with the football. Listen to the answer:

**"No, I didn't see that, really getting cute with the ball. I just saw more people there than should have been there. We didn't do as good a job blocking on that play as we need to. So you say dancing, but I'm dancing too when there's 12 people in front of me. So I just think we did a poor job of execution all the way around on that."**

"I'm dancing too when there's 12 people in front of me."

That is a coach refusing to let a young returner get scapegoated for a blocking failure in front of him, and tying it directly to Dylan Laube's fumble the week before as the same structural problem. Two return fumbles in two weeks, and the coordinator's diagnosis is the same both times: we're letting defenders reach the returner far too fast.

Raider Nation, that's not an excuse. That's a correct diagnosis — and it's the one that can actually be fixed.

## THE SPECIAL TEAMS WARNING FOR SUNDAY

But then DeCamillis put the whole room on notice, and this is a genuine concern.

**"And I'm going to tell you, this week we better eliminate it, because this is a heck of a group coming in. They just activated Schooler, which — if he's not the best special teams player in the league, he's right up there with everybody else. He's a good one. Really good one. So it's going to be a heck of a challenge for us. I know, playing against Vrabel all those years when I was in Jacksonville and he was in Tennessee — he emphasizes it, both with players and with how much time he spends on it, and the fakes and some of the things he does. So we've got our challenge in front of us."**

Two warnings there, and both are real.

New England just activated one of the best special teams players in football. And Mike Vrabel is a head coach who historically pours time into this phase and isn't afraid to run fakes. So after a week where we gave up a return fumble that became a touchdown and got caught drifting back on a fourth-and-nine, we're walking into the one opponent most likely to punish exactly those mistakes.

## THE CHESS MATCH NOBODY MENTIONED

And there's a detail here almost nobody caught.

**"Our stuff's on tape. I have a lot of tape, so they've looked at a lot of tape. This is another one that's going to be a challenge this week because Jeremy Springer, who's the coordinator there, was my assistant in LA for the first year when I was there. So they've got some info on me too."**

New England's special teams coordinator used to work *for* DeCamillis. He knows his tendencies, his fakes, his philosophy.

So that phase of Sunday's game is a chess match between a mentor and a former assistant, with both men holding the other's playbook. Watch the first punt of this game closely.

## THE WEATHER, AND WHY IT MIGHT HELP US

One more thing, and it's a nice note of optimism.

Kubiak on playing outdoors: **"Rain, wind. That's football. That's the way it's supposed to be played, in the elements."**

And DeCamillis was even more specific: **"You look at the weather reports. Luckily at this place you get a lot of wind, which we've had during training camp and all that stuff — now, it's 115 degrees when it's like that. But I do think being in the wind is going to help Matt and A.J. and Alex. That's really the three that are the most important. And then the returners also."**

This is the first outdoor game of the season for a team that plays in a dome. And the coordinator's honest read is that his kicking operation has actually practiced in wind, because Las Vegas gets plenty of it — just at 115 degrees instead of 50.

A kicker who's 10 for 10 on the season, in a game likely decided by a field goal, with a coordinator who thinks the wind won't rattle his operation. I'll take that.

## SO WHAT DID WE ACTUALLY LEARN?

Let me pull it all together.

The shocking decision is that Jackson Powers-Johnson is cleared and still not guaranteed his starting spot, which tells you this staff values earned reps over reputation — and tells you the guard competition is live right up to Sunday. Ashton Jeanty has a foot issue on top of the ankle and the head coach is openly preparing for "next man up." Jalen Nailor is out, Dante Thornton Jr. isn't activated yet, and three receivers are walking into a defense built on taking the ball away, in rain and wind, against a special teams unit that just got better.

That is a hard week. I'm not going to dress it up.

But here's what I heard underneath all of it. A head coach who takes every bullet himself. A coordinator who said "I got greedy" out loud and refused to let his returner take the fall. And a quarterback who described this group as resilient, said the last four games confirmed rather than taught him who they are, and then blamed himself for two throws he missed to a practice-squad call-up.

Three men, three podiums, zero finger-pointing.
