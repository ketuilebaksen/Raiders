STOCK UP: THE WINNERS OF THE SUMMER

Start with the man whose arrow points straight up: Mike Washington Jr. You've heard his name on this channel all summer, and the final verdict is in — no Raider helped himself more. The explosiveness showed up every single week, but here's the part that locks in his role: he flashed as a RECEIVER too. That matters enormously, because Kubiak's offense lives on the run game and the play-action off it — a back who threatens defenses through the air on checkdowns and swings isn't a luxury in this system, he's a requirement. Washington entered April as a fourth-round flyer Spytek pounded the table for. He enters September as a player whose role could be far bigger than anyone projected. That's what a won summer looks like.

Patrick Johnson deserves his flowers too, and I want to frame this one properly: Johnson entered camp fighting for his professional life, with a hyped practice-squad darling breathing down his neck — and he flat-out EARNED his spot on the initial 53. No leaning on veteran status, no benefit of the doubt. In a summer where this new staff made merit the only currency, Johnson is the proof the message was real.

And on defense, keep your eye on Kwity Paye, because the math around him just keeps improving. Playing next to Maxx Crosby already meant lighter attention and better matchups — offenses simply cannot slide everything toward number 98 without paying on the other side. Paye's versatility fits Rob Leonard's aggressive scheme like a glove, and if the reported Nolan Smith arrival adds even MORE gravity to this front, Paye might be the quiet beneficiary nobody's pricing in. Stock: up, with room to run.

And a quick word on HOW this ledger got graded, because it explains every entry on it. This offseason was never a normal one — under a brand-new head coach, virtually every position on the roster was a live evaluation. Young players got real chances to seize bigger roles. Veterans had to defend their jobs instead of inheriting them. Newcomers had to prove they fit the system, not just the depth chart. The Raiders weren't collecting 53 talented individuals this summer — they were building an IDENTITY under Kubiak, and every practice rep, every preseason snap, every cut was a brick in it. That's the lens: stock didn't rise because a player looked good. It rose because a player looked like he BELONGS in what this team is becoming.

STOCK DOWN: THE PROVE-IT LIST

Now the harder half of the ledger, because this channel doesn't do participation trophies. Malik Benson, rookie receiver — the speed is real, the tools are real, and the DROPS were real too. In a receiver room this thin, August drops aren't a footnote; they're a flashing light, and they're exactly why the front office spent this week adding competition behind him (more on that in a minute). The physical gifts bought Benson a roster spot. Only consistency keeps it. When the opportunity comes now, it has to become a completed play — full stop.

Fernando Mendoza's summer deserves an honest grade too: inconsistent, including the rough stretch against Houston — while Aidan O'Connell's comeback performance that same night lit up the quarterback discussion all over again. But let's be crystal clear about what that means and what it doesn't: the Raiders have NOT wavered on Mendoza. The offseason simply confirmed the plan we've tracked from day one — the rookie needs runway, Cousins provides the bridge, and the QB2 role keeps him one play away while he develops. There's a difference between being excited about the number one pick and rushing him before he's ready, and this building understands it. You know my calendar: before Thanksgiving. Nothing this summer moved that date — in either direction.

Jackson Powers-Johnson closes the prove-it list. He won the right guard job, and camp made clear the staff believes in the talent — but consistency has chased him since spring, and this front office has invested far too much in rebuilding the line to grade anyone on potential. With Caleb Rogers still pushing and a certain veteran interior name still floating in the rumor mill, JPJ's leash is shorter than his draft pedigree suggests. Win the job every week — that's the standard now.

THE BOWERS KNEE WATCH — AND MAYER'S MOMENT

And now the note that made every Raiders fan's stomach drop a little: Brock Bowers is dealing with a KNEE issue heading into Week One. Deep breath — this is being framed as an issue to manage, not a sky-is-falling situation. But let's not pretend it's nothing, either: this offense's entire identity runs through number 89, and even a Bowers at ninety percent changes what Kubiak can call.

Which makes this Michael Mayer's moment — and honestly, the timing is almost poetic. All summer, the conversation around Mayer was "how big can his role really be behind Bowers?" We kept saying the two-tight-end identity makes them partners, not competitors. Well, if Bowers is limited even a little, Mayer doesn't just get more snaps — he inherits real responsibility in the passing game AND as the tone-setting blocker in the run game. These are exactly the moments that flip the perception of a player's entire season. Mayer has the talent; now he might get the volume. Be ready, big fella — the whole Nation is watching.

Think through the game-plan ripples too, because they touch everyone. If Bowers is managed on a snap count, the twelve-personnel packages don't disappear — Ian Thomas and the depth get stress-tested instead, and every target Bowers doesn't command has to land somewhere: more work for Tucker underneath, more chances for Bech to make his year-two case, more pressure on the room we just spent a week dissecting. And there's a silver lining hiding in the caution: this staff has shown all summer — with Jeanty, with Thornton's paperwork, with Crawford's rehab plan — that it plays the LONG game with its cornerstone players. If managing September means a fully weaponized Bowers in December, when the schedule turns cruel and the games get heavy? Take that trade every single time. Championships aren't won in Week One. They're lost there, by teams that couldn't wait.

THE SECOND-CHANCE TRIO: NEAL'S FULL STORY, OJULARI, AND BELL

Now to story two, because even with the roster set, this front office REFUSES to stand still — the practice squad churn continued this week with three arrivals, and each one tells you something about how this building thinks.

Start with the headliner, because now is the perfect time to tell his full story: Evan Neal — the stash we've been celebrating on this channel for a week straight. Understand the scale of this reclamation bet: seventh OVERALL pick, 2022, out of Alabama. The Giants drafted him to anchor their line for a decade — 29 games, 27 starts across three seasons — and then injuries devoured his trajectory, wiping out his entire last season. Now he's in Vegas on a practice squad deal, which means zero risk and pure lottery-ticket upside. Nobody — including me — is penciling him in as a starter. But remember the interior line questions we've documented all month: the seventeen-snap center depth, the Mafi wobbles, the JPJ consistency watch. A 25-year-old former top-ten pick rebuilding his body one building over from that uncertainty? That's not charity. That's ARBITRAGE. If he's healthy, you've found a starter for free. If he's not, you've lost a practice squad slot. Spytek keeps making this exact bet for a reason.

Arrival two: edge rusher Azeez Ojulari — and Raider Nation, look at THIS pattern. Fiftieth overall pick in 2021, out of GEORGIA. He joins a locker room already carrying four other Bulldogs, including Nakobe Dean — his old Athens brother — and get this: Ojulari and Dean were teammates in Philadelphia just last season. Now line that up with the Nolan Smith reports we've been covering — another Georgia edge, another Eagle. The Georgia-Philadelphia pipeline into this building is now impossible to ignore, and it makes complete sense: championship programs, championship habits, schemes that translate. Ojulari has legitimate pass-rush juice when healthy — a second-round pick for a reason — and after the Crawford injury thinned this edge room, stacking low-cost speed with familiarity already baked in is exactly the kind of depth play smart teams make. He knows Dean's calls. He knows Smith's game. He fits before he even unpacks.

The honest fine print on Ojulari, because there's a reason a former fiftieth pick is available for a practice squad deal: health. The talent was never the question — the flashes as a rusher were real from his rookie year — but the seasons kept getting interrupted, and availability is the one stat that ends up defining careers like his. That's also exactly why this structure is perfect for both sides: no roster spot burned, no promises made, just a proving ground. And take a step back and look at what this edge room could become if the pieces land — Crosby and the reported Smith arrival at the top, Paye setting edges, Koonce and Johnson in rotation, Sloan developing, and now Ojulari as the break-glass depth with real pedigree. Two months ago, the Crawford injury left this room four deep and hollow. Today it might be the deepest position group renovation of the entire offseason — built almost entirely out of other teams' discarded first- and second-round bets.

And arrival three: receiver Ronnie Bell — six feet, 192 pounds, seventh-round pick of the 49ers in 2023 out of MICHIGAN. Yes, another Wolverine — Ruiz, Jenkins, Benny, and now Bell; the Michigan thread keeps weaving. His career line is modest — 28 games between San Francisco and New Orleans, ten catches, 113 yards, four touchdowns — but read that last number again: four of his ten career catches ended in the end zone. That's a player who does something with opportunity. And the message of this signing is aimed squarely at the receiver room: after Benson's summer of drops, competition just walked in the door. Nobody's spot in that room is safe below the top two — exactly how it should be.

To make room, the Raiders moved on from three practice squad pieces, including tight end Albert Okwuegbunam and young edge Jahfari Harvey — the cold arithmetic of the roster's basement, where every arrival has a cost. And zoom out one more time, because the pattern across ALL of this churn — Keenan, Laird, Neal, Ojulari, Bell — is unmistakable: pedigree reclamations and pipeline familiarity, stacked at the exact positions we've flagged as thin. The front office reads the same weaknesses we do. It just shops in the bargain bin while it decides whether to shop in the boutique.
