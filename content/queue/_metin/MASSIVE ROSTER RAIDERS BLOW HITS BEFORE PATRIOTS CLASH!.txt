## THE BLOW: THORNTON WILL NOT PLAY

Las Vegas desperately needs help at wide receiver. And Dante Thornton Jr. will not provide it against the Patriots, despite appearing physically close to returning.

Let me set the full context, because the timing of this is brutal.

The Raiders entered Week Five with Jalen Nailor unavailable because of a concussion and Jack Bech still on injured reserve. That's an obvious, screaming need for another receiving option. The organization allowed Thornton to return to practice this week after he'd been sidelined since training camp with a hamstring injury.

And he participated **fully** throughout the week. Fully. Not limited, not managed. So there was legitimate hope that the 6-foot-5 receiver could make his season debut in New England.

Then Kubiak eliminated that possibility entirely by confirming Thornton would not play.

## WHY THIS DECISION IS SURPRISING — AND WHY IT'S PROBABLY RIGHT

This is genuinely surprising, because full practice participation suggests the hamstring has recovered. If he can run all week, why can't he play Sunday?

But here's the thing I want you to understand, Raider Nation: physical clearance is only one part of preparing a receiver to return.

Think about everything Thornton is missing beyond the hamstring. He lost months of valuable practice time. He has **never played in Klint Kubiak's offense in a regular season game** — not one snap, not one series. And he's had extremely limited opportunities to develop timing with Kirk Cousins.

Timing is the whole job for a receiver. A go-ball isn't a fast man running in a straight line; it's a quarterback putting a football at a spot the receiver hasn't reached yet, trusting he'll arrive at the exact moment it lands. That trust is built in hundreds of repetitions, and Thornton hasn't had them since August.

So Las Vegas may simply believe that giving him another week of preparation is safer than dropping him into an important road game, in rain and wind, before he's completely reacclimated.

And honestly? As much as I wanted him out there, I can't argue with that logic. A receiver who's a half-step off on a route in a one-score road game doesn't help you. He gets you an interception.

## WHAT THORNTON HIMSELF SAID

And the way he's handled this tells you who he is.

Thornton told reporters that the extended recovery period allowed him to get his body right, study more of the offense, and prepare himself to return. He described himself as **extremely confident** in the hamstring, and said that when he commits himself to something — whether training, practicing or playing — he believes he can accomplish it.

Raider Nation, that confidence matters. His rookie season was disappointing, and nobody's hiding from that. But his combination of size and speed still gives him characteristics this receiving group flatly does not have.

Look at the room. Las Vegas has smaller receivers capable of creating separation — Tucker with his speed, Cody White with his hands, Benson still developing. What they don't have is a large boundary target who can compete for contested passes.

That's what Thornton could offer Cousins. A 6-foot-5 body on the sideline, and a genuinely useful red zone presence, which is the exact area where Michael Mayer and Brock Bowers have been carrying the entire load.

## COUSINS WAS HONEST ABOUT IT TOO

Cousins praised Thornton's patience, attitude and work ethic, describing him as a great teammate and a player people naturally want to support.

But he also acknowledged something important: it has been a long time since the two worked together consistently. Which makes Thornton's eventual return another developmental process rather than an immediate solution.

That's your veteran quarterback telling you the truth without diminishing a young teammate. Thornton should get an opportunity to establish a role once he returns — potentially as early as Week Six. But the Raiders first need to determine whether his physical talent can translate into dependable execution.

Until then, Las Vegas has to face New England with a depleted receiving room and depend on the players who have already earned Cousins' trust.

## AND THAT BRINGS ME TO MY FAVORITE STORY ON THIS ROSTER

One of those trusted players is Cody White, and I want to spend real time here, because this is the best story on this football team and most of you don't know the half of it.

White entered this season on the practice squad after spending **more than half a decade** moving between active rosters and practice squads in Pittsburgh, Kansas City, Denver and Seattle — where he earned a Super Bowl ring.

Think about that journey. Four organizations. Six-plus years of signing, releasing, re-signing. He was never treated like a future star, and to this day he doesn't receive the defensive attention normally reserved for a number one receiver.

And yet.

**Through the early portion of this season, White has recorded only five receptions — and three of them have produced touchdowns. All five moved the chains.**

Raider Nation, read that one more time. Five catches. Three touchdowns. Five first downs. There is not a single wasted reception on his season ledger.

## WHY HE KEEPS DELIVERING

He has repeatedly moved between the active roster and the practice squad, and those transactions have not changed his preparation or his confidence one bit.

Kubiak said he's proud of White because the receiver keeps making plays whenever his number is called. And that reliability has allowed him to quietly emerge as one of Cousins' preferred red zone options — where timing, positioning and trust matter far more than elite speed.

Which is exactly why his route to this opportunity explains the urgency in his game.

White arrived at Michigan State as a four-star recruit and eventually caught 143 passes with 12 touchdowns, placing him **seventh on that school's career receptions list.** That's not a walk-on story. That was a real college producer.

Then came the pro day. **A 4.66-second 40-yard dash** damaged his draft prospects and contributed to him entering this league as an undrafted free agent.

Raider Nation, that is the entire injustice of the scouting process in one number. One stopwatch reading erased 143 catches.

## HOW HE SURVIVED

So instead of depending exclusively on receiving opportunities, he stayed employed by embracing special teams and accepting temporary roles wherever they became available.

ESPN's Field Yates highlighted White's story after he received a **game ball for his special teams effort and then caught two touchdowns, including a go-ahead score.** A game ball for covering kicks, then two touchdowns on offense. In the same afternoon.

And White told The Athletic's Sam Warren something that I think everybody watching this should sit with. His career taught him to treat every opportunity as something that must count. All of his preparation is designed for the moment the offense calls his number, because he understands another chance is never guaranteed.

He admitted there were periods when his professional future looked bleak enough for him to consider quitting. But his belief that he could make plays at this level kept him moving forward.

He now approaches every game as though it could be his last. And that mentality has earned him the trust of his teammates.

## THE DETAIL I LOVE

There's also that Michigan State link with Cousins, even though the two played there in different eras. Two Spartans, two completely different career paths — a first-round quarterback with fourteen years of starts and an undrafted receiver on his fifth organization — connected by the same school and now by a touchdown in tight coverage.

White explained that becoming dependable requires staying mentally prepared and refusing to disappoint teammates when a play is designed for you.

He may never become the primary receiver in this offense. But he does not need that role to be valuable.

And with Nailor unavailable, Bech on injured reserve, and Thornton still waiting, White's ability to contribute on special teams while converting limited offensive chances could make him more important against New England than at any point in his career.

## THE BIGGER PROBLEM: ASHTON JEANTY

Now the uncertainty that genuinely concerns me, because the Raiders could enter this game without the centerpiece of their backfield.

Let's retrace the whole arc. Jeanty suffered an ankle sprain during practice before the season, but still produced an excellent opening performance against Miami — 102 rushing yards and two touchdown catches.

His effectiveness has declined since that game. He's averaging only 3.4 yards per rushing attempt, which is below the 3.7 he averaged last season, and he hasn't consistently displayed the same burst that defined his Week One performance.

Those struggles created speculation that the ankle has remained more problematic than he's let on. And I want to be careful here, the way I always am with this: I'm not going to accuse a 22-year-old of hiding an injury or a team of concealing one. What I'll say is what the evidence supports — cleared to play and fully explosive are two different conditions, and the second one has not been there since Miami.

## THE PRACTICE REPORT, AND THE OFFICIAL WORD

Then Las Vegas added to the concern. Jeanty was **limited on Thursday because of a foot injury, and did not participate at all on Friday.**

The Raiders have officially listed him as **questionable** for Week Five — although he traveled with the team to New England.

And that travel detail matters. His presence on the trip keeps open the possibility that the missed practice was designed to provide additional recovery time rather than signal an expected absence. Teams don't fly injured players across the country for decoration.

Still, the progression from limited participation to no participation cannot be ignored, particularly for a running back whose entire game depends on healthy feet and ankles. Everything Jeanty does well — the jump cut, the contact balance, the burst through a crease — originates in his lower legs.

So allowing him to rest may ultimately be wiser than forcing him through another game if the injury is contributing to his reduced effectiveness. That's the calculation, and it's not an easy one.

## JEANTY TOOK ACCOUNTABILITY TOO

Here's the part that raised my opinion of this young man.

The offensive line deserves part of the responsibility for the disappointing rushing production — we've spent a week on the box counts and the numbers game. But Jeanty acknowledged that he also has to improve.

He said the Raiders need to execute better, **beginning with himself**, and emphasized the importance of breaking tackles and creating larger gains when opportunities appear. He also identified pass protection and his reads in the running game as areas requiring improvement.

Raider Nation, that is a rookie running back with a hurt foot and a hurt ankle, publicly listing his own flaws instead of pointing at his blockers. Add him to the list. Kubiak took the blame. Cousins took the blame. DeCamillis took the blame. Now the 22-year-old is doing it too.

This is a culture, not a coincidence.

## WHY LOSING HIM WOULD HURT MORE THAN YOU THINK

And his contribution has not been limited to carries, which is the part casual fans miss.

He's already caught **19 passes** this season and expressed excitement about being more involved as a receiver than he was previously.

That versatility would be genuinely difficult to replace. It gives Cousins a dependable underneath option, and more importantly, it forces linebackers to account for Jeanty outside the traditional running game. A linebacker who has to respect a back in the passing game is a linebacker who can't sit in the box and crash downhill.

In other words, Jeanty's receiving role is itself one of the few tools this offense has for unstacking the box. Lose him and you lose a run threat *and* a coverage problem in the same move.

## KUBIAK DOESN'T WANT A PASS-DEPENDENT TEAM

And this is where the philosophy comes in. Kubiak has made it clear he does not want Las Vegas becoming a pass-dependent offense.

Here's the fascinating truth underneath that. The Raiders have reached 3-1 **despite** failing to establish a consistent rushing attack after Week One. That demonstrates real adaptability — this offense has found a way to score more than 28 points a game without its ground game functioning.

But it's also a warning about long-term balance. You can win games in October throwing it 52 times. You cannot win in January in bad weather doing that, and you cannot protect a 38-year-old quarterback across seventeen games that way.

Kubiak said the entire unit executed more efficiently against Miami and emphasized that the staff is working diligently to recapture that performance. That Week One tape is the proof of concept, and it's what they're chasing.

## TWO CONNECTED DECISIONS

So the Patriots game now creates two decisions that are tied together.

First, Las Vegas must determine whether Jeanty is healthy enough to play without increasing the risk of a larger problem. Second, Kubiak must find a way to improve the running game regardless of who takes the carries.

If Jeanty plays, the Raiders need to give him better blocking and help him rediscover the decisive running that showed up against Miami.

If he sits, the remaining backs — Washington Jr. and newly promoted Roman Hemby — must protect Cousins, contribute as receivers, and prevent New England from treating this offense as completely one-dimensional. And I want to be specific about which of those matters most: **pass protection.** A missed blitz pickup by a young back against a Vrabel defense is a strip-sack, and this is a team that takes the ball away.

## THE WAR OF WORDS

Now the story that's creating buzz, and I enjoyed this more than I probably should have.

Let's revisit the play. Las Vegas forced the Chiefs into a fourth down during the first quarter — and Kansas City fooled the punt return unit with a fake. The Raiders sent multiple players downfield to prepare for the return, leaving an open lane for the punter to run through and convert the first down.

The play immediately drew criticism, because the alignment appeared to give Kansas City exactly the opening it needed.

Then former Indianapolis Colts head coach **Chuck Pagano** addressed the mistake on the Pat McAfee Show. And Joe DeCamillis made it very clear that he heard the criticism.

## DECAMILLIS FIRES BACK

His response had two parts, and I respect both of them.

First, the defense of the scheme. He explained that Las Vegas had used a similar approach against Miami because the concept is intended to block coverage players downfield, and noted that other teams around the league use it too.

Then he fired back at Pagano's suggestion that he'd never seen anything like it — reminding the former head coach that DeCamillis' unit once **scored a touchdown against him** with a similar concept.

That's a coordinator saying "you've seen it, because it beat you."

But here's why I take him seriously instead of thinking he's deflecting: he did not attempt to excuse the results against Kansas City. He admitted the call was not appropriate for that part of the field and said he should have been smarter. He also connected the decision to his history against Chiefs special teams coordinator Dave Toub — in their previous meeting in Kansas City, DeCamillis successfully called a fake that earned a first down and created a turnover, which gave Toub plenty of motivation to be ready this time.

So the full picture is: a veteran coordinator who defends his system's logic, admits the specific call was wrong, and acknowledges the opponent out-prepared him. That's not arrogance. That's a professional who knows the difference between a bad concept and a bad moment to use it.

And it matters this week, because New England just activated one of the best special teams players in football and Mike Vrabel is a head coach who pours time into this phase.

## SO HOW BIG IS THIS BLOW, REALLY?

Let me give you the honest accounting.

On the negative side: Thornton is out, so the receiver room is Tucker, Cody White and Malik Benson. Nailor is out. Bech is on IR. Jeanty is questionable with a foot and an ankle after going limited, then out, in practice. Trey Zuhn is out. That's a road game at 10 a.m. Pacific, in the elements, against a 2-2 team that just beat Buffalo, with a defense built on taking the football away.

That is a massive roster blow. I'm not going to soften it.

But here's the other side of the ledger, and it's the reason I'm not writing this team off.

This offense is averaging over 28 points a game and sitting at 3-1 without a functioning run game, without Bech, with Bowers having missed time, and with a receiver room nobody in the league respects. The pass protection leads the NFL in success rate. The kicker is 10 for 10. The defense has been taking the ball away all season. And the guy they keep calling up from the practice squad has turned five catches into three touchdowns.

This team has been winning with depth and attitude all year. Sunday is just the biggest version of the same test.
