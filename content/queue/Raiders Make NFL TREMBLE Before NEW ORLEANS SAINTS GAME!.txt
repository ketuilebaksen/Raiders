THE STAT THAT SAYS EVERYTHING

Let's start with the Stabler stat, because I want you to understand how rare it is.

Three touchdown passes in Week One. Three more in Week Two. Six touchdowns in two games, and not a single Raiders quarterback has opened a season that way since the 1970s. Think about all the quarterbacks who have come through this building since then. All the first-round picks, the veteran bridges, the free agent signings, the guys who were going to be the answer. None of them started a season like this.

And notice how he did it on Sunday. As one analyst put it, Cousins wasn't spectacular because he threw the ball sixty times. He was dangerous because he kept finding the right answer. Nineteen of twenty-nine. Two hundred fifty-three yards. Three touchdowns. Ten different players targeted. That's not a quarterback carrying an offense on his back. That's a quarterback operating a system that's working.

One panelist also framed his season this way: this is a man who controlled where he was going, showed up with something to prove, and is playing like he wants to prove somebody wrong. I can't confirm the details of any of that, and I'm not going to pretend I can. But the attitude is visible on the field. He looks like a quarterback who's enjoying himself for the first time in a while.

THE DECISION EVERYONE IS NOW PRAISING

Now here's the part I really want to highlight, because a month ago this same decision was being criticized.

When the Raiders drafted Fernando Mendoza first overall and didn't hand him the starting job, the national reaction was confusion. As one analyst pointed out this week, it's almost unheard of now. Go back seven or eight years and every quarterback taken first overall has started right away. Every single one.

Klint Kubiak and John Spytek went the other way. They signed a veteran, they let the rookie learn, and they took the pressure off a 22-year-old kid who would otherwise be trying to fix a franchise by himself. And the panel gave real credit to ownership for not forcing the issue, because in a lot of buildings, the pressure to play the first overall pick right away comes from the top and never stops.

Look at where that decision has landed. Two games into the season, the Raiders are 2-0, the veteran quarterback is playing at a level nobody expected, and Mendoza is watching, learning, and getting to breathe. One analyst said it directly: "I thought early in the season they'd go to Mendoza. You can't now. You're 2-0."

And I want to be clear about what that means, because I've seen some confusion in the comments. This isn't bad news for Mendoza. This is the best possible situation for him. He gets to enter the league without carrying a rebuild on his shoulders, he gets to see the speed of the NFL from the sideline, and when he does take over, he'll be walking into a winning locker room instead of a losing one. Patience with a rookie quarterback is a luxury. Winning is what buys it.

HOW THE NATIONAL PANEL SEES THIS TEAM

So what did they actually say about how we're playing? The praise broke into four pieces, and every one of them lines up with what we've been tracking on this channel.

First, the offensive line. One analyst put it simply: you go get Tyler Linderbaum, you get Kolton Miller back healthy, your offensive line is going to be better. That group is giving Cousins the most valuable thing a quarterback can have, which is time.

Second, the run game as a threat rather than a stat line. Ashton Jeanty didn't put up big numbers against the Chargers, and we were honest about that in our recap. But the panel made a point that's worth hearing: it isn't about the rushing total, it's about the efficiency and the threat. When a defense has to respect the run, the play-action windows open, and the quarterback's job gets easier. As one analyst put it, the threat of the run is what makes Cousins a better quarterback.

Third, the scheme. This was my favorite line of the whole segment. One former player described this offense as a system that schemes people open and creates easy throws, and said that if you're the quarterback in it, all you have to do is listen, because the design is telling you where to go with the football. That's a first-year head coach calling his own plays, and he's already got an offense that looks organized. That's not nothing.

Fourth, the defense. They noticed the energy. Young players flying around, attacking, forcing mistakes. And they noticed the philosophy behind it: this organization is letting its young players play and grow instead of burying them. Hezekiah Masses is the perfect example. A young corner gets thrown into a start on the road and comes out with two interceptions.

THE SEQUENCE THAT WON THE GAME

Now let's relive the part of the broadcast that had the studio buzzing, because the way this game turned was beautiful.

It started with the deep shot. Cousins let one go to Tre Tucker for 40 yards and a touchdown, the throw that told the whole league the deep-ball problem from Week One was over. As the analyst said on the highlight, Tucker is turning into a very viable option: explosive, catches it in stride, and knows how to finish in the end zone.

Then Los Angeles answered. In the third quarter, Justin Herbert escaped pressure and found Oronde Gadsden II for a touchdown, and the Chargers took a 14-10 lead. That's the moment where last year's Raiders lose this game. Down four, on the road, in a building where this franchise hadn't won in five straight tries.

This team did the opposite. Cousins found Cody White for a touchdown on a contested catch that one analyst described perfectly: "This is want-to. I want the ball more than you do, and I'm going to get it." A practice-squad-level receiver with one career touchdown coming into the day, outmuscling an NFL defender in a game the Raiders had to have.

Then the defense finished it. Under four minutes to go, Omarion Hampton took the handoff, Nakobe Dean punched the ball out, and Jeremy Chinn scooped it up. It was the sixth fumble of the day in that game and the first one actually lost, and it went to the wrong team at the worst possible time for Los Angeles. On the very next possession, Cousins found Cody White again for his second touchdown of the day.

Sixteen unanswered points after trailing 14-10. On the road. Against Jim Harbaugh, who had beaten this franchise four straight times. Final score: 26-14.

THE CHARGERS SIDE: THE ALARMS ARE GOING OFF

And now the other sideline, because the panel was brutal about it, and there are things in there that matter for our division.

They said the Chargers "stink" right now. They said something looks off. They said that through eight quarters of football this season, Los Angeles has been shoved around in at least seven of them. And the stat they dropped is remarkable: the Chargers are the first team to start 0-2 with both losses coming against teams that lost fourteen or more games the year before.

Their criticism had three parts. One, the receivers. The panel pointed out that Jim Harbaugh's teams have historically never been built around receivers, going back to Stanford, the 49ers, and Michigan, and that their general manager came from a Baltimore organization whose first Pro Bowl receiver arrived only recently. Their best receiver is a slot player, and outside of him, there's not much.

Two, the spacing. They showed a play where two Chargers receivers ended up in the same area and neither caught the ball, and asked the obvious question: where's the spacing in this passing game? That's a direct shot at Mike McDaniel, the coordinator who joked last week that he'd be fine if Maxx Crosby wanted to take a nap. Nobody on our defense took a nap, and nobody on their offense found any room.

Three, the identity. The panel's sharpest point was about toughness. Harbaugh's whole philosophy is "we'll out-tough you." And one analyst said flatly: I'm watching them, and they're not out-toughing anybody.

Now, I want to be fair, because this channel doesn't do cheap shots. Omarion Hampton ran hard and had a productive day before the fumble. Justin Herbert is still an outstanding quarterback having a rough stretch behind a line that can't protect him. And nobody on that panel thought Harbaugh was on any kind of hot seat. As one of them said, sometimes you can't coach your way out of a personnel problem.

But here's the Raiders angle. We play them again. This division just got a lot more winnable, and the team everybody picked ahead of us is 0-2 with the hardest part of its schedule still coming.

THE HONEST WARNING INSIDE THE PRAISE

Now the part I really want you to sit with, because it was the smartest thing said all morning.

One analyst said: "I don't know if they are a good football team. They're playing good football right now."

Read that twice. That's the whole thing in one sentence. Two games do not make a season. We said the same thing after Miami, and we were right to. Last year this franchise won its opener and then won two games the rest of the way. The national media remembers that, and so do we.

And there's a legitimate caveat sitting inside this 2-0 start: both wins came against teams that have looked rough. Miami may finish near the bottom of the league. The Chargers are 0-2 and searching for an identity. The panel admitted the Miami game felt like a toss-up beforehand. So this start is real, but the schedule hasn't tested it yet.

That test is coming. New Orleans at Allegiant this week. Then Kansas City. Then a stretch with Buffalo, the Rams, and New England before Week Eight. The gauntlet we've been talking about since summer is right there. A 3-0 start would change the entire conversation around this team, and a loss at home would hand every skeptic their argument back.

That's why I like where this locker room is. They're getting praised on national television for the first time in years, and the task in front of them is the hardest stretch of their season. This is exactly the week where a team finds out what it actually is.
