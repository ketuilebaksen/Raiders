THE BUY: MARVIN HARRISON JR.

Let's start with the name that will fill the comments.

The Raiders have proven their offense can score without Brock Bowers. Twenty-six or more points in both games. But the question we've asked for weeks still doesn't have an answer: who is the true number one receiver on this roster? Several players have contributed. Nobody has stamped the position as his own.

That's the hole that put Marvin Harrison Jr. in this conversation.

Harrison was the fourth overall pick in the 2024 draft out of Ohio State, and he arrived in Arizona with about as much hype as any receiver prospect in recent memory. The NFL hasn't gone the way anybody expected. Through two games this season, he has one reception for 33 yards, and that has fueled real questions about his future there.

According to a Bleacher Report piece by Moe Moton, if Arizona ever considered moving him, Las Vegas would be one of the most logical destinations. The reasoning is straightforward. The Raiders need a potential number one receiver. Harrison is only 24 years old. And this franchise has no major long-term investment at the position: the largest contract in the entire receiver room belongs to Jalen Nailor, who signed a three-year, $35 million deal this offseason.

Now the price. Reports suggest Arizona might struggle to get a first or even a second-round pick for him at this point, and that a third-rounder could be enough to get a deal done.

Let that sink in. A fourth overall pick, 24 years old, for a third-round pick.

And there's one more layer that makes this interesting for us specifically. Klint Kubiak has a track record of developing talented young receivers. In Seattle, he was part of the staff that helped Jaxon Smith-Njigba take a major leap. That's the exact type of project Harrison would be: a gifted young player who needs the right system and the right coaching to unlock what everybody saw in college.

Now picture it. Harrison on the outside, Tre Tucker stretching the field, Jack Bech in the slot, Michael Mayer and a healthy Brock Bowers in two-tight-end sets, and eventually Fernando Mendoza taking over at quarterback with all of that already in place. That's not a patch for this season. That's a foundation.

THE HONEST RISK

But I'm not going to sell you this without the warning label, because it's a real one.

If you trade for Harrison, you're betting that the elite prospect everybody expected to dominate this league is still in there. That's a bet on potential, not production. His numbers right now are not the numbers of a number one receiver. And there's a reason the price has fallen from "untouchable" to "third-round pick." The league has watched two seasons of this.

Sometimes a change of scenery fixes everything. Sometimes it doesn't, and you find out you bought the hype rather than the player.

So compare the two receiver swings we've now discussed on this channel. Yesterday we broke down Jerry Jeudy: a proven Pro Bowl producer having a bad stretch, reportedly available for a conditional fourth-round pick. Today it's Harrison: unproven production, enormous ceiling, reportedly available for a third.

Jeudy is the safer bet on a floor. Harrison is the bet on a ceiling. If you asked me to pick one, I'd take the player whose best football might still be ahead of him, as long as the price stays at a third and not higher. At 24 years old, you're not renting a piece for one playoff push. You're trying to solve the position for the next five years.

But I understand anybody who says this roster needs help now, not a project. Both answers are defensible, and if Spytek somehow gets either of them cheap, this offense looks very different in December.

WHY THE RECEIVER CONTRACT SITUATION MATTERS

Before we move on, I want to sit on one detail from that Bleacher Report piece, because I think it's the most revealing thing in it.

The biggest contract in this entire receiver room is Jalen Nailor's three-year, $35 million deal. That's it. That's the franchise's largest commitment at the position.

Now think about what that tells you. Every other team in this league that wins consistently has either a highly paid receiver or a young one on a rookie deal who's clearly on his way to being highly paid. Las Vegas has neither. Tucker is emerging, but he wasn't a premium investment. Bech is developing. Cody White was on the practice squad ten days ago.

That's not automatically a criticism, by the way. Spending big at receiver has burned plenty of teams. But it does explain why this front office keeps showing up in every receiver trade scenario the national media writes. When an outside analyst looks at our roster, he sees a defense with real investment, a quarterback room with a plan, an offensive line that just got a $81 million center, and then a receiver group with no anchor. That gap is why Legette, Jefferson, Jeudy, and now Harrison have all been connected to us within about three weeks.

Somebody is going to have to become the answer at that position. Either Spytek buys one, or Tucker keeps playing the way he did at SoFi and becomes one himself.

THE SELL: AIDAN O'CONNELL JUST BECAME VALUABLE

Now the other side of the ledger, which nobody was talking about two weeks ago.

The quarterback landscape across this league has changed fast. Sam Darnold, Caleb Williams, Jayden Daniels, and Jaxson Dart are all dealing with injuries, and some are expected to miss multiple games. When that many starters go down at once, experienced quarterbacks stop being roster depth and start being a commodity.

And the Raiders happen to have one. Aidan O'Connell isn't going to start here this season. Kirk Cousins is playing at a level nobody predicted, and Mendoza is the future. But O'Connell has 20 touchdown passes and 11 interceptions in 17 career starts. He's still young, he saw limited action last season, and another team could reasonably believe there's development left in him.

Look at the fits. Chicago could need help with Caleb Williams hurt and their backup entering the concussion protocol. The Giants might need help after Jaxson Dart's injury, especially if the veteran behind him is struggling. Those are teams with real seasons to save.

What could Las Vegas actually get? A fourth-round pick is probably the realistic ceiling. And here's why that might still appeal to Spytek: O'Connell is entering the final year of his contract, and Mendoza is the long-term answer. If you're not going to play him and you're not going to pay him, a fourth-round pick is real value for a player who might otherwise walk for nothing.

There's a case for keeping him too. He's the experienced backup behind a 38-year-old quarterback, and if Cousins ever gets hurt, you do not want your only fallback to be a rookie thrown into the fire in the middle of a playoff race. That insurance has genuine value on a 2-0 team. He could also be Mendoza's backup next season if this staff believes he's still improving.

So this becomes a judgment call: immediate compensation, or insurance for a season that suddenly matters. My take? On a team that's winning, I keep the insurance unless somebody offers more than a fourth. The moment Cousins misses a game, that pick you traded for looks a lot smaller than the quarterback you gave away.

AND WHAT ABOUT COUSINS HIMSELF?

There's a version of this story that goes one step further, and I want to address it because it'll come up.

Cousins has this team at 2-0 with six touchdown passes. There is no argument for taking the job away from him right now, and I don't want to hear one. But the schedule gets harder from here. If Las Vegas starts losing, the organization would eventually have to think about giving Mendoza his first NFL opportunity. And if that happened before the deadline, Cousins himself could become a player other teams monitor. His age would limit the trade value, but his experience wouldn't.

I want to be clear that this is a hypothetical, not a rumor. Nobody is reporting anything like it. I'm laying it out because it shows you how quickly a season can change direction in this league, and because it explains why winning on Sunday matters for more reasons than the standings.

THE INJURY: TRAYDEN STOKES IS WAITING ON DOCTORS

Now the news that actually affects Sunday.

Trayden Stokes, the second-round rookie safety who picked off a pass in his debut, suffered a concussion in the Chargers game and entered the NFL's concussion protocol. He did not practice on Wednesday, and that puts his availability against New Orleans in real doubt.

Kubiak was asked about it and kept it simple: the player is doing everything that's been asked of him, and in the protocol that's all you can do. Listen to the doctors. He'll be back when he can.

I want to say clearly that this is the right approach, and I don't want to hear a single complaint about it. Head injuries are not a next-man-up situation or a toughness question. There is no game worth rushing. This is the same philosophy we praised when Kubiak refused to rush Bowers back: the player's health comes before the week.

There is good news in the secondary, though. Darien Porter, who missed the Chargers game with a foot issue, has returned to practice. So while the Raiders may be down a starting safety, the corner room gets healthier, and that's the group that just watched Hezekiah Masses pick off Justin Herbert twice. If Porter comes back and Masses keeps playing like that, this secondary has more options than it's had all season.

But there's a reason the timing is uncomfortable, and it has a name.

THE MAN WHO KNOWS THE SAINTS BETTER THAN ANYBODY

Here's the storyline I'm most fascinated by this week: Klint Kubiak spent the 2024 season as the New Orleans Saints' offensive coordinator.

He knows that building. He knows those players. He knows what they do well and what they're trying to hide. And he said so himself, describing that offense as very explosive and very well coached, with a lot of playmakers, many of whom he knows very well, before adding that the Raiders have their work cut out for them on Sunday.

That cuts both ways, and I love it. Our head coach has inside knowledge of their personnel. But their staff also knows exactly how Kubiak thinks, because they worked with him. This is going to be a chess match between people who have sat in the same meeting rooms.

And for a defense that might be missing a starting safety, the challenge is clear. Tyler Shough is leading that offense, and they've shown the ability to hit big plays. We already know the weakness on this defense: three completions of twenty-plus yards allowed in Week One. If the Raiders lose focus for one quarter against an explosive offense, that's how a 2-0 start turns into 2-1.

WHAT'S ACTUALLY AT STAKE

Let's finish with the big picture, because it's simple.

Win on Sunday and the Raiders are 3-0. That's a completely different conversation than 2-0. That's a team the front office has to take seriously as a buyer at the deadline. That's when names like Harrison and Jeudy stop being website scenarios and start being phone calls. That's when John Spytek has to decide whether this roster is worth adding to, and what he's willing to give up.

Lose, and everything cools down. The skeptics get their talking point, the buyer conversation goes quiet, and the questions about the run game and the offensive line get louder.

That's the beauty of where this team sits. Two weeks ago, this franchise's biggest storyline was whether our best defender would be traded away. Now the storylines are which star we might add and which backup we might sell. That's what winning does.
