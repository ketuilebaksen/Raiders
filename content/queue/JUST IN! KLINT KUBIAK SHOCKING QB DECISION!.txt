## HOW THIS OFFENSE WILL ATTACK THE STACKED BOX — FINALLY, AN ANSWER

Now the exchange I've been waiting five weeks for, and the reporter who asked it deserves credit.

Reporter Going back and looking at the film from last year with Seattle, people stacking the box against you is not something you're not used to, or even using cover zero — but I noticed there was a lot of slants and rubs. Would you like to see that implemented more in that situation with your Raiders
Kubiak Absolutely. Yeah. Just trying to attack what the defense gives you. When you're getting man coverage, that's always an option. Yes.

Absolutely.

Raider Nation, this is the whole puzzle clicking into place. Let me explain why this matters so much.

We've established that the Raiders have faced the second-most stacked boxes in football. We've established that a stacked box means the defense has more bodies in the box than the offense has blockers — so somebody comes free no matter how well your line plays. And we've established that defenses only do this when they're willing to play man coverage behind it, because that's what frees up the extra body.

And the counter to man coverage is not a deep bomb. It's a rub.

A rub route — sometimes called a mesh or a pick concept — has two receivers cross paths at a precise depth so that the defender in man coverage physically runs into traffic. It is legal, it's in every playbook in football, and against cover zero it produces an instantly open receiver about three yards past the line of scrimmage. Slants work for the same reason a quick, flat-angle route into the space a man-coverage defender can't recover to.

So the defense's own aggression becomes the weapon you use against it. You don't need a 6-foot-5 receiver to run a rub. You don't need to win a jump ball. You need a design, and the head coach just confirmed on the record that it's coming.

That is the single most encouraging football answer of this entire week, and almost nobody is going to report it.

Then Cousins came up and was asked about the same subject — and gave what I think is the most educational answer of the season.

Reporter Going back even to Holland Christian High School, you have always been gifted at the pre-snap read. When teams are stacking the box or throwing in some cover zero, how much more critical is the pre-snap read for an NFL quarterback nowadays
Cousins Yeah. No, pre-snap's very important. I would say post-snap probably becomes more important. And then there's teams who want to hide their zeros. There's teams that are going to show it. Raider Nation, sit with that for a second, because it's a window into what this job actually is.

There are three different defenses described in that answer. The team that hides its zero blitz until the snap. The team that shows it openly to intimidate you into a check. And the team that shows it and then bails out — meaning they line up like they're sending everybody, you change your play to beat it, and then they drop into coverage and your new play is wrong.

That third one is the trap, and it's why he says post-snap matters more. You can make the perfect pre-snap decision and still be wrong a half-second later, which means the real skill isn't reading the picture — it's being willing to abandon your read the instant the picture lies to you.

This is the exact thing Mike Vrabel praised him for two weeks ago when he called Cousins particularly effective in situational football. And it's why a 38-year-old with fourteen years of starts is worth what this team is paying him, even behind a line that allowed pressure on 44.8 percent of his dropbacks.
