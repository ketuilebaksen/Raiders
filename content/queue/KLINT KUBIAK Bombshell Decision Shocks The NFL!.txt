## THE INVESTMENT: $48 MILLION FOR A PASS RUSH

Start with the contract, because it frames everything.

**Las Vegas signed Kwity Paye to a three-year, $48 million contract** with one clear expectation: strengthen the pass rush and become a dependable partner for Maxx Crosby.

And for one week, that plan looked brilliant. **In the season-opening victory over Miami he recorded two sacks and five pressures** — immediately suggesting the Raiders had finally found someone capable of punishing offenses for devoting so much attention to Crosby.

Raider Nation, I remember how that felt. A two-sack debut from the big free agent signing was the single most encouraging individual performance of Week One, and I said on this channel that it was the proof of concept for the entire defensive plan.

Since then, the production has declined dramatically.

## THE NUMBERS SINCE THEN

ESPN reporter **Ryan McFadden** laid it out, and these are the figures that force the conversation.

**Over the past four games, Paye has played 154 defensive snaps and produced six tackles, four run stops, three quarterback pressures — and no sacks.**

One hundred and fifty-four snaps. Three pressures. Zero sacks.

And those numbers are especially damaging because the defense has gotten worse as the competition has gotten better. **Las Vegas is now surrendering 377 yards per game — the sixth-worst mark in the entire National Football League** — despite investing heavily in this unit in free agency.

Sixth worst, Raider Nation. On a defense that was being called Super Bowl caliber four weeks ago.

And here's the detail that shows this isn't about the staff giving up on him: **he received a season-high 49 snaps against New England.** Nearly fifty opportunities in one afternoon.

**He finished with one combined tackle.**

## THE COMPARISON THAT MAKES IT IMPOSSIBLE TO IGNORE

Now the part that turns a concern into a decision.

Over that exact same four-game stretch, **Malcolm Koonce played 110 defensive snaps and recorded eight tackles, eight run stops, 12 pressures and three sacks.**

Let me put those side by side so there's no confusion.

**Paye: 154 snaps. 6 tackles. 4 run stops. 3 pressures. 0 sacks.**
**Koonce: 110 snaps. 8 tackles. 8 run stops. 12 pressures. 3 sacks.**

**Forty-four fewer snaps. Four times the pressures. Three more sacks. Twice the run stops.**

And McFadden reported the season totals too: **Koonce's 17 total pressures rank second on this team, and his three sacks lead the Raiders.**

Raider Nation, the man leading this football team in sacks is playing fewer snaps than the man who hasn't recorded one in four games. That is the whole story in a single sentence, and it has created a legitimate argument for expanding Koonce's role immediately.

## WHY THIS MATTERS MORE THAN ANY OTHER POSITION

And here's the context that makes this the most consequential snap-count question on the roster — the Crosby data.

**Maxx Crosby leads Las Vegas with 25 pressures. Opponents have chipped him 36 times, the fifth-highest total in the league. And he's been double-teamed on 10.1 percent of his pass-rushing snaps.**

Thirty-six chips. Fifth in football.

Raider Nation, I have built an entire month of analysis on this one idea, so let me state it as plainly as I can. **The theory of this defense is that the respect Crosby commands creates a one-on-one somewhere else.** When a tight end chips him, when a tackle gets help, when a back stays in to block him — somebody on the other side of that formation is unaccounted for.

That theory only works if the player on the other side cashes it in.

Mike Vrabel proved what happens when nobody does. New England protected with every available body, Crosby finished with one tackle and zero sacks, and the Raiders recorded a single sack against Drake Maye. **The Raiders cannot allow every offensive line in football to devote extra resources to Crosby without suffering consequences elsewhere** — and right now the consequence is that the free runner isn't getting home.

Koonce is getting home. Twelve pressures and three sacks in four games says he's the one punishing those chips.

## THE FAIR DEFENSE OF KWITY PAYE

Now I'm going to defend him, because that's what honest analysis requires.

**He is not responsible for every defensive problem.** The secondary gave up balls over its head. The linebackers lost the middle of the field. The run defense has been poor since September. One edge rusher does not create a 377-yard-per-game defense.

**Five games in a new system are not enough to declare a signing a failure.** New techniques, new calls, new rush partners, new stunts — veterans genuinely do take time, and we've watched this exact process with other players on this roster.

**And the structure of his contract means the Raiders have every reason to keep searching for solutions with him.** You don't walk away from $48 million in October. You coach it, you move him around, you try him inside on third down, you find the alignment where he wins.

The 49 snaps against New England prove the staff hasn't abandoned him. They're still investing.

**But a player carrying that contract has to influence games more consistently than this.** That's not a criticism of the man; it's the job description.

## MY VERDICT, AND IT'S SIMPLE

Here's where I land, and I'm going to say it cleanly.

**If Koonce keeps creating pressure while the more expensive defender stays invisible, playing time should start reflecting production rather than salary.**

That's it. That's the whole principle. The contract is a sunk cost — it gets paid either way. The snaps are the only resource that's actually still in play, and they belong to whoever is winning with them.

And Buffalo gives Paye the chance to change this entire conversation. Josh Allen holds the football, extends plays and invites pass rushers to finish. **If Paye can't produce in a game where the quarterback creates extra rush opportunities by design, while Crosby absorbs constant extra attention, then the Raiders may have to admit the better option is already waiting behind him.**

That's the bombshell. Not an announcement. A decision becoming unavoidable.

## THE THREE OFFICIALLY RULED OUT

Now the injury report, and it's arrived at the worst possible time against one of the league's strongest opponents.

**Las Vegas activated the practice windows for tight end Carter Renan and wide receiver Dante Thornton Jr.**, allowing both to resume portions of team activity. Neither is ready for the active roster, and **both have been ruled out for Week Six.**

Those decisions aren't surprising — Kubiak had already told us both men needed additional time, that Thornton was "getting closer," and that he had "a lot to prove to himself and to us." Consistency, at least.

**The more damaging absence is defensive tackle Tonka Hemingway**, who missed practice all week and is now officially out.

## WHY HEMINGWAY'S ABSENCE IS THE REAL PROBLEM

Raider Nation, this is the one I'd circle.

Hemingway hasn't generated spectacular individual statistics. But **he started each of the Raiders' first five games**, and he occupies a position where this roster lacks proven depth.

His absence creates immediate pressure on **Jonah Laulu and JJ Pegues** to handle bigger responsibilities inside.

That would be challenging in any week. It becomes genuinely serious because **Las Vegas already owns one of the league's least reliable run defenses** — and here come the Buffalo Bills, with James Cook at 5.6 yards a carry and a quarterback who ran for 121 yards on Monday night.

**The Bills punish poor gap discipline, weak edges and missed tackles.** All three of those are documented Raiders weaknesses. So replacing a starting defensive tackle cannot be a simple matter of inserting another body into the lineup — the replacement has to actually hold his ground against a team built to find exactly this kind of soft spot.

## THE PLAYERS KNOW IT — AND SAID SO

And I respect how directly the veterans addressed it.

**Adam Butler said the Raiders must maintain a firm defensive edge and avoid being moved away from the line of scrimmage.** In his view, **run defense begins with mentality** — meaning this unit has to bring the necessary physical commitment every single week instead of expecting scheme alone to solve the problem.

Raider Nation, that's the best-graded defensive tackle on this roster saying the fix is attitude before it's a whiteboard. And remember Kubiak's own words about Butler: your true character shows up when you're down multiple scores late in a game, and Butler kept making plays.

**Cornerback Taron Johnson emphasized details and discipline, explaining that defenders cannot continue leaving their assigned gaps and creating openings for the offense.**

Those two quotes together identify the real danger, and I want to be precise about it: **Hemingway's absence reduces the margin for error, but injuries do not excuse repeated breakdowns in positioning and technique.** A man being out doesn't make another man vacate his gap. Those are two separate problems and only one of them is bad luck.

## AND THE TAKEAWAYS CAN'T SAVE THEM ANYMORE

Las Vegas also cannot depend on takeaways to conceal those weaknesses.

Turnovers helped this defense control the first three games. That advantage **disappeared during the consecutive losses to Kansas City and New England.**

And Kubiak has already explained why: **opponents have recognized how frequently the Raiders attacked the football and are now placing greater emphasis on protecting it.** Our own reputation changed how teams play us.

Which means Las Vegas has to keep pursuing takeaways **while simultaneously building a defense capable of surviving when they don't arrive.**

Safety Jeremy Chinn put the balance perfectly — he acknowledged that every performance contains areas requiring improvement whether you win or lose, but also explained that taking the football away dramatically increases your chance of winning.

**He's right on both counts. Turnovers are valuable. They have to complement sound defense, not replace it.** That's the entire lesson of the last two Sundays.

## NOW THE OFFENSE: THE BLUEPRINT IS PUBLIC

Let's switch sides, because the offensive crisis is no longer about one bad afternoon. **Opposing defenses appear to have identified a repeatable formula for stopping this team.**

Early in the season the Raiders ranked near the top of the league in scoring and looked capable of adjusting whenever an opponent took away their first option. Injuries and declining execution have changed that completely.

**Jack Bech remains unavailable. Brock Bowers only recently returned after missing the opening weeks. And Ashton Jeanty is trying to contribute while clearly being far from full strength.**

Those limitations have exposed a receiver group that entered this season with legitimate questions in the first place.

## HERE IS THE EXACT FORMULA

New England was the worst version of it, but the same weaknesses appeared against Kansas City.

Las Vegas could not establish the running game, which allowed the Patriots to pressure Kirk Cousins **without fearing consistent punishment on the ground.** Once New England controlled the line of scrimmage, the drives became increasingly predictable.

**So the blueprint is three steps: stop the run, pressure Cousins, and force Las Vegas to prove its quarterback and depleted receiving group can consistently win under difficult conditions.**

Raider Nation, write that down, because until this team demonstrates otherwise, **Buffalo and every future opponent will attack them exactly the same way.** Nobody has to be clever anymore. The recipe is on tape.

And Kubiak has acknowledged this openly — weaknesses inevitably appear on film, and opponents will target both what you do poorly *and* what you do well. Which means this staff has a double assignment: **improve the vulnerable areas while preparing counters for defenses determined to erase the strengths.** That's the Bowers problem and the run-game problem in one sentence.

## WHAT THE OFFENSIVE COORDINATOR SAID — AND THE KEY INSIGHT

And this responsibility extends beyond the head coach.

Offensive coordinator **Andrew Janocko** said the coaches must place players in better situations, understand the circumstances surrounding each play, and create answers for unexpected defensive looks. **And he emphasized allowing the players to operate quickly instead of hesitating while trying to diagnose unfamiliar pressure or coverage.**

Raider Nation, that last part is the most important football sentence in this entire video, so let me explain why.

**Hesitation is the real killer.** Not confusion — hesitation. When an offense sees a look it hasn't prepared for, there are two failure modes. One is guessing wrong. The other, which is far more common and far more damaging, is **thinking too long.** A receiver who hesitates for a quarter-second on his break is covered. A lineman who hesitates on a protection call is beaten. A quarterback who hesitates eats a sack.

And remember what Kubiak told us about Bowers: **the coverages you see on Sunday are typically not what you expected going in.** So this offense has been facing unfamiliar pictures for two straight weeks — which is exactly the condition that produces hesitation.

Janocko's answer is the correct one: **simplify the decision so the player can play fast.** That connects directly to Kubiak's "don't get too cute with your schemes — give guys a good plan they can go execute fast," and to the slants and rub concepts he promised. Those are quick-decision plays. One read, one break, go.

**That's the whole offensive fix in two words: play faster.**

## THE HONEST PART ABOUT THE REBUILD

And now the hard truth that this channel is not going to hide from you.

**Some roster problems cannot be repaired during one week of preparation.** Previous personnel and coaching decisions left holes across this offense, and the current staff entered the season knowing several of them might remain until another offseason. The receiver room is the clearest example — that gap was visible in August and the deadline may not fix it.

**But rebuilding cannot become permission to repeat the same failures.**

That's the line I'd put on the wall, Raider Nation. "We're still building" explains why the talent is uneven. It does not explain eight penalties, an abandoned run game, a back averaging five yards a carry getting one carry, or a coverage plan that goes unadjusted for four quarters. Those are process failures, and process is available to every team regardless of talent.

So the Raiders must find productive carries for a healthier option when Jeanty is limited, protect their quarterback better than 44.8 percent pressure, and give their receivers plays they can execute at full speed. None of that requires a trade. All of it requires better decisions.

## AND THE OVERLOOKED LINEMAN WHO'S EARNING IT

One last thing, because amid all of this criticism there's a player quietly becoming a cornerstone of Kubiak's plans — and the head coach has now praised him two weeks in a row while everybody else on that line is under scrutiny.

**DJ Glaze.**

Kubiak's own words: **"DJ's one of our most consistent guys. He never misses practice. He's always getting better, and the game's really important to him. He's becoming a quiet leader that can even find his voice more — because his teammates respect him, as does his coaching staff."**

Raider Nation, think about the context. The interior of this line is in open competition. Spencer Burford has allowed the most pressures of any left guard in football. Jackson Powers-Johnson has missed more than a dozen games in three seasons. Trey Zuhn is questionable again.

**And the man at right tackle never misses a practice.** In a week where the head coach promised "a new approach" built partly on evaluating who's playing and keeping bodies fresh, availability itself is a skill — and Glaze is the only lineman nobody has to think about.

That's how you become a key piece without a headline.
