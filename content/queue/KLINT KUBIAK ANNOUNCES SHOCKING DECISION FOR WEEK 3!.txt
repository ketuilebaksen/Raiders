THE RED FLAG IS GONE: COUSINS WENT DEEP

Let's start with the biggest question of the week, because we spent an entire episode on it. After the Miami game, Kirk Cousins was nearly perfect on short throws and went one for seven on passes that traveled more than ten yards in the air. We called it a red flag. We said if defenses stopped respecting the deep ball, safeties would creep down, linebackers would sit on the short routes, and the formula that beat Miami would get strangled.

Cousins answered that in one afternoon.

He finished 19 of 29 for 253 yards, three touchdowns, and one interception. But the number that matters is this one: on throws of ten or more air yards, he went eight for twelve for 181 yards and all three of his touchdowns. One for seven in Week One. Eight for twelve in Week Two. That isn't a small improvement. That's a completely different quarterback profile from one Sunday to the next.

And look at how he spread the ball around. Cousins targeted ten different players, and eight of them caught at least one pass. That's what this offense is supposed to look like without Brock Bowers. No single go-to weapon, so everybody has to be a threat, and the quarterback has to trust all of them. He did.

He also did it under real pressure. The Chargers got to him eight times and sacked him twice. And the interception? It came on a play where Tre Tucker slipped coming out of his break. That's not a decision problem. That's a footing problem, and I'm not holding it against anybody.

Six touchdown passes in two games as a Raider. For a franchise that has spent years searching for stability at the most important position on the field, that's the kind of start that lets you breathe again.

TRE TUCKER'S BREAKOUT DAY

And that brings us to the man who made the deep ball work. A week ago, Tucker finished with 27 receiving yards against Miami, and a lot of fans, some of you included, were asking whether this receiver room could ever produce without Bowers. Tucker gave his answer on Sunday: five catches, 119 yards, including a 42-yard grab and a 40-yard touchdown.

Here's why that matters beyond the box score. Tucker's speed is only valuable if the quarterback is willing to throw to it. In Week One, the deep shots weren't landing, so defenses had no reason to fear him. On Sunday, Cousins let it fly and Tucker ran under it. Now every defensive coordinator who studies this tape has to keep a safety deep to account for him. And every safety that stays deep is one fewer body in the box against Ashton Jeanty.

That's how an offense gets stretched open: not with one big play, but with the threat of the next one. Tucker created that threat in a single afternoon.

CODY WHITE: SEVEN SNAPS, TWO TOUCHDOWNS

Now here's the stat line that made me laugh out loud when I saw it. Cody White played seven offensive snaps. Seven. He caught two passes for 24 yards. And both of them were touchdowns.

That's efficiency you almost never see in this league. The second score came in the fourth quarter and pushed the lead out of reach, the moment the whole Raiders sideline could finally exhale.

Think about what that tells you about Kubiak's approach. This coaching staff isn't building an offense around one star. It's building a system where the right player gets the right route at the right time, and the quarterback trusts him to finish. White wasn't on the field a lot, but when the play was called for him, he delivered. That's the kind of depth this roster didn't have a year ago.

And it matters for game planning. When a player with seven snaps can score twice, defenses can't just key on Jeanty, Mayer, and Tucker. They have to respect whoever runs onto the field, especially in the red zone, where space shrinks and one missed assignment turns into six points.

HEZEKIAH MASSES: THE CORNER NOBODY HAD NAMED

Remember our pregame breakdown? Darien Porter was out, and the Raiders still hadn't said who would start opposite Eric Stokes. I told you my expectation: Masses gets the first opportunity, and the Raiders ride the hot hand. I also told you Justin Herbert would test whoever lined up across from Stokes.

Herbert tested him. Masses passed.

Two interceptions. Five tackles. One tackle for loss. Targeted six times in coverage, he allowed just three catches for 23 yards. For a rookie making a start on the road, in a division game, against one of the best quarterbacks in the conference, that is an unbelievable performance.

And think back to those eight coverage snaps against Miami. He came in cold, got targeted once, broke up the pass, stayed on top of routes, and looked like he belonged. We said there was a lot to like. Well, Sunday is what "a lot to like" looks like when a young player gets a full game. The hot hand stayed hot all afternoon.

Now the big question: what happens when Porter comes back? That's a good problem, and it's the first time in a long time this secondary has had a problem like that. Competition at cornerback is exactly what a young defense needs.

THE HONEST PART: WHAT STILL NEEDS FIXING

Now, this channel doesn't do blind celebration. We said after Miami that feeling great about one win is how teams repeat last year, and the same rule applies after two. So let's be honest about what didn't work, because the Saints and the Chiefs will watch this tape too.

First, the running game. Ashton Jeanty was held under 50 rushing yards. After a 100-yard opener, that's a real step back, and it wasn't all on Jeanty. The offensive line struggled in run blocking for long stretches, and it had trouble in pass protection too. Eight pressures and two sacks allowed isn't a disaster, but against a Chiefs front, that number gets much uglier if nothing changes.

Second, the drops. The receivers have three drops across the first two games. That might not sound like much, but in close games, a drop on third down is the difference between a field goal and a punt.

Third, the tight end usage. Michael Mayer, who just signed a three-year, $45 million extension, has 11 targets for only 55 yards on the season. He's been a key blocker, and his value doesn't all show up in the box score, but this offense needs more from him as a receiver while Bowers is out.

And here's the context that should make you feel a little better: the Raiders scored 26 points, 24 of them from the offense, while the run game sputtered and the line had a rough day. That's 53 points in two games. When the thing you're worst at still produces two dozen points, the ceiling is high. Fix the run blocking and cut down the drops, and this offense gets scary.

THE WEEK THREE DECISION: BOWERS IS CLOSE

So now let's get to the decision Kubiak is facing for Week Three, because it's the one that could change the entire shape of this offense.

Quick timeline. Bowers had the procedure on his knee five days before the season. He missed Week One and Week Two. On Friday, he practiced for the first time since the surgery and was listed as questionable. And before the Chargers game, he went through pregame warmups at SoFi.

After the win, Kubiak was asked about it, and he was clearly positive. He liked what he saw in the warmup, and he said Bowers is close to being ready.

So here's the decision: when to bring back your best offensive weapon, and how much to give him. And after what this team just did without him, Kubiak can make that decision from a position of strength. That's the part I want you to understand.

Two weeks ago, the fear was that without Bowers, this offense had no go-to weapon and no deep ball. Now Cousins has proven he can hit the intermediate and deep throws, Tucker has proven he can win downfield, and White and the rest of the rotation have proven they can finish. The Raiders don't need Bowers to rescue them. They can bring him back to make an already-functioning offense better.

That changes everything about the risk. If Bowers plays against the Saints, the smart move is a limited package: a set number of snaps, specific routes, and no pressure to carry the offense. If the staff decides he needs one more week, the team just proved it can win without him. Either way, there's no desperation driving the call, and after the history with that left knee, no desperation is exactly what you want.

And now picture it. A defense that just had 18 pressures and three takeaways. A quarterback who went eight for twelve deep. Tucker stretching the field, Mayer and Bech working the middle, Jeanty in the backfield. And then you add Brock Bowers to that picture. That's the offense Kubiak designed in the spring, and it's closer than it has been all season.
