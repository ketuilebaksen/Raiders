## THE STAGE: THEY THREW A PARTY ABOUT BEATING US

Let's set this up properly, because this game was dressed up like a movie premiere before a single snap.

The Raiders came into Foxborough at 3-1, the only blemish that heartbreaking 30-27 loss to Kansas City. The Patriots were 2-2 and looking to protect their home field.

And then New England decided to turn the afternoon into a full-blown celebration — because it was the **25th anniversary of their Super Bowl 36 team.**

Raider Nation, do I need to finish that sentence? That's the team that went on to beat the Rams after a certain snowy January playoff night against the Raiders that every single one of you still remembers with a twitch in your eye.

Yes. **The Tuck Rule game.**

So let me make sure you understand the full picture here. The Patriots were literally holding a ceremony honoring a team whose glory began over our broken hearts — and then they proceeded to rub salt in the wound by dropping 45 points on us.

If this were a script, you'd tell the writer it was too on the nose. But this is the NFL, and the NFL absolutely loves a villain arc.

## AND SPEAKING OF VILLAIN ARCS

We cannot skip the man standing on the Patriots sideline with a headset and a chip on his shoulder roughly the size of Mount Rushmore.

**Josh McDaniels.** The former Raiders head coach who ran the show in Las Vegas in 2023 and 2024, now the offensive coordinator in New England.

And here's the context that makes it worse: the Raiders had won their last three games against the Patriots, including that 20-13 season-opening victory a year ago.

So you *knew* McDaniels had this date circled in red marker, underlined twice, and probably stapled to his bathroom mirror.

And guess what? He cooked.

The man put together a game plan that looked like a master class, and Drake Maye played like a guy who woke up Sunday morning and decided to audition for MVP all over again. If revenge is a dish best served cold, McDaniels served it in a New England blizzard of touchdowns.

Now, I want to be fair here, because this channel doesn't do cheap shots. McDaniels' tenure in Las Vegas didn't work, and both sides moved on. But a coordinator who knows a building — who knows the tendencies, the terminology, the way a staff self-scouts — is the most dangerous opponent in football. Combine that with what Mike Vrabel told us about recognizing our zero-blitz situations, and you get a very clear picture: **New England didn't just beat us. They knew us.**

## THE DAMAGE REPORT (WARNING LABEL REQUIRED)

Okay. Let's talk numbers, and these need a warning label.

**The Patriots piled up 536 total yards of offense.**

Five hundred and thirty-six. That's not a game, Raider Nation. That's a road trip.

Drake Maye looked comfortable all day. He handled the pressure when the Raiders actually managed to send it — and when we didn't, he sliced up the coverage like a man carving a Thanksgiving turkey.

**The Patriots converted six of twelve on third down**, which doesn't sound outrageous until you realize how many of those conversions came when our defense had that deer-in-the-headlights look. Fifty percent on third down means a defense never gets off the field, and a defense that never gets off the field is a defense that gets tired, which is how the fourth quarter became a formality.

Meanwhile the Las Vegas offense limped to **306 total yards.**

A 230-yard deficit in total offense. That's the actual story of the football game, and no amount of spin changes it.

## ABOUT THE REFS — I'M GOING TO BE HONEST WITH YOU

Now, we were flagged **eight times for 101 yards.** And sure, the officiating was not exactly a love letter to the silver and black.

But here's the honest truth, and I'd rather lose your agreement than your trust: **the Patriots also had more penalties and more penalty yards than we did.**

You cannot hide behind the refs on this one, as tempting as that feels at three in the afternoon on a Sunday with your blood pressure spiking.

Eight penalties for 101 yards from a team that had been one of the most disciplined in football for a month is a self-inflicted wound. And it feeds directly into the thing Kubiak himself identified: second-and-long and third-and-long is advantage defense, every single time. You cannot manufacture that many bad down-and-distance situations against a good team and expect to survive.

## SO THE BIG QUESTION: ARE THE RAIDERS FRAUDS?

That word is flying around every group chat, every comment section, and every sports radio call-in show in America right now.

And I get why. Look at the path honestly.

The Raiders won their first three games, and the teams they beat have won **once between them all season.** Then they lost to Kansas City. Then they got their doors blown off in New England.

If you're the cynical type, you look at that and say, "Aha — the cupcake schedule got exposed."

And you know what? That's a legitimate argument. I'm not going to pretend it isn't. I've been telling you about that one-win number for two weeks precisely because I didn't want this fan base to get blindsided.

## BUT HERE'S THE MORE MEASURED TAKE

Now let me give you the other side, because this is not a team that deserves to be dragged to the guillotine after one blowout.

**Four of the five games this season showed a team with real identity, real fight, and a genuine ability to take the football away.**

And here's the stat that cuts through all the noise: **the Raiders lead the league with 10 takeaways.**

That is not something you stumble into by accident. You don't lead the entire NFL in takeaways through five weeks by luck. That requires coaching, technique, and a specific culture of attacking the football — and Rob Leonard's unit has it.

One absolutely atrocious afternoon does not erase that.

Sometimes a game is just a dumpster fire, and the smart move is to crumple it into a ball, throw it in the trash, and light the trash can on fire for good measure. Kubiak said it himself: **you cannot let one game beat you twice.**

## THE BIGGER PICTURE EVERYBODY FORGETS WHEN THEY'RE SCREAMING

And here's what gets lost when we're all yelling into our pillows.

**This team is not built solely for right now.**

The Raiders drafted Fernando Mendoza with the number one overall pick. That was a franchise-altering decision aimed squarely at the future. Klint Kubiak is building something, and the foundation of that something is not a single Sunday in Foxborough.

**3-2 is a perfectly respectable place to be after five weeks.** If somebody had offered you that record back in September — a first-year head coach, a 38-year-old quarterback, a receiver room nobody respected, a rookie running back, a rookie corner starting — most of you would have grabbed it with both hands and sprinted for the hills.

## AND LOOK AT THIS DIVISION

Now look around the AFC West, because the standings are genuinely useful medicine right now.

**The Raiders are 3-2, tied with the Denver Broncos. The Chiefs sit at 4-0 coming off a bye. And the Los Angeles Chargers are somehow still stuck at 0-5** — which makes them look like they're playing this season on hard mode.

So no, the sky is not falling. The sky just dropped a piano on our heads this week, and we're going to dust ourselves off.

And Raider Nation, file that Chargers number away, because 0-5 has a specific meaning we discussed on this channel: **no team in NFL history has ever reached the playoffs after starting 0-5.** Which means a certain nine-time Pro Bowl edge rusher who began his career in silver and black just became a lot more gettable. We'll come back to that in the next few weeks.

## BUT — AND IT'S A BIG BUT — THE HEAT HAS TO LAND SOMEWHERE

A performance like this demands accountability. So let's hand it out, fairly and specifically.

**Start with the coaching staff, because teams take on the personality of their leaders, and the Raiders looked completely lost.**

Klint Kubiak is an offensive mind — that's his entire identity as a coach. And offensively this team never found a rhythm that made any sense. He abandoned the run by the second quarter after telling us all week he didn't want a pass-dependent offense. He gave his most efficient back one carry. He called a reverse that lost eleven yards at 14-7. And he's admitted most of it himself, which I respect — but admissions don't score points.

## THE DEFENSE IS THE PART THAT SCARES ME

And now the one that actually hurts.

**Rob Leonard's defense — which was being hyped as a legitimate Super Bowl caliber unit based on some early numbers — got picked apart for 536 yards.**

That's the part I keep coming back to, because it's the thing I think this fan base was least prepared for.

And here's the uncomfortable truth: **the last two weeks should have been a flashing red warning light.** This defense gave up 30 points to the Chiefs. It gave up 27 to the Saints before that. Those weren't dominant performances with ugly final scores — those were points on the board.

**Those eye-popping takeaway numbers may have been covering up some real weaknesses.**

Raider Nation, when you force turnovers, you can look like a juggernaut even when your actual defensive fundamentals are leaking oil. A takeaway doesn't just stop a drive — it erases everything that happened before it. The 60-yard completion before the interception disappears from how we remember the game. The third-down conversions vanish. All anybody sees is the highlight of the strip and the celebration.

So you end up with a defense that leads the league in takeaways and *also* allows 27, 30 and 45 points in three consecutive weeks. Both of those things are real at the same time. The takeaways are a genuine skill. They were also a mask.

**And in New England, the mask came off, because they didn't turn it over.** No takeaways means no erasing. You just have to actually stop people, and for 536 yards, this defense couldn't.

## WHAT THAT ACTUALLY MEANS GOING FORWARD

Let me be precise about the diagnosis, because "the defense is bad" is lazy and wrong.

This is a young unit with a thin third level that generates chaos and lives off it. When the chaos arrives, they're genuinely dangerous — nine takeaways in three games, a historic fourth-quarter stretch, the no-name defense that flipped a game in the Superdome. When the chaos doesn't arrive, there isn't enough technique and depth underneath it yet to hold up for sixty minutes against a quarterback with a clean pocket.

That's not a character flaw. That's a roster at an early stage of construction, playing above its station for three weeks and then meeting a Super Bowl caliber opponent.

The fix list is the same one I gave you after the film: help the young corners, don't leave them on an island, find a way to free Maxx Crosby, and change the pre-snap picture so your pressure isn't predictable. All correctable. None of them optional.

## THE RECEIVER THAT GOT AWAY

One more thing, because it's the cruelest detail of this entire week.

That same Sunday, the trade rumor about a former top-five wide receiver landed right in our lap — a 24-year-old, high-upside target who could have genuinely changed the structure of this passing game.

And by the time the dust settled, **he was a Houston Texan.**

Marvin Harrison Jr. went to Houston. Arizona wanted at least a second-round pick plus additional compensation, Houston was willing, and we weren't. I told you a week ago that two bidders means the price never falls, and I told you I wouldn't pay it.

I stand by that. I also won't insult you by pretending it doesn't sting. On the exact day our offense managed 306 yards with three healthy receivers, the best available solution signed up to play somewhere else. Discipline has a price, and Sunday was the invoice.

The board now reads Jerry Jeudy at a possible fourth-rounder, Calvin Ridley at a possible sixth or seventh, and Brian Thomas Jr. at a price Jacksonville isn't even interested in discussing. The deadline is coming, and this offense cannot keep asking Brock Bowers to be an elite slot receiver *and* the reason defenses stop crowding the line.

## THE RIVALRY NOBODY OUTSIDE LAS VEGAS UNDERSTANDS

Let me take one more minute on that anniversary ceremony, because I think it matters more than a stat line.

For most of this league, Raiders-Patriots is just another interconference game on a schedule. For us it's a wound with a date on it. That snowy night in January 2002 is the moment where one franchise's dynasty started and another franchise's decade ended, and the call at the center of it is still argued about in every bar in this fan base twenty-five years later.

So standing on their field while they honored it, and then surrendering 45 points, is the kind of afternoon that lives in a fan base's memory longer than it deserves to. I understand why some of you were furious in a way that a normal loss doesn't produce.

But here's the thing I want to leave you with about that. The Raiders had beaten New England three straight times coming into Sunday, including a 20-13 win in last year's opener. We've had our turns. Rivalries aren't one-way streets, and the only revenge available in this sport is the kind you take on a scoreboard in the future.

Write the date down. We'll get another one.

## FIVE NUMBERS THAT EXPLAIN 45-17

If you want the whole game in thirty seconds, here it is.

**536 to 306.** A 230-yard gap in total offense. That's not a bad break — that's one team controlling a football game from start to finish.

**Six of twelve on third down.** Fifty percent conversion means a defense never rests, and a defense that never rests gives up a fourth quarter.

**Eight penalties for 101 yards.** From a team that had been among the league's most disciplined. And with the Patriots taking more flags than we did, the refs are not an available excuse.

**Thirty-eight rushing yards at 2.1 a carry.** The identity this staff said it wanted, erased — partly by the opponent, partly by a play-caller who admitted he walked away from it too early.

**Ten league-leading takeaways — and zero on Sunday.** That's the tell. This defense is built on chaos, and on the one afternoon the chaos didn't come, nothing underneath it held.

## WHAT I NEED TO SEE AGAINST BUFFALO

So here's my checklist for Sunday at Allegiant, and it's short on purpose.

**Fifteen or more rushing attempts before the fourth quarter, regardless of score.** Not yards. Attempts. I want evidence that the stated identity survives adversity.

**A different interior offensive line combination**, or a clear explanation of why the worst left guard in football by pressures allowed kept his job.

**Safety help over the top on early downs.** If a young corner gets attacked twice in the first quarter and nothing changes, that's a pattern now instead of a bad day.

**Two penalties or fewer in the first half.** Discipline is free and it's the fastest thing on this list to fix.

**And one explosive play from a wide receiver.** Just one. A twenty-yard completion to someone who isn't a tight end would tell us this offense has a second dimension.

Hit four of those five and I don't care much about the final score — this team is fine. Miss four of five and the conversation changes from "bad week" to "that's who they are."

## WHERE I LAND

So — are the Raiders frauds?

**No. But they're not contenders yet either, and both of those statements have to live in the same sentence.**

Here's my honest verdict. This is a 3-2 football team that is better than it looked Sunday and not as good as it looked in September. It leads the league in takeaways and has a defense whose fundamentals need work. It has a top-10 pass protection unit by success rate and the worst left guard in football by pressures allowed. It has a first-year head coach who keeps taking blame like a veteran and keeps making mistakes like a rookie. All of it is true at once, and anybody selling you a simpler story is selling you something.

The piano landed on our heads. That's all that happened. Now we find out whether this team is the kind that gets up.

Buffalo comes to Allegiant Stadium on Sunday. Both teams could walk in on two-game losing streaks. Home crowd, short week for the Bills, and a franchise that needs to prove that the first three weeks weren't a mirage.
