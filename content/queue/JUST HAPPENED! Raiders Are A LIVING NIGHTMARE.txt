## THE BOX SCORE DOESN'T LOOK LIKE A LOSS

Start with the numbers, because 30-27 doesn't tell you what actually happened in that stadium.

Kirk Cousins threw the football 52 times. He completed 31 of them for 365 yards and two touchdowns. Against a Kansas City defense with Steve Spagnuolo pulling the strings, disguising pressures and rotating coverages the way he has for two decades.

Three hundred and sixty-five yards. Fifty-two attempts. A 38-year-old quarterback who arrived in April as a bridge, standing in against the best defensive mind in the conference and slinging it.

Was it perfect? No. He threw an interception on the final play of the game, which stings, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But understand the context: by that point the Raiders were throwing from behind, in a hole they never needed to dig, on a play that existed only because the clock and the scoreboard forced it. That's not a quarterback losing a game. That's a quarterback trying to steal one back.

On the other side, Kenneth Walker put together a nice afternoon, and a massive chunk of it came on one explosive 77-yard touchdown run. Seventy-seven yards. One play.

Think about what that means. In a three-point game, Kansas City's rushing production was substantially one snap. Take away that single run and we're having an entirely different conversation about how this defense handled the league's leading rusher. That run should have been one of the headlines of this game. Instead it's a footnote, because of what happened in the third phase.

## MISTAKE ONE AND TWO: THE RETURNS

Here's the thread that actually decided this game, and it goes back further than Sunday.

Remember Week Three in New Orleans? That win included a kickoff return fumble from Dylan Laube. The Raiders survived it because the defense forced four takeaways in the fourth quarter and bailed everybody out. We mentioned it in our winners and losers breakdown and flagged it as the kind of error that gets forgiven against a bad team and punished against a good one.

Maybe surviving it created a false sense of security. Maybe it didn't. But you would think that heading into a measuring-stick game against Kansas City, a veteran coaching staff would have had alarm bells ringing about ball security on returns.

It didn't get fixed. In the fourth quarter of this game, the Raiders fumbled another kickoff return. The ball was credited to Dariq Young, though there's some uncertainty floating around about exactly how that's being reported and confirmed.

What is not uncertain is the impact. That fumble handed Kansas City the football, and with it handed them the lead right back.

Sit with that in a three-point game. You are not losing because Patrick Mahomes carved you up for 400 yards. You are not losing because your defensive front got manhandled. You are losing because your own kicking game coughed up the football, not once across two weeks, but twice, and the second one came in the fourth quarter of a game that absolutely mattered.

And I want to be careful here, because I'm not interested in making a young player the villain of this channel. Ball security on returns is about technique, focus, and the drills you do on Wednesday and Thursday. It is one of the most coachable things in football. That's exactly why it belongs in the coaching conversation and not on one man's shoulders.

## MISTAKE THREE: THE FOURTH-AND-TWO

Now the decision that's going to be discussed in this fan base for a long time, and not fondly.

Klint Kubiak had a choice on fourth-and-two. Matt Gay had been warming up all day and hitting his attempts with the kind of consistency that makes a head coach's job easy, and he'd had another strong game. The leg was absolutely available. There were points on the table.

Kubiak went for it. The Raiders didn't convert. Turnover on downs.

In a game decided by three points, I don't need to draw you a diagram. You give the ball back to Kansas City with better field position than they would have had, you take points off the board that Gay very likely delivers, and you ask a defense that had already been asked to do plenty to go bail you out again.

So here's my honest opinion, and I'm not going to pretend both sides of this are equally strong: it was the wrong call.

When your kicker is as hot as Gay has been, when the game is this close, and when the opponent is this dangerous, you take the three. A bird in the hand wins you that football game. Aggressive coaching is great and I generally love it, but aggression without situational awareness isn't bold, it's reckless, and that one felt reckless.

Now let me give you the full picture, because I'm not here to burn a head coach I believe in.

Kubiak was asked about this at the podium and his answer was: "I don't regret it. I regret not getting it." They came into the game planning to be aggressive and he stuck to it. And there's a real argument that against a team with Mahomes at quarterback, trading touchdowns for field goals is how you lose by four, and that playing it safe against that franchise has a long and humiliating track record.

Cousins' version was the professional one: he leaves those decisions to the staff, it's always a would-have, could-have, should-have, and it's the players' job to execute and make the coach right. He even said he probably could have squeezed the ball into Cody White on that fourth down, that he got to him a little late and White had gotten covered up.

So you can respect the philosophy and still believe the specific call was wrong. That's where I am.

## MISTAKE FOUR: THE ONSIDE KICK WITH TWO TIMEOUTS IN YOUR POCKET

And the kicking game wasn't done.

Late in the game the Raiders attempted an onside kick. Here's the part that should bother you if you were paying close attention: at the time of that kick, Las Vegas still had two timeouts remaining and 1:46 on the clock.

Two timeouts. Over a minute and a half.

That's not nothing. That's real time and two real stoppages, which is exactly the toolkit you need to get the ball back conventionally: force a stop on defense, use your timeouts to preserve clock, get the offense back on the field with something to work with.

Instead the staff gambled on an onside kick, which fails far more often than it succeeds unless there's a surprise element, and recovering one against a team expecting it in that spot is an uphill climb. And when it fails, you've essentially handed the opposing offense a short field and a chance to close out the game without ever facing real pressure.

That's three separate moments in the kicking game that worked against this team in a three-point loss. Two fumbled returns across two weeks, and an onside kick call that ignored the clock situation. Add the fourth-down decision and you've got four correctable moments in a game lost by one field goal.

Do the math on how differently this game goes if even one of them breaks the other way.

I'll be fair to Joe DeCamillis, because piling on is easy. He has been in this league a long time, he's had excellent units before, and one rough stretch over two weeks does not erase a career. But it absolutely deserves scrutiny when the stakes are this high, and this fan base is right to ask for it.

## THE HALFTIME QUOTE THAT'S GOING TO FOLLOW HIM

There's one more layer to the Kubiak conversation that I have to address, because it's circulating.

At halftime, Kubiak told the people around him that the Raiders were going to win this game. He was asked about it afterward and his answer was simple and, frankly, charming: that's how he felt, so he said what he felt.

Confidence is good. I want my head coach believing in his team. And when a reporter followed up by noting that he doesn't coach this team like he's convincing them they can compete, he coaches them as though they already know they're going to win, Kubiak said that's why you play the game, that's why you suit up, and that's the expectation every week.

I love that posture. It's the thing that turned a 3-14 roster into a team that fights for sixty minutes against Kansas City.

But here's the honest warning: when you say that out loud and then your team loses on decisions made in the fourth quarter, the quote doesn't disappear. It follows you. Raiders fans are going to remember it, and if this season goes sideways it becomes a clip that gets replayed. I'm not saying that's fair, because confidence isn't a crime. I'm saying perception matters in this league, and right now the perception outside this building is that the head coach talked big and then made the call that cost it.

The only cure for that is winning in New England.

## THE THREE MEN WHO PLAYED LIKE STARS

Let's talk about who actually showed up, because in a loss this close, three Raiders were genuinely outstanding and all three deserve their names said.

**Brock Bowers.** Still a matchup problem for any defense alive, regardless of the final score. The tight end room produced well over 100 yards and two of this team's touchdowns, and Kubiak named the trio at the podium: Brock, Mike, Ian, and he made sure to credit tight ends coach Luke Steckel by name. In a game where the receiver room was thin and got thinner, the two-tight-end identity carried this offense.

**Kirk Cousins.** Over 350 yards and multiple touchdowns against a defense that has historically given quarterbacks fits. He also took a beating, which he deflected afterward by crediting the wellness team and saying movement is medicine. That's your 38-year-old quarterback absorbing hits behind an interior line missing its starting guard, and still throwing for 365.

**Matt Gay.** Money all afternoon on the attempts he got, and the cruelest detail of this whole game is that he never got the chance to potentially win it, because of the fourth-down decision. Cousins went out of his way to praise him, saying the team has put him in tough spots with the length of his kicks and he keeps responding.

Three men doing their jobs at a high level in a losing effort. That tells you exactly where the problem lived on Sunday, and it wasn't with the stars.

## THE PROBLEM THAT ISN'T GOING AWAY

Now the thing bothering everybody who watches this offense closely: Spencer Burford has struggled at guard this season, and it showed up again in the run blocking.

When the opponent has a back capable of a 77-yard touchdown run, you want your own interior line creating lanes consistently rather than waiting for athleticism to bail out the scheme. Burford being a liability up front is not a new storyline on this channel, and it's only going to get louder if it isn't addressed, whether through coaching, scheme adjustment, or eventually a roster move.

Kubiak's own answer on the run game was the most quotable thing he said: they have to go to the drawing board, they have really good backs, they have a good offensive line, and "there's more meat on that bone."

Cousins gave the technical version: it's about getting the combo blocks right, about the backs continuing to learn, and about continuity that builds with time on task together. In plain English, two blockers working in tandem before one climbs to the linebacker is a timing exercise, and timing comes from the same five men practicing together for weeks.

Which is exactly why Jackson Powers-Johnson being out with a groin injury mattered so much, why Caleb Rogers' first start at right guard was such a difficult assignment, and why the Cesar Ruiz addition is the most important thing on this roster right now even though he can't fix it by Sunday.

## ONE THING I'M NOT GOING TO DO

There's chatter circulating that questions whether Ashton Jeanty's ankle situation is being fully and accurately represented, and I want to handle this the way this channel always handles unconfirmed things.

Nobody has produced any evidence that this team is hiding anything. That is speculation, not reporting, and I'm not going to accuse a medical staff of dishonesty based on vibes.

What I will say is the football part, because that's fair game. Jeanty has been getting his ankles taped during games. Kubiak said at the podium that everybody is sore and nobody is 100 percent, which is true of every roster in October. And the sixth pick of the 2025 draft is averaging a little over three yards a carry while a fourth-round rookie, Mike Washington Jr., is averaging nearly seven.

So the honest conclusion isn't a conspiracy. It's a usage question. If a young back is banged up, manage his workload: 15 or 16 carries for him, eight to 10 for Washington, and protect the asset. That's not a controversy, that's just good roster management.

## THE GOOD NEWS BURIED IN THE BAD

Here's the part that should actually give you hope, and it's the cleanest conclusion from this entire film.

This wasn't a talent gap. This was not Kansas City imposing its will from start to finish and proving its roster is simply better. This was a game the Raiders were right there in, with real chances to win, undone by mistakes that are correctable.

Fumbles on returns are about technique, focus, and ball security drills. Fourth-down decisions are about situational command and trusting your personnel. Onside kick timing is about clock-management fundamentals. None of those are talent problems. They are execution and decision-making problems, and those get fixed much faster than a deficiency at a premium position.

That's genuinely good news. If this loss had come because the offensive line got destroyed or because the secondary couldn't cover anybody, you'd be looking at a problem that takes a draft class and a free agency cycle to solve. Instead it's Wednesday-through-Friday work, which happens to be exactly what this head coach talks about every single week.

And he owned his part publicly, which matters more than most fans realize. On the end-of-game sequence, when the play didn't get in fast enough, Kubiak said he didn't get the call to the quarterback quickly enough, called it a total missed opportunity, and said four words you almost never hear: "I blew it." Then Cousins refused to let him carry it alone, pointing out that Clint always points the thumb at himself, and taking his own share for not taking a delay of game to get into the right play.

That's the standard in that building. Nobody hid.

## WHERE I STAND

So here's my position, and it hasn't moved.

The Raiders are a good football team. I said it before this game and I'm saying it after this loss. They're 3-1, which already matches last season's win total in four weeks. They have the best tight end in football, a quarterback who made NFL history in his first three games here, a defense that leads the league in takeaways, and a head coach who says "I blew it" into a microphone instead of hiding behind coachspeak.

What they don't yet have is the margin for error that Kansas City has. The Chiefs win games like this because they don't hand you two kickoff fumbles and they don't give points away. That's the difference between good and great, and it's a difference measured in details rather than talent.
