THE DETAIL THAT TELLS YOU EVERYTHING ABOUT THIS KID

Here's the part of the Stokes story I love most.

He reportedly wanted to play last week against New Orleans. He was pushing to be out there. And the Raiders looked at a young player with a head injury, thought about the long game, and told him on Friday that the plan was patience.

That is exactly the kind of adult decision you want from a franchise. Nobody wins a Week Three game and then loses a season because they got impatient with a rookie's brain. They held him out, they won anyway, and now they get him at full strength for the one game where every ounce of talent matters.

It's the same philosophy we praised with Brock Bowers and the knee procedure, and look how that turned out: ten catches, 116 yards, and a touchdown in his first game back.

WHY STOKES MATTERS SO MUCH

Now here's the honest version of why this is such a big deal, and it isn't only about who's coming back. It's about who isn't starting.

With Stokes at free safety, Isaiah Pola-Mao won't be lining up as the starter there on Sunday. I'm not going to dress that up: he had a difficult afternoon in New Orleans, and we watched Tyler Shough throw four touchdown passes, every single one of them to a tight end, with the middle of the field wide open.

That isn't personal. It's the film. And against an offense with Patrick Mahomes at quarterback, you cannot afford a communication breakdown on the back end. One busted coverage and it's a 40-yard touchdown with a celebration attached.

So what does Stokes actually bring? He plays free safety. He slides down and plays nickel. He moves around that secondary like a chess piece that keeps changing its own rules, which means an offense can't simply find him before the snap and attack somewhere else, because next play he's in a different spot.

And the thing that jumps off the tape is the communication. You can see it: players pointing, adjusting, everybody on the same page before the snap. In today's NFL, a secondary that talks survives. A secondary that doesn't becomes the other team's highlight reel.

Look at the pattern. He played in Weeks One and Two. He missed Week Three. The defense was excellent in the games he played and spent three quarters getting carved up in the one he didn't. I'll be fair and admit the takeaway battle helped in those first two games and turnovers can be streaky, so the whole story doesn't live in one stat. But anybody who actually watched the film will tell you the same thing: it's night and day.

THE NAME ON THE WHITEBOARD ISN'T MAHOMES

Now the part of this week that genuinely surprised me.

Everybody assumes a Chiefs game plan means one thing: figure out how to slow down Mahomes and Travis Kelce and pray Andy Reid doesn't unfold some ridiculous trick play. That's the normal approach.

But the word out of the building is that the Raiders' number one objective this week, the thing circled in red, is stopping running back Kenneth Walker.

And when you look at his season, it makes complete sense. Sixty-five carries for 360 yards. Nineteen runs that produced first downs. Four hundred and forty total yards from scrimmage and four touchdowns. By the numbers, he's playing like one of the top five running backs in football right now.

Here's why a back that good is the real problem. He doesn't just gain yards; he changes the geometry of your defense. Safeties creep toward the box to stop him. Once safeties come down, there's more space downfield. Once there's more space downfield, Mahomes starts throwing the ball the way he did a few seasons ago, when every deep shot looked like a magic trick.

Take away Walker and the whole machine stops humming. Leave him alone and you're defending the best quarterback of this era with your safeties out of position. That's the entire chess match in one sentence.

THE CONNECTION CHIEFS FANS WON'T LIKE

And here's where it gets fun.

Klint Kubiak knows Kenneth Walker. Last season Kubiak was the offensive coordinator in Seattle, and Walker was one of his players. He knows how Walker likes to cut, how he reads a hole, what he does when he's fresh and what he does when he's tired.

That's the kind of knowledge you cannot get from watching tape. Tape shows you what a player did. Coaching a player shows you why he did it.

From what I'm told, Kubiak isn't only working from memory either. He's leaning on connections from past coaching stops, including his father, Gary Kubiak, a name that carries serious football weight. Coaches talk to coaches, and everybody who has coached Walker or coached against him is a phone call away.

If there's a blueprint for slowing this guy down, Kubiak might be the one man in the league best positioned to assemble it.

Now, let me be honest, because somebody is already typing it in the comments: the Raiders have a plan, and Andy Reid has been beating plans for a decade. That's fair. Sometimes great players are just great. That's why Mahomes is Mahomes and Kelce is Kelce, and it's why this division has been terrorized for years. You can prepare perfectly all week and still watch them do something absurd.

But this week feels different for three specific reasons: a head coach who knows the opposing running back inside out, a defensive coordinator in Rob Leonard whose unit finally has an identity and leads the league in pressure rate without blitzing, and a free safety returning at exactly the right moment. That's a lot of variables pointing the same direction.

THE BIGGER PRIZE INSIDE THIS GAME

There's a long-term angle here too, and I don't think enough people are talking about it.

In this league, blueprints spread. When one team figures out how to slow down a dominant player or a dominant offense, that tape gets passed around the entire NFL by Monday morning. So if this defense finds a way to control Walker, even if the Raiders don't win the game, every defensive coordinator in football is stealing that film next week.

That's bad news for Kansas City over the long haul, because a team that gets beaten by a specific plan tends to get beaten by that plan again. And it's good news for us, because it means Sunday isn't just about one win. It's about writing the script for how you challenge the standard in this conference.

THE COACH NOBODY SAW COMING

Speaking of Kubiak, let's appreciate where he sits right now, because it's absurd.

He isn't even 40 years old. Look at the rest of this division: Sean Payton, a veteran. Jim Harbaugh, a lifetime of football. Andy Reid, a legend who has been drawing up plays since the nineties. And then there's the young guy in Las Vegas.

The stat making the rounds this week is that he's the first coach in a very long time to start 3-0 with the first overall pick in the building, and the last name attached to that kind of run is Vince Lombardi.

Let me be clear about what I'm saying and what I'm not. I am not saying Klint Kubiak is Vince Lombardi. I'm saying that when a statistic puts your name in that neighborhood three games into your head coaching career, the rest of the league has earned the right to pay attention. And so have we.

ALLEGIANT NEEDS TO BE BLACK

Now the part that belongs to you.

There's a call going out across the fan base for a blackout at Allegiant Stadium on Sunday. Everybody wears black. Everybody stands up. Everybody screams until their voice sounds like a garbage disposal.

THE QUIET ROSTER MOVE

One more piece of news that looks small and isn't, because this league is a domino game.

The Raiders signed wide receiver Tyler Scott to the practice squad and parted ways with Ronnie Bell. Scott was a fourth-round pick in the 2023 draft who has bounced around, including practice squad time with the Rams and the Seahawks, and he hasn't played in a regular season game since 2024.

So why does it matter? Two reasons. First, the Seahawks connection, which overlaps with Kubiak's past, and we've now seen three times this season what happens when this head coach brings in someone he already knows: Cody White has three touchdowns in two weeks, Dariq Young is contributing on special teams, and Kubiak said both have surpassed expectations.

Second, and more importantly, it's about what's coming. Jack Bech is going to be out for a while, and Kubiak said the roster decision there is still to be determined. When a team adds a receiver who can play special teams right as another receiver heads toward injured reserve, that's a front office lining up its dominoes before it has to push them.
