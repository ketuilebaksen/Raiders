6.7 PERCENT: The Number That Explains Why the Whole League Is Arguing About Us, and the One Question Nobody Can Answer Yet 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, ESPN's analytics gave the Las Vegas Raiders a 6.7 percent chance of starting this season 3-0.

Six point seven percent. Roughly a one in fifteen shot. And here we are.

For just the second time since 2002, this franchise has opened a season with three straight wins. And the reaction across national television this week has been fascinating, because you can hear the entire sport trying to decide what to do with us. Some of them are giving credit. Some are still hedging. One of them found the perfect phrase for it, and I'll get to that later because I love it.

So today we're going through the verdicts, the numbers behind them, the one legitimate question nobody on any of those panels could answer, and the hidden benefit of this start that has nothing to do with the standings.

I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. Smash that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we chase 6,000 believers. Let's get into it.

THE VERDICTS, FROM HEDGING TO CONVERTED

Let's start with the skeptics, because being honest about them is how this channel earns your trust.

One legendary former receiver said it plainly: he doesn't think this team is a contender, he doesn't think they're a very good team yet, and he expects a falloff from Kirk Cousins at some point because we all know who Kirk Cousins is. But then he gave credit where it's due. He said Klint Kubiak has proven to be a good coach doing what he's doing with less than superior talent, that this young team is gaining confidence, that there is real talent on the roster, and that the Raiders are relevant.

Relevant. Three weeks ago the conversation was whether we'd be the worst team in football, so I'll take relevant and keep going.

Then another panelist made the point that I want tattooed on the inside of every skeptic's eyelids. The Raiders beat the Saints. The Saints beat the Ravens. And the entire national media spent a week crowning the Ravens after they beat the Cowboys. You cannot follow that chain and then tell me this win doesn't count.

And my favorite verdict of the week came from a panel that went around in circles on the question "are the Raiders good?" until somebody finally said it: they're 3-and-good.

That's it. That's the answer. This is a league about momentum. Are the Patriots 1-2? Are the Texans struggling? Those things change every single year. As one analyst put it, this looks more like the Raiders' year than the Patriots' or the Texans' or the Packers' year. You are what your record says you are until the schedule proves otherwise.

THE STAT THAT SEPARATES US FROM EVERY OTHER 3-0 TEAM

Here's a note I hadn't seen anywhere else, and it's remarkable.

The Raiders are the fourth team in the common draft era to start 3-0 after making the first overall pick in the previous draft. But they are the only one of those four whose number one pick hasn't played at any point.

Read that again. Every other franchise in that situation had its prize rookie on the field. Las Vegas has won three straight games with the first overall pick standing on the sideline in his helmet, listening to play calls.

That's either the most patient decision this organization has made in twenty years, or the luckiest. I think it's the former, and the national panels are starting to agree.

THE HIDDEN VALUE OF EVERY WIN

Which brings me to the smartest observation I heard all week, and it had nothing to do with the standings.

One analyst pointed out that there's hidden value in every one of these wins: with each victory, the Raiders get to keep Fernando Mendoza on the bench that much longer.

Think about how that works. A losing team gets impatient. The fans want the rookie, the media demands the rookie, the front office needs something to sell tickets with, and suddenly a 22-year-old is starting on a team that can't protect him with a coaching staff fighting for its job. That's how quarterbacks get ruined.

A winning team gets to do it properly. And one of the panelists, who actually played for Klint Kubiak's father, said exactly that: knowing that family, they are not going to rush Mendoza. He also relayed something Tom Brady has said, that this league rushes these guys too much, that nobody wants to learn how to play the position anymore, they just want to throw you out there and let you learn on the run with no development at all.

His advice for a young quarterback sitting and watching was simple and perfect: be the first one in and the last one to leave, always ask questions, and understand that there are no dumb questions, because just sitting there by itself doesn't get you where you need to be.

That's exactly what Mendoza is doing with that helmet on, and it's why I've stopped worrying about when he plays. He'll play when he's ready, and this franchise has bought him the most valuable thing a young quarterback can have: time.

THE ONE QUESTION NOBODY COULD ANSWER

Now here's the part of the conversation I found most interesting, because it's a real football question and nobody on those panels could settle it.

One former coach made the point that this team is dramatically better than last year's on both sides of the line of scrimmage. He described last season's offensive line as an absolute sieve. Then he asked the question: is the line actually better, or does it just look better because Kirk Cousins gets the football out of his hands and doesn't take sacks?

I've been going back and forth on that all week, and here's my honest answer: it's both, and the proportions matter.

The line is genuinely better. Tyler Linderbaum at center is a real upgrade, and Kolton Miller being healthy after missing 13 games last season changes everything on the left side. But we also watched the interior get pushed around at SoFi while Ashton Jeanty averaged 2.3 yards a carry, and we watched DJ Glaze give up a sack and miss a run assignment in that same game. That's not a finished unit.

And a quarterback who processes fast and throws on time makes any line look better than it is. That's the least glamorous skill in football and the most valuable one for a team with questions up front.

Which is why the panel landed where it did: it always boils down to the guys up front. The blocking is the boring part, but you don't get to the fun stuff without it. That's also exactly why I said earlier this week that if John Spytek has one move to make before November 10th, the interior offensive line has a stronger claim on it than another receiver.

THE BIGGER POINT ABOUT LAST YEAR

There was one more thread on those shows that I think Raider Nation should sit with.

Look at what's happening around the league with players who left this building. Geno Smith is playing well in New York. Look at how Ashton Jeanty is being used now versus last year. Look at this roster's record with essentially the same young core. One analyst drew the obvious conclusion: maybe last year's team wasn't as bad as its record, and maybe the coaching and the leadership were a significant part of the problem.

Now, in fairness, another panelist pushed back correctly. A lot of additions were made this offseason. The roster genuinely changed. Swapping one veteran quarterback for another isn't the whole story.

Both things are true, and together they tell you what actually happened here: the talent was never as bad as 3-14 suggested, and the structure around it was worse than anybody admitted. Fix the structure, add the right pieces, and you get 3-0.

THE NUMBERS FROM SUNDAY

Let me give you the full accounting, because the box score deserves it.

Cousins has now thrown at least three touchdown passes in all three games: 160 yards and three touchdowns in the win over Miami, 253 yards and three touchdowns at SoFi, and 22 of 33 for 248 yards with three touchdowns in New Orleans. No Raiders quarterback has ever opened a season that way. One of the panelists called him surgical when you give him time in the pocket, and that's the right word. He's not asked to carry this team. He's asked to be accurate and available, and he has been both.

Brock Bowers had ten catches on thirteen targets for 116 yards and a touchdown in his first game back. Thirteen targets for a tight end, in his season debut, off a knee procedure. One panel spent a full minute arguing about whether he or Trey McBride is the best tight end in football and concluded it's a coin flip depending on taste. That's the company he's in now.

Mike Washington Jr. broke off a 36-yard touchdown run, and the veterans on that panel loved it, pointing out that this isn't just preseason production anymore. When he gets his chances, he makes plays. The Raiders suddenly have a real one-two punch in that backfield.

On the other side, Tyler Shough went 29 of 42 for 255 yards with four touchdowns and an interception. Juwan Johnson caught eight passes for 53 yards and two touchdowns, and then lost the fumble that ended the game. New Orleans committed eight penalties and turned the football over four times: two Shough fumbles lost, the Johnson fumble, and the interception to Hezekiah Masses.

And one detail I want to add, because it shows how fine the margins were. On the Masses interception, the ball was punched out during the return, and Adam Butler fell on it to keep possession for Las Vegas. Two failed two-point conversions in the fourth quarter kept this to a one-score game longer than it needed to be. This was not a comfortable afternoon.

THE CALL THE SAINTS WILL BE COMPLAINING ABOUT

I should mention one more thing, because if you watched that game you saw it, and their fan base is going to talk about it all week.

There was a play where Maxx Crosby got to Shough and the ball came loose, and the panel spent a while arguing about whether it should have been ruled a forward pass instead of a fumble. One analyst was adamant that he saw the hand come forward and that it should have been a forward pass. The others told him he was crazy. He cheerfully agreed that he might be crazy and stood by it anyway.

I'll be fair, because this channel doesn't hide behind officiating. New Orleans has been on the wrong side of a couple of these calls already this season, including a strange one in Week One against Detroit where the ball clearly went backwards and it was ruled incomplete. If you're a Saints fan, you've earned the right to be annoyed.

But here's the honest accounting of Sunday: eight penalties, four turnovers, two lost fumbles by the quarterback, and a tight end losing the ball in the final minute. No single call decided that game. You cannot hand the football to the other team four times in the fourth quarter on the road and blame a replay booth.

GOOD TEAMS FIND WAYS TO WIN

The best framing of all came from the veterans on one show, and it's a principle worth keeping all season.

You measure an offense and a defense by what they do when they absolutely have to have it. Can you get into field goal range when you need it? Can you get the game-winning touchdown? And defensively, can you get off the field on third or fourth down when a stop is the whole game?

What good is averaging 35 points a game if you can't pick up one first down on the last drive? What good is being the top-ranked defense if you can't get a stop when a stop is all that's left?

On Sunday, New Orleans couldn't do it. They had a lead in the fourth quarter, the game was there for the taking, and their offense couldn't stay on the field. Las Vegas could. Thirteen straight points, four takeaways, and a veteran quarterback who knows how to spread the ball around when the pressure is highest.

Good teams find ways to win. Bad teams find ways to lose. This franchise has been the second one for years. Right now it's the first one, and that's a habit, not an accident.
