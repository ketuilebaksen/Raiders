THE DEFENSIVE FORMULA, IN NUMBERS

Start with the defense, because this is where the surprise lives.

Through two games, the Raiders are tied for the most sacks in the NFL with eight. They held the Chargers to 14 points. And here's the number that made me sit up: this defense has generated pressure on 51.5 percent of opponent dropbacks through two weeks. That figure sits in the 98th percentile of team performances since 2022.

Now here's the part that makes it sustainable. They're doing it with a blitz rate of just 26 percent.

Let me explain why that combination matters so much, because this is the heart of Rob Leonard's system. Any defense can generate pressure by sending six rushers. The problem is what happens behind it. When you blitz, you're playing man coverage with fewer defenders, and if the quarterback gets the ball out, you're giving up an explosive play. Pressure that comes with risk attached isn't really pressure. It's a coin flip.

What Leonard is doing is different. He's getting quarterbacks uncomfortable on roughly half of their dropbacks while keeping enough bodies in coverage to take away the easy answer. The quarterback feels the rush, looks downfield, and there's nowhere to go. That's when sacks and interceptions happen.

And that's why the Chargers looked like they were fighting for every yard on Sunday. They weren't facing an all-out blitz. They were facing four or five rushers who were simply winning.

MAXX CROSBY: IMPACT THAT DOESN'T NEED A SACK

The best example of what I mean is Maxx Crosby, and this is something I want every fan to understand about how to watch him.

Crosby drew three false starts out of Joe Alt on Sunday. Three. Including a stretch where the Chargers pushed themselves backward into much worse down-and-distance situations without the Raiders doing anything except lining up. Then, late in the game, he came up with the safety that put this thing away.

That's the two points that turned 24-14 into 26-14, by the way. Crosby scored the last points of the game himself, in a game where he wasn't piling up sacks.

I say this every week and I'll keep saying it: you don't need number 98 to finish every play in the backfield to get his value. If a left tackle is so worried about him that he jumps before the snap, that's impact. If an offensive coordinator changes his protection to account for where Crosby is lined up, that's impact. And all of that attention is what creates one-on-one opportunities for everybody else on the field.

THE KOONCE ADJUSTMENT: WE CALLED IT, AND KUBIAK DID IT

Now this is my favorite part of the entire rewatch, and I need you to go back and check our episode from last week, because we laid this out in detail.

Here's what we said about Malcolm Koonce. Not that he isn't good, but that he was being used in the wrong situations. We showed the numbers: in Week One he played 29 snaps to Foley Fatukasi's 18, and a lot of those snaps came on early downs defending the run. And we said the blueprint should be to shave early-down run snaps away from Koonce, give those reps to Fatukasi, and keep Koonce fresh for the pass-rush packages where he's a genuine problem.

That is exactly what happened at SoFi.

Koonce played fewer snaps overall, spent less time in run-stopping situations, and got more chances to do the one thing he does best: attack the quarterback. The result? A sack and a half, two quarterback hits, and a forced fumble.

That's a completely different player from Week One, and nothing about him changed. The usage changed. That's coaching, and it's exactly the kind of in-season adjustment this franchise hasn't made in years. Same player, right role, and suddenly he looks like the investment the Raiders thought they were making.

MASSES AND THE GREEN DOT

We already talked about Hezekiah Masses after the game, but the film adds something to his day. He played 50 snaps, finished with five tackles, two pass breakups, and two interceptions.

And here's what the box score can't show you: confidence. He wasn't playing scared. He trusted what he saw, jumped routes, and put himself in position to make plays. Now, I'll be honest, like I always am with you: he got a little fortunate on one of those interceptions. The ball found him. But you have to be in the right position for the ball to find you, and he was, over and over.

Then there's Nakobe Dean, who might be the most important piece of this whole thing. Ten tackles, half a sack, two quarterback hits, and the forced fumble that Jeremy Chinn scooped up to break the game open.

But what I really want to point out is that Dean is wearing the green dot. That means he's the one getting the defensive call in his helmet and lining everybody else up. And you can see the difference on tape. Nobody is looking around confused before the snap. Nobody is hesitating. Players are reacting instead of thinking. A defense that communicates well plays fast, and this one is playing fast.

Remember when the national panel praised these young defenders flying around? That's what they were seeing. Fast play starts with clear communication.

WHY LEONARD GETS THE GAME BALL

So if I have to name one person as the reason this formula works, I'm not picking a player. There were too many good individual performances to choose from: Crosby, Koonce, Masses, Dean. I'm picking Rob Leonard.

Because the common thread isn't that the Raiders suddenly have superstars everywhere. It's that every player is being asked to do the thing he's actually good at. Koonce rushes the passer instead of setting an edge. Fatukasi eats blocks in the middle. Crosby gets moved around to create the matchup they want. Masses gets to play aggressive with safety help behind him. Dean gets the keys to the communication.

Put players in position to succeed and average units start playing above their talent level. That's what a good coordinator does, and that's what's happening here.

NOW THE HARD PART: THE OFFENSIVE LINE

Okay. Time for the honest section, because this rewatch left me genuinely concerned about one group.

I went through every snap watching Kolton Miller, Spencer Burford, Tyler Linderbaum, Jackson Powers-Johnson, and DJ Glaze, and this was the biggest worry coming out of the game. Strangely, the player I thought performed best was Glaze, and he gave up a sack.

The bigger problem was the interior. The Raiders were not consistently winning at the point of attack. Linderbaum didn't play the kind of game you expect from the money this team invested in him. Miller had some rough moments, especially in run blocking. Burford and Powers-Johnson weren't good enough either.

And this is a real issue, not a nitpick, because of what this team says it wants to be. Klint Kubiak's identity is built on running the football, two-back personnel, and play action off the run. Remember the Miami game: two-back packages on over a third of their snaps, 6.4 yards a play out of it. That identity requires winning up front. On Sunday, they didn't, and the run game showed it.

Ashton Jeanty finished with 21 carries for 48 yards. That's 2.3 yards a carry. Now, some of that is on the blocking. But I also have to hold Jeanty to the standard he set for himself, and the film showed three drops. We mentioned drops in our recap, and the rewatch made clear how many belonged to the bell-cow back. When you're getting that volume of opportunities, you have to cash them in.

I also would have liked to see more of the backup running back once it was clear Jeanty wasn't having his best day. Sometimes a fresh set of legs finds a crease that a struggling starter can't.

And at receiver, Jalen Nailor had one catch for nine yards on three targets. That's not enough, especially with Brock Bowers still out. There are real questions about his separation right now, and that's worth watching.

Here's the crazy part, though: the Raiders won by twelve anyway. That's the whole point of today's episode. They lost some of the trench battles and still won, because other players made the right play at the right moment.

THE RIGHT PLAY AT THE RIGHT TIME

Which brings me to the guys who did exactly that.

Tre Tucker was the first. Five catches, 119 yards, and honestly he played the role of a true number one receiver. The 42-yarder stretched the field. But my favorite was the play right before halftime, when Cousins threw it into double coverage and Tucker just went up and took it. That set up Matt Gay's 59-yard field goal, three points that never happen if Tucker doesn't win that ball. And once the Chargers had to start respecting him, the field opened up.

Then Cody White. Two catches, 24 yards, two touchdowns. But here's the detail that matters: both catches came on third down. Both times the Raiders needed a play. Both times he made it. And it wasn't out of nowhere, because he made an important special teams play the week before. That's a man earning a permanent spot on this roster.

That also says something about Kubiak. You have to trust a player enough to put him in that spot, believe he'll execute, and then actually call the play. A lot of coaches wouldn't.

Cousins deserves credit too. Was he perfect? No. He got away with a couple of throws, and the arm isn't what it was ten years ago. But 253 yards, three touchdowns, six on the season, and a 2-0 record. The job is winning games, and he's winning them.

And don't overlook Connor Hayward converting a fourth-and-one early in the game. In a season where this team wants to be physical, that's the kind of play that sets a tone even though nobody remembers it on Monday.

And one more thing about that scoring sequence. Look at how the 26 points were built: a touchdown, a long field goal, two third-down touchdown catches from a backup receiver, and a safety from your best defender. That is not a team riding one superstar. That is a team where six or seven different people contributed points. Balance like that is harder to game-plan against than one big name.

WHAT STILL NEEDS CLEANING UP ON DEFENSE

The defense wasn't flawless either. Kwity Paye didn't make the same impact he did against Miami. Isaiah Pola-Mao had a tougher time after Trayden Stokes left the game and the Chargers started going at him. And Jermod McCoy had a rough afternoon; he looked young, and next to what Masses gave this team, the contrast was obvious.

I'm not overreacting to one game from a young corner. But there's an argument for getting another young defensive back some reps, not to bury McCoy, but to take pressure off him, clean up his technique, and make sure one bad day doesn't become a confidence problem. Young players need to be protected as much as they need to be pushed.

THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's my takeaway from the rewatch, Raider Nation. The biggest story of this 2-0 start isn't that the Raiders have a perfect offense. They don't. The offensive line has real problems, the run game hasn't found its footing, and the receiver room still needs answers.

The story is that this team has found a way to win while those problems exist. A defense that pressures you without gambling. A coordinator who puts his players in the right spots. Role players who show up on third down. That's how good teams survive their bad days, and surviving bad days is exactly what a 3-14 team never knew how to do.

Fix the interior line, and this team gets dangerous. Don't fix it, and the Chiefs will find it in Week Four.
