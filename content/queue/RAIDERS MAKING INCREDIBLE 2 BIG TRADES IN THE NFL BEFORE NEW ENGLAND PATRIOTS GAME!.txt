## THE OPPONENT: NEW ENGLAND IS 2-2 AND DANGEROUS

First, the setup. The Raiders are 3-1 and travel to face a New England team that just improved to 2-2 with an important win over the Buffalo Bills.

Read that again. The Patriots beat Buffalo. That's not a rebuilding team we're flying into. That's a team with a legitimate victory over one of the best rosters in this conference, trying to climb above .500, hosting a Raiders team trying to make sure one loss doesn't become two.

And Mike Vrabel is not treating this like an ordinary matchup, which is the most interesting part of the week.

## VRABEL'S WARNING: WHAT HE SAID ABOUT COUSINS

Speaking on the October 5th edition of the Greg Hill Show, Vrabel talked about Las Vegas, and the respect was obvious.

He faced this organization last season, but as he pointed out, he's now preparing for a different head coach, a different quarterback, and a team playing with significantly more confidence.

On Kirk Cousins specifically, he praised him for performing at a high level and explained that a veteran's long history of success is not accidental. His words: Cousins is intelligent, accurate, and particularly effective in situational football.

That last phrase is the one coaches use when they mean something specific. Situational football is third down, red zone, two-minute, and short-yardage. It's the moments where knowing the answer matters more than arm strength. Vrabel is telling his own fan base that this quarterback is going to make the right decision in exactly the moments where games are decided.

He also said the Raiders are playing well and displaying the confidence every coaching staff wants its team to possess.

Here's why that matters practically: New England cannot build a defensive plan around the assumption that Cousins is just managing games. Kansas City pressured him consistently and he kept moving the offense, finishing with 365 yards and giving his team a chance to tie the game on the final possession. Vrabel has watched that tape and is preparing accordingly.

## VRABEL'S WARNING: WHAT SCARES HIM ABOUT OUR DEFENSE

Then he identified the Raiders' pass rush and turnover production as serious concerns for his team, and he got specific about the fix, which is where this becomes a roadmap for Rob Leonard.

New England had protection problems against Buffalo. So Vrabel emphasized that the Patriots have to secure the football when the pocket starts collapsing. He said the offensive line may need to hold protection slightly longer while the quarterback looks downfield, but that the quarterback himself has to recognize when pressure is arriving, put two hands on the football, and decide whether to keep scanning or move to the checkdown. And he wants the interior protection to create space for his quarterback to step forward instead of letting the pocket collapse from every direction.

Raider Nation, that is a head coach publicly listing his own vulnerabilities, and it's a gift.

Translate it into our terms. Las Vegas sacked Patrick Mahomes twice in the 30-27 loss and has been generating takeaways all season, with nine through the first three games leading the league. If New England's interior protection is the worry, then the interior rush is the answer: Adam Butler, Foley Fatukasi, Tonka Hemingway, and whatever pressure Leonard can manufacture up the middle to prevent that quarterback from stepping forward.

And the outside plan writes itself. Maxx Crosby and the supporting rush, now including Nolan Smith in the rotation, have to create pressure without exposing a secondary that's playing with young corners. Force the quarterback to hold the football, then convert that pressure into sacks or turnovers. That's the formula that produced nine takeaways in three weeks, and it's exactly the formula Vrabel just admitted he's worried about.

## THE CHARGERS ARE 0-4, AND IT CHANGES EVERYTHING

Now the development that reshapes this entire division and creates every trade scenario we're about to discuss.

The Los Angeles Chargers entered this season carrying Super Bowl expectations. They have opened with four consecutive losses and are now competing near the top of the draft order instead of the top of the AFC West.

There's still time for them to recover, technically. But history is brutal here: only one team in NFL history has reached the playoffs after starting without a victory through four games, and no team has ever qualified after beginning 0-5.

Think about what that means for us. We beat them at SoFi in Week Two. They're 0-4. Kansas City is undefeated and in first. Kay Adams made this exact point on her show, noting that the Chargers have struggled and the Broncos still have questions to answer, which leaves real room for the Raiders to establish themselves in the postseason race.

And it means something else, something a lot more interesting: if a team is 0-4 and sliding, it starts thinking about trading veterans for future draft capital.

Which brings us to the name every Raiders fan over the age of twenty just felt in their chest.

## KHALIL MACK, COMING HOME?

Khalil Mack would immediately become one of the most interesting possibilities in football if Los Angeles becomes a seller.

Let me set the table on who we're talking about. He's 35 years old. He began his NFL career with the Raiders. He won Defensive Player of the Year while representing this franchise. He's a nine-time Pro Bowl selection, and he is still producing at a high level, credited with three sacks this season, though ESPN's Dan Graziano referenced two sacks through his first three games when he explained why Mack could attract interest.

Graziano's framing is the honest one, and I want to represent it accurately. He does not believe Los Angeles trades a respected veteran if the team recovers and starts resembling the contender everybody expected. But if the Chargers are still outside the playoff race as early November approaches, he believes Mack could appeal to a contender looking for pass rush help for the stretch run.

## THE CONTRACT MATH THAT MAKES THIS REAL

Here's the part that moves this from fantasy to football, and it's the detail that most fans miss.

Mack signed a one-year, $18 million agreement during the offseason. But $10 million of that came through a signing bonus that Los Angeles has already paid. Which means an acquiring team would be responsible only for the remaining portion of his $8 million salary at the time of a trade.

In other words, by the time you get to early November, you'd be paying a fraction of eight million dollars for a nine-time Pro Bowl edge rusher for a playoff push.

That's not a cap problem. That's a rounding error for a contender, and it's exactly the kind of structure that makes deadline deals actually happen instead of just getting discussed on television.

## THE FOOTBALL FIT IS OBVIOUS

And the football case needs about one paragraph, because we've been making it all season.

Maxx Crosby remains the foundation of this defense, and he's drawn more double teams than any player in the league. The entire theory of this front is that when protections slide toward number 98, somebody else has to punish it. That's why Kwity Paye had a two-sack debut. That's why Malcolm Koonce has turned into a closer in pass-rush situations. That's why Nolan Smith was added to the rotation.

Now add a nine-time Pro Bowler to that group. Rob Leonard suddenly has four or five legitimate rushers to rotate, which means fresh legs in the fourth quarter, which is where this defense already does its best work. And it means offenses can't slide help anywhere without creating a one-on-one somewhere else.

Plus the emotional layer, which is real even if it isn't tactical. It would bring Mack back to the organization where his career began, with a chance to pursue unfinished business on a Raiders team that suddenly looks capable of competing.

## WHY IT'S COMPLICATED

Now the honest obstacles, because this isn't as simple as fans want it to be.

**It's a divisional trade.** The Chargers are a longtime rival, and Jim Harbaugh would have little desire to strengthen a team Los Angeles has to face twice a year. Trades inside a division are unusual precisely because the selling team risks watching that player beat them.

**Mack may have to want it.** Realistically, he might need to express a clear desire to finish his career in Las Vegas before the Chargers would seriously consider helping create that reunion. Nothing has been reported on that front.

**The compensation has to stay disciplined.** He's still effective, but he's 35 and could retire after this season. Surrendering a major package for a one-year rental conflicts directly with the long-term foundation John Spytek is trying to build. This is the same discipline test as every other name on this board.

The one thing that would lower the barrier: if Los Angeles falls completely out of contention, the downside of trading within the division shrinks, because by then they'd already be looking at next year's roster.

And I want to be absolutely clear about what this is. There is no evidence that Las Vegas and Los Angeles are negotiating. There is no confirmation that Mack has asked to return. This is a scenario created by three things happening at once: the Chargers' disastrous start, the Raiders' unexpected emergence, and a respected insider saying out loud that Mack could become available if the collapse continues.

If all three conditions are still in place in early November, one of the most emotional reunions this fan base could imagine becomes a conversation worth having.

## THE RECEIVER PROBLEM, IN NUMBERS

Now the other half of the deadline conversation, and the numbers from the Chiefs game make the case better than I can.

Against Kansas City, the Raiders' most productive pass catchers both played tight end. Brock Bowers caught six of 11 targets for 86 yards. Michael Mayer caught eight of 10 targets for 82 yards. And the leading wide receiver was Tre Tucker with four receptions on eight targets for 44 yards.

That's your passing attack: two tight ends combining for 168 yards and a wide receiver corps producing 44.

The urgency has increased because Jack Bech and Dante Thornton Jr. are both on injured reserve, leaving an already limited position group with even fewer proven options, and Jalen Nailor is in the concussion protocol.

So if the Raiders stay above .500 as the November 10th deadline approaches, this becomes the single most obvious need on the roster.

## THE JEUDY FILE, FULLY UPDATED

Which brings us back to Cleveland, and there's real new information.

Jerry Jeudy has recorded only five receptions for 62 yards through four games this season, according to StatMuse, while Cleveland has begun directing more opportunities toward younger players.

And this is the part that matters: Cade Crankshaw of Cleveland Browns on Sports Illustrated argued in an October 5th article that the 27-year-old receiver is likely to be traded before the deadline, because he no longer appears to fit the organization's future plans.

Look at where Cleveland's targets are going instead. Rookie receivers J.C. Conception and Denzel Boston have already absorbed a meaningful share. Tight end Harold Fannin Jr., Isaiah Bond, and their running backs have all received expanded opportunities. Moving Jeudy would let the Browns create more work for the players they're developing while reducing his impact on the salary cap.

Now, his slow start does not mean his ability has evaporated. He's still in his prime years, and in 2024 he produced the strongest season of his career: 90 receptions, 1,229 yards and four touchdowns across 17 games, which earned him his first Pro Bowl selection. That's what he can do in a system that consistently creates opportunities for him.

## THE KUBIAK CONNECTION, MORE PRECISE THAN I HAD IT

And here's a detail I want to correct and sharpen from our earlier breakdowns, because the specifics matter.

Klint Kubiak served as the Denver Broncos' passing game coordinator and quarterbacks coach in 2022 while Jeudy was with that organization. That's not "they were in the same building." That's the man who coordinated the passing game having direct, daily familiarity with this receiver's strengths, his preparation habits, and how he fits an offense.

Jeudy also understands the AFC West, having begun his career in Denver.

And the national view is lining up. Carter Bonds of CBS Sports recently included the Raiders among five potential destinations for the Browns receiver. In his October 1st analysis, Bonds praised Kubiak for constructing a productive offense despite working with an older quarterback and a receiver group lacking established star power, and argued that while Bowers will remain the featured target, a tight end cannot be responsible for carrying an entire passing game, especially with injuries thinning the options around him.

His conclusion is the one I agree with: Jeudy could immediately become the best wide receiver on this roster even if Bowers stayed above him in the target hierarchy.

That distinction is the whole point. The purpose isn't to replace Bowers. It's to create a second threat capable of winning outside and separating from coverage, so that defenses can't devote almost everything to the tight ends. Cousins has shown he can distribute the football efficiently when the protection holds. The offense becomes much easier to defend when opponents already know where most of the targets are going.

And there's a bonus effect: adding Jeudy would give Tucker a role better suited to his speed instead of forcing him to operate as the unquestioned number one receiver, which he isn't built to be.

## MY PRICE, AND THE HONEST CAVEAT

Same discipline as always. Crankshaw believes Cleveland should trade Jeudy and Bonds considers Las Vegas a logical landing spot, but neither report establishes that the Raiders and Browns have open negotiations. This is analysis, not reporting.

Spytek would need to evaluate the asking price, the contract obligations, and the real question: does Jeudy's limited production this season reflect Cleveland's changing priorities, or a more serious decline?

My position hasn't moved. At a conditional fourth-round pick, you make that trade. At a straight third, I'd hesitate and want the medical and the tape to be clean. Above that, I'd rather spend the capital on the interior offensive line, because 72 rushing yards at 2.8 a carry is also a crisis.

But understand that this connection is more than a random name on a list. Previous experience with the head coach, proven Pro Bowl production, and a skill set that addresses the clearest weakness on this roster. That's three boxes checked.

## THE THIRD-QUARTER SEQUENCE THAT ACTUALLY DECIDED IT

Before we close, I want to give you the precise detail of the decision everybody's arguing about, because I've now seen the full sequence and it's more nuanced than the shorthand version.

Las Vegas entered halftime with the lead, and Kubiak had told the broadcast his team intended to win.

On the Raiders' first possession of the second half, the offense moved into scoring position, the drive stalled, and Matt Gay converted the field goal. Then an illegal formation penalty against Kansas City gave Las Vegas the option to take the points off the board and attempt a fourth-down conversion instead.

Kubiak chose aggression. The Raiders accepted the penalty, erased the field goal, and sent the offense back out. Kansas City stopped the play. No points, and possession changed hands.

In a game decided by three, that abandoned field goal became one of the defining decisions of the afternoon.

And I'll say what I said before: I understand the philosophy, I disagree with that specific call, and I respect enormously that he didn't hide from it. He called the sequence a total missed opportunity and said, "So, I blew it." He also took the blame for the run game, insisting the Raiders have solid backs and a solid offensive line, and putting the responsibility on his staff to design better situations for those players.

## THE RELATIONSHIP THAT WILL CARRY THIS TEAM

And then Cousins refused to let him carry it alone, which is the part I keep thinking about.

The quarterback told reporters that Kubiak consistently points the thumb at himself and looks for ways he can improve, and that he appreciates that quality. But he explained that multiple factors affected that fourth-down sequence: they were trying to reach the ideal play while the play clock kept running, and he now believes he should have accepted a delay of game penalty instead of rushing to snap the football. Losing five yards would have been frustrating, but it might have let Las Vegas reset, pick a better play, and avoid attacking Kansas City from a bad situation. He said he'd spend the night examining whether taking the penalty would have been better, and acknowledged the game contained hundreds of smaller mistakes beyond his coach's choice.

Think about the structure of what just happened there. Kubiak protected his players by accepting public blame. Cousins protected his coach by identifying what he could have handled differently.

That shared accountability is how you keep one painful play from creating division inside an organization. And it's worth remembering the context: Kubiak is a first-year head coach who was matching wits with Andy Reid, a three-time Super Bowl champion with decades of experience in exactly those situations. Being out-coached in one sequence by that man is not an indictment. It's tuition.
