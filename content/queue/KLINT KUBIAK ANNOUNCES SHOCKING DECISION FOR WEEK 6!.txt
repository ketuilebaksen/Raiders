Raider Nation, Klint Kubiak just walked to the podium and opened with the roster news before anybody could ask a question — and the first thing out of his mouth was a decision that is going to frustrate a lot of you.

**Dante Thornton Jr. will not be elevated this week.** Neither will Carter Renan. After three weeks of practice-window talk, after a full week of work, after "he proved some things last week" — the 6-foot-5 receiver is still not playing on Sunday.

But there's a second announcement that changes the entire complexion of this offense, and it's the reason the first one is survivable.

So today: the shocking decision and the honest football reason behind it, the one piece of genuinely great news, Tonka Hemingway's absence and what it costs this defense, the competition that still has no winner four days out, and then one of the best special teams breakdowns Joe DeCamillis has given all season — including exactly what happened on Matt Gay's missed field goal and the Buffalo returner who scares him.

This is the press conference format: quote, meaning, then what it means for Sunday. I'm Victor, this is Raiders Tunnel, and we're chasing 6,000 believers. Hit that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and let's go.

## THE OPENING STATEMENT — ALL OF IT

**Kubiak: "So Tonka is going to be out. Thornton and Renan will not be elevated this week, and Trey Zuhn's questionable."**

Three pieces of news in one breath. Let's take them one at a time, because each one matters.

## THE DECISION: THORNTON STILL ISN'T PLAYING

A reporter immediately followed up on whether Dante Thornton Jr. was ready.

**Kubiak: "Getting closer. Getting closer."**

Raider Nation, I understand the frustration, so let me say the obvious part first: this receiver room has been in crisis for a month, a 6-foot-5 target with 4.3 speed has been practicing in full, and he still isn't suiting up. On its face that's maddening.

But go back and look at the three benchmarks this coach laid out on the record. **Conditioning. Being assignment sound. Making plays.** He told us conditioning was already checked. He told us Thornton had put himself in position to prove the other two "this week — and maybe next week."

That "maybe next week" was not throwaway language. It was a warning, and now it's the answer.

And listen to how he described the progress when he was asked directly:

**"I'm impressed. He's looking good. He's getting back into football shape. Really doing a great job in the meeting room. He's putting himself in position."**

**"Really doing a great job in the meeting room."**

That's the tell, and it's the most important sentence about Thornton in three weeks. The meeting room is where "assignment sound" gets built. The coach is telling you the mental side is coming along — but coming along is not the same as finished, and the difference between those two things is an interception on third down.

**My honest take: I don't love it, and I don't disagree with it.** Not elevating him is consistent with everything this staff has said out loud since Week One, and a staff that holds its standard when the room is desperate is a staff whose word means something. The player isn't being buried — he's being finished. Week Seven is the realistic target now.

And remember, Carter Renan isn't being elevated either, which tells you this is a process, not a verdict on one man.

## THE GREAT NEWS: NAILOR IS AVAILABLE

**Reporter: "So Jalen will be available this week?"**
**Kubiak: "Yes."**

Raider Nation, that's the headline that actually helps this football team on Sunday.

Jalen Nailor cleared concussion protocol and he plays. I told you three days ago that his return was quietly the single biggest upgrade available this week, and now it's official.

And then Kubiak said something about him that I want to spend real time on, because it's the most sophisticated point anybody in that building has made in weeks.

**"In terms of just his production, the catch numbers aren't necessarily there — but in terms of spacing and his impact, Jalen's doing a great job. Really excited to have him back this week."**

## WHY "SPACING" IS THE WHOLE ARGUMENT

Let me defend that quote, because on the surface it looks like a coach making excuses for a $35 million receiver with five catches for 52 yards.

It isn't. Here's what spacing actually means.

Every passing concept is a geometry problem. You have five eligible receivers and a defense trying to cover the whole field with eleven bodies. **A receiver's job is not only to catch the football — it's to occupy a defender in a specific place so that somebody else is open.** If Nailor runs a vertical route on the outside and the corner goes with him, that corner is no longer available to help underneath, and Brock Bowers gets a cleaner window. If he lines up in a spot that forces a safety to widen, the box gets lighter and the run game breathes.

That's spacing. It never appears in a box score, and it's the reason good coaches value players that fans want to cut.

**And it's the direct answer to the thing we've discussed for three weeks.** The Raiders have faced the second-most stacked boxes in football because defenses weren't afraid of the players on the field. Nailor isn't a cure for that by himself, but he's a professional NFL starter whose presence changes where defenders have to stand. Without him in New England, this offense had three healthy receivers and the Patriots simply ignored two of them.

So I'll keep my criticism from earlier in the week — **he still has 35 million dollars worth of explaining to do, and I want to see one Sunday where he wins on the boundary.** But I was wrong to measure him only by catches, and this is the coaching explanation for why.

Kubiak also framed the silver lining of the absence, which is a nice bit of perspective:

**"Jalen's a playmaker. He's going to help us win a lot of games this year. You don't want him to be out for a game, but it gets him a chance to freshen up, and he'll be an impactful player for us."**

## TONKA HEMINGWAY IS OUT — AND IT COSTS MORE THAN YOU'D THINK

**Reporter: "You've put a pretty balanced rotation at defensive tackle. What does losing Tonka mean to the rest of the group?"**
**Kubiak: "Tonka has been a very steady leader for us. He had a really good April, a really good training camp. We're going to miss Tonka this week — but it's opportunities for others."**

Raider Nation, this one worries me more than most people will realize, and here's why.

We just watched New England run for whatever it wanted and we just heard this head coach say the entire weekly priority is **"we have to run the football and stop the run better."** Stopping the run starts with the interior defensive line. And now the rotation loses a body in exactly that spot, four days before James Cook comes to town.

Remember what the Kansas City film told us: Adam Butler was the team's best-graded defensive tackle on 28 snaps precisely *because* Jonah Laulu was a healthy scratch and the workload went up. Now Butler and Laulu and whoever else is active have to absorb more again, against a team that will try to run at them all afternoon.

"Opportunities for others" is the right coaching language. It's also the honest translation of "we're thinner where we can least afford it."

And note the detail about Hemingway's absence being **concussion-related** from the Week Five game — he needs medical clearance to return. Nobody should be rushing that, and I'm glad they aren't.

## AND TREY ZUHN IS QUESTIONABLE

The third piece of the opening statement: **Trey Zuhn is questionable.**

That's the rookie whose versatility across tackle, guard and center is what lets this staff field heavy packages with extra linemen. He missed New England with a knee issue. "Questionable" is better than "out," but if he can't go again, the offensive line's flexibility shrinks right as the staff is trying to fix the run game — and in a game where you want jumbo packages to help a struggling ground attack, that's a real limitation.

## THE COMPETITION STILL HAS NO WINNER — AND THAT'S THE POINT

**Reporter: "What did you see out of the competition at guard and corner this week?"**
**Kubiak: "They're all making each other better. I've said it all along — all those guys are players for us. They're all going to play. And them not knowing who's going to play, I think that only helps them improve and helps our team improve, until we kick the ball off. I do."**

So four days out, the starters at guard and cornerback are still unannounced — and the coach says the *uncertainty itself* is the developmental tool.

Then he was pushed on it: do they really not know?

**Kubiak: "I think when you practice, you probably have an idea of how many reps you get. But that's the thing — you might not think that you're the guy going, and then second play of the game, you're up. So if you're feeling sorry for yourself, if you're moping, that's not going to help yourself or the team."**

Raider Nation, that is a culture statement dressed up as a personnel answer, and it's the sharpest thing he said all day.

He's telling every player in that room: your snap count is not a measure of your worth, the depth chart can change in ninety seconds, and self-pity is a luxury nobody here can afford. **"Second play of the game, you're up."**

That's also a quiet message to Spencer Burford and to Hezekiah Masses, the two players under the most scrutiny. Neither has been benched. Neither has been promised anything. Both have to be ready. I said last week that a change needed to be made — and whatever the lineup ends up being, this is a healthier version of the message than a public benching would have been.

## THE DON SHULA QUESTION — AND A GREAT ANSWER

This was my favorite exchange of the week.

**Reporter: "I interviewed Don Shula once, and I asked him what advice he'd give a younger Don Shula. He said: pay attention to body language and how guys respond — not what they say, but what they do. So how have your guys responded this week?"**
**Kubiak: "I've been impressed. That's who they are. They're a very mature group. They want to win, and they know that it takes great practices to win. So I've been impressed with their response — and whatever Don Shula says is okay with me."**

"Whatever Don Shula says is okay with me." That's a good line from a 39-year-old head coach, and the humility in it is real.

But the substance is in the middle: **"they know that it takes great practices to win."** That's a staff that just lost by 28 and whose entire corrective action is Wednesday, Thursday and Friday. He's not promising schemes. He's pointing at habits.

And on his own bite-sized approach from earlier in the week:

**"I feel like they've responded to the coaching. The coaching staff has been very intentional. And that's the NFL — whether you're winning or losing, you're always fixing something. That's part of the league."**

**"Whether you're winning or losing, you're always fixing something."** Put that next to the 3-0 start, Raider Nation. The problems didn't appear in New England. They were always there; the takeaways were just hiding them, which this same coach admitted on Wednesday.

## "VERY IMPORTANT. VERY IMPORTANT."

**Reporter: "How important is it this week to get more contribution from your receivers to take some attention off Brock?"**
**Kubiak: "It's very important. Very important."**
**Reporter: "Have you worked on that this week?"**
**Kubiak: "We have. Yeah, we have. We have. And that's on coaching as much as it is playing — and getting those guys touches."**

He said "very important" twice and "we have" three times. In coach language, that's shouting.

And the structural admission is right there at the end: **getting those players touches is a coaching responsibility.** This is the same thread as Michael Mayer disappearing into protection duties and Washington Jr. getting one carry. The staff has now publicly accepted, three separate times this week, that the distribution of opportunity is their job and they haven't done it well enough.

That's exactly what should be fixed, and Sunday is the exam. If Nailor, Cody White and Malik Benson combine for six or seven targets and a chunk play, this offense looks completely different. If Bowers gets ten targets and everyone else gets four, nothing has changed.

## NOW THE SPECIAL TEAMS MASTER CLASS

Joe DeCamillis followed, and this was one of the most instructive sessions of the season. Pour a coffee, because there's real football in here.

## THE RETURN THAT ALMOST BROKE

**Reporter: "Devin Tomkins gave you a little bit of a spark on a return last week. What did you see from him?"**
**DeCamillis: "He did a nice job on that return. I wish we would have blocked for about a second longer and it might have been to the house right there. But he did a good job, and we'll see how things go this week — Malik's had some close ones too. So we've just got to keep working on that."**

So Devin Tomkins — the same name Mendoza said he should have thrown to on fourth down — produced the one genuine spark of that afternoon on a kick return. And the coordinator's read is that it was **one second of blocking away from a touchdown.**

Then he was asked whether seeing something that close is encouraging or discouraging, and the answer is the honest craft of the job:

**"That's really the kicking game, big time. There's just those little detail things that every once in a while you wish you'd get just right, and it's hard to do because there's so much space. But encouraged — at the same time, you're always looking to get a score. In a game like that it would be critical. Maybe we get a little closer and maybe things change right there. So obviously you'd want to get the score."**

Raider Nation, "there's so much space" is the whole explanation for why return games are so volatile. Eleven men covering sixty yards of width, and one man a half-step late turns a touchdown into a 24-yard return.

## HE EXPLAINED THE CHIEFS GOAL-LINE DECISION

This is the kind of detail you almost never get, so pay attention.

**Reporter: "A lot of special teams coordinators want their returners to put their feet on the 10-yard line and not take a ball. I noticed Malik earlier in the year took one at the goal line. Is there a place where you say, put your feet here and don't take the ball?"**
**DeCamillis: "Yeah, that's the Chiefs game you're talking about. And that was more of a game plan type situation. We knew that that guy was going to at times outkick his coverage, and we were hoping we could get a better hold up than what we did on that one — but I think we got out to like the 17 or something like that. And you don't know — at the end of the game they did the same thing and we let it go over our head, which they were taught to do and which they should have done. But the ball stuck right in the ground. So it's just one of those things. An oblong ball does some weird things."**

So catching that ball at the goal line wasn't a mistake by Malik Benson. **It was a designed decision** based on film study showing Kansas City's kicker sometimes outkicks his own coverage — meaning the ball travels farther than the cover team can run, which creates return space. The plan was sound, the blocking wasn't quite there, and they still escaped to the 17.

And then the honest counterpoint from the same game: later they *let one go*, which is textbook, and the football **stuck in the ground instead of bouncing into the end zone.** No touchback, bad field position, nobody's fault.

**"An oblong ball does some weird things."** Raider Nation, that's the most honest sentence in football. Half of special teams is physics you cannot control.

## THE RETURNER THEY SURVIVED — AND THE ONE COMING

**Reporter: "You had a couple of decent punt returns against you — I think they had 15 and 13. Did you see anything in coverage?"**
**DeCamillis: "Yeah, I mean, that guy's world class. That's what I saw. I'm glad we got out of that one with what we did, because that guy is a stud. I think he's averaged 14 and a half for his career, which is right up there with everybody that's top."**

A coordinator giving an opponent credit instead of blaming his own players. And a 14.5-yard career punt return average genuinely is elite — that's a man who turns every punt into a first-down conversation.

But then he turned to Sunday and the warning got specific:

**"We've got another challenge this week with Buffalo. Dortch is a guy that I played a lot against in LA when he was with the Cardinals, and now he's up there and he's done a good job. And then Davis hits a 70-yarder the other night. So they've got a really good kickoff return tandem — plus Dortch is a heck of a punt returner. And then Coach Jeff Rogers is very, very aggressive with the fakes and things like that, so you've got to be really smart there. I've got a lot of respect for him; he's done a good job in his career."**

Raider Nation, read that list again. **Greg Dortch returning punts. A kickoff man who just broke a 70-yarder. And a coordinator who loves fakes.**

That is the exact profile that has beaten us three weeks running. New England's Jeremy Springer out-chessed us. Kansas City's Dave Toub converted a fake punt against us. And now a third aggressive coordinator arrives with a better return tandem than either of them.

If this unit gives up one more game-changing special teams play, it won't be bad luck. It'll be a pattern with three data points.

## THE DOWNED PUNT, AND A PHILOSOPHY I LOVE

**Reporter: "Going back to a downed punt last week that could have pinned them at the one — how do you even practice that?"**
**DeCamillis: "We practice it quite a bit. We were doing it again today. A lot of times if the punter is not available, you use a jugs machine and give him that same ball flight. He just missed the ball flight — and you've got to catch the ball flight and not get greedy. Like you see so many times, guys are right on the two-yard line: let's get it on the one. No. I'd rather have it on the five or the six rather than the 20. So you would have liked to have cashed in on that one, and hopefully we will next time."**

**"I'd rather have it on the five or the six rather than the 20."**

Raider Nation, that is a coaching principle worth remembering, and it applies far beyond special teams. The greedy version of that play — reaching for the one-yard line instead of securing the five — risks twenty yards of field position to gain four. The expected value is terrible, and the man knows it.

Which, by the way, is the same lesson he taught himself publicly two weeks ago when he said **"I got way too greedy"** about the kickoff return decision in Kansas City. Same coordinator, same diagnosis, same word. He's consistent about his own flaw, and that's how coaches actually improve.

## WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED ON MATT GAY'S MISS

And here's the technical answer I wanted all week.

**Reporter: "The field goal operation has been so good this year — you'd made all of them until this one, and it was so close. Do you just say that happens?"**
**DeCamillis: "You analyze every kick, first of all. You try to look and see technique-wise what it looked like. It just stayed dead straight, hit that pole and stayed dead straight, on the left hash. The great thing about Matt was, I believe the next kick was on the left hash about the same distance, and he nailed it. He made the correction. That's a sign of a good kicker, and hopefully that continues. We don't want to see any more misses, but you want to be able to come back from that miss."**

So the ball **stayed dead straight** from the left hash and hit the upright. Not a shank, not a hook — a kick that never curved back.

And then the detail that tells you everything about Matt Gay: **the very next attempt came from the same hash at roughly the same distance, and he made the correction and drilled it.** That is a professional diagnosing his own miss in real time, mid-game, with no practice reps in between.

He was 10 for 10 before that. He's a man who fixed his own mechanics on the fly. That's still the most reliable asset on this roster.

## AND THE WIND TRUTH

**Reporter: "There was quite a breeze until literally the moment of the kick — and if you're not there you don't know that. But that plays into it too, doesn't it?"**
**DeCamillis: "Oh yeah, no question. A lot of times you warm up a certain way and you think the wind's going a certain way, and all of a sudden during a game it changes a little bit. That's why you've got to hit as true a ball as you can. That's the biggest key. If you have guys that have funky ball flight, it's not going to be good for him. But his ball flight has been good, and we need to keep it that way."**

Raider Nation, that's a lovely bit of insight. **The defense against wind is a true ball.** A kick with natural curve gets amplified by a gust; a kick struck dead true gets pushed but keeps its shape. You cannot control weather, so you control the strike.

And remember, this was a dome team's first outdoor game of the season. The wind was real, and so was the correction.

## THE ALEX WARD STORY

And then the moment that made me smile.

**Reporter: "An unsung guy on your special teams this year has been Alex Ward — unsung because nobody's talking about him. He is incredibly consistent. Watching the film this week, that stood out to me."**
**DeCamillis: "He's done a really good job. He had some issues at the Rams with injury situations, and he had some other issues there too. But being here the whole offseason, being able to train with Alex and those guys, getting him healthy and getting him right physically first and then mentally — you just want to try to give a guy like that confidence. He's put the work in where he's got the confidence part of it, but also you can show him positive things on film that he's done, and we just want to continue to get that done. **But please don't bring him up today.**"**

"Please don't bring him up today."

Raider Nation, that's a veteran coach refusing to jinx his long snapper four days before a game, and it's the most human thing anybody said this week.

But look at the substance underneath the joke. A specialist who had injury problems and other difficulties elsewhere came here, got a full offseason, got healthy physically first and then mentally, and is now so consistent that a reporter noticed him on film. **Physically first, then mentally.** That's a coaching staff that understands confidence is built on evidence, not pep talks — the same philosophy Kubiak described when he said he shows players film because "whatever hits the player's brain the fastest, you've got to use."

Nobody buys a jersey with a long snapper's name on it. But a clean snap is the reason Gay is 10 for 11, and now you know who's responsible.

## SO WHERE DOES WEEK SIX STAND?

Let me pull the whole thing together.

**Out:** Tonka Hemingway, which thins the interior defensive line in the week this staff declared run defense its top priority. **Not elevated:** Dante Thornton Jr. and Carter Renan, because "assignment sound" isn't finished and this staff won't lower its standard. **Questionable:** Trey Zuhn, which limits the heavy packages. **Available:** Jalen Nailor, which is the biggest addition to this offense in a month.

The guard and corner jobs are still open on purpose. The receivers getting touches has been declared "very important, very important," and the staff has taken responsibility for it. And the special teams unit knows exactly what's coming: Greg Dortch, a kickoff man with a 70-yard return on tape, and a coordinator who loves fakes.

Here's my watch list for Sunday, short and specific. **Nailor's target count.** **Who lines up at left guard on the first snap.** **The first punt of the game**, because that's where Jeff Rogers tests us. And **the first six carries**, because 15 attempts before the fourth quarter is the only proof that any of this week's talk was real.

Raider Nation, this was a week of right answers. Honest injury reporting, a standard held when it would have been easy to bend it, an explicit admission that opportunity distribution is a coaching failure, and a special teams coordinator who analyzed his own greed, his kicker's ball flight and his long snapper's confidence in the same press conference.

Now Buffalo comes to Allegiant and all of it gets graded.
