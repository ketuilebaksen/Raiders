Raider Nation, there's a number buried in Klint Kubiak's Monday press conference that almost nobody is going to report, and it changes how you should feel about this football team.

**The Raiders scored on six of their ten possessions against the Buffalo Bills.**

Six of ten. Sixty percent. Against a 5-1 team. With six receptions from the entire wide receiver room and the best tight end in football erased for two and a half quarters.

Raider Nation, **that** is what the rest of this league was afraid of when they looked at this schedule. An offense that keeps finding points no matter what you take away from it. And the only reason it isn't terrifying everybody right now is one specific failure that the head coach spent this entire press conference dissecting.

So today: the six-of-ten number and what it actually means, the red zone autopsy possession by possession, the timeout Kubiak called "unacceptable," the third-quarter problem he's now officially overhauling, a bounce-back nobody expected, a correction on Jalen Nailor's day, and his own evaluation of himself at 3-3 — which came with a laugh he couldn't explain.

## THE NUMBER: SIX OF TEN

It came out sideways, in an answer about Tyler Linderbaum of all things.

**Kubiak: "I think he's been a leader on our offense. I think he's been a major bright spot in our offense, and a huge reason why we were able to score on six of 10 possessions — because of his leadership. And for a guy that is hard on himself, that usually tells you that he cares. He is a leader and he is accountable, and that's Tyler."**

**Six of ten possessions.**

Raider Nation, let me give you the context for how good that is. An offense that scores on sixty percent of its drives is, in most weeks, a top-five offense in football. That's not "moving the ball." That's a machine.

And they did it against a team that is 5-1, in a game where Buffalo built a specific plan to erase Brock Bowers with brackets and leverage, with one wide receiver producing.

**And they still lost.** Which tells you exactly where the problem lives — and the head coach said it out loud.

## "YOU'VE GOT TO SCORE ON ALL FIVE POSSESSIONS IN THE RED ZONE"

**Reporter: "Even with the limitations in the second half and the run game, you're still figuring out ways to put points on the board. How much optimism does that bring?"**
**Kubiak: "That was a big part of our message five minutes ago. The object of the game is to score points — whether you're getting it done from the ground or from the air, or they're coming from special teams or defensive scores. It's a team game and it's about outscoring the opponent. We always want to be great in all facets, but you're going to play to your strength. So moving the ball and scoring points was good yesterday. However — what do you have to do to get over the hump? You've got to score. You've got to score on ALL FIVE possessions in the red zone, not just two of five."**

**"You've got to score on all five possessions in the red zone, not just two of five."**

Raider Nation, that's a head coach who understands his own team perfectly. The offense isn't broken. **The last twenty yards are broken.** Six scoring drives out of ten, and only two touchdowns out of five red zone trips — that's an offense being throttled at the precise moment it should be cashing in.

And notice the first part of that answer, because it's a philosophy worth hearing: **"the object of the game is to score points."** Not to establish the run for its own sake, not to win a style debate. Score, however you can, and play to your strengths. That's why the pass-heavy split in the fourth quarter was fine.

## THE RED ZONE AUTOPSY, POSSESSION BY POSSESSION

Then he walked through it, and there are two pieces of brand-new information in here.

**Kubiak: "Key fourth-and-one, we have a penalty. We dropped the ball in the red zone, in two-minute right before half. Had an opportunity to go score before half — didn't get it done. Penalties, lack of execution — it's not one thing. But mainly, when you want to be a good red zone team, you've got to run the ball well in the red zone. We didn't do that."**

So the three failures break down like this.

**One: the fourth-and-one penalty.** That's the Linderbaum false start — the third time a pre-snap penalty has killed a fourth-down attempt, the one he said "shame on me" about.

**Two — and this is new — THERE WAS A DROP IN THE RED ZONE** during the two-minute drive before halftime. Nobody had reported that. A catchable football in scoring position, on the clock, before the half, and it hit the ground.

**Three: the two-minute situation itself.** This matches what Cousins described — the clock was so tight they had to "pick and stick" rather than work a progression, and they settled for a field goal.

**And then the diagnosis that ties this entire season together: "when you want to be a good red zone team, you've got to run the ball well in the red zone. We didn't do that."**

Raider Nation, there it is. **The red zone problem and the run game problem are the same problem.**

Think about why. Inside the twenty, the field shrinks — there's no deep third to threaten, so every defender crowds the box and the throwing windows close. The only way to score reliably down there is to be able to hand the football off and gain two yards when you need two yards. This team averaged 2.1 yards a carry in New England and couldn't advance from **first-and-goal at the two** against Buffalo.

That's not a scheme failure. That's a short-yardage, physicality failure at the line of scrimmage — and it's why the run game has been the central story of this entire season on this channel.

## THE TIMEOUT HE CALLED UNACCEPTABLE

Now the admission that genuinely surprised me.

**Reporter: "You used a timeout to prevent a 12-man penalty. It's been a couple of times that's happened this year. How consequential was that, going back to the red zone? It would have been nice to have that timeout at the end of the first half."**
**Kubiak: "Yeah, that is — as a coaching staff, that's unacceptable."**

**"As a coaching staff, that's unacceptable."**

Raider Nation, follow this chain, because it's the most damning sequence of the week and nobody is talking about it.

This team burned a timeout to avoid having twelve men on the field. **And it has happened more than once this season.** Then at the end of the first half, with a chance to score before the break, the clock was so tight they had to "pick and stick" — Cousins' own words — and settled for a field goal. **And then the game ended with Cousins throwing to Tre Tucker with no timeouts left at all.**

A timeout spent on a substitution error in the first half. A rushed two-minute drive that produced three points instead of seven. A final drive with no timeouts available.

That is three points and probably more, thrown away by a personnel-grouping mistake on the sideline. And to his enormous credit, Kubiak didn't dress it up, didn't blame a signal, didn't mention the crowd. **One word: unacceptable.** Aimed at the coaching staff, which includes himself.

## THE THIRD QUARTER IS NOW AN OFFICIAL PROJECT

And here's the part where I have to say something, because this channel called this three weeks ago.

**Reporter: "When you look at the third quarters, is there any common thread causing problems?"**
**Kubiak: "Overall it's just a complete lack of production. It's really putting us in a bad spot as a team, not getting it done, getting outscored in the third quarter. So as a coaching staff we're hard at work trying to identify what are those common things and what do we need to do to put our players in better position — because yesterday was a great example of how much it can hurt you, and it did. So we're looking at our PROCESS AT HALFTIME, at what we're asking the players to do coming out in the third quarter. And that's a MAJOR POINT OF EMPHASIS."**

**"A complete lack of production."** **"Getting outscored in the third quarter."** **"We're looking at our process at halftime."**

Raider Nation, I identified this pattern after the Buffalo game and traced it back three weeks: against Kansas City the first drive after halftime ended with an erased field goal and a failed fourth down; against New England the third quarter is where the game vanished; against Buffalo it was a three-and-out. I said whatever is being said at halftime isn't translating to the first drive after it, and that it was a coaching question.

**The head coach just confirmed it is a coaching question, and told us the halftime process itself is under review.**

That is exactly the right response. Not "we need more energy." **A review of the process** — what's said, when it's said, how players are prepared in those twelve minutes, and what the opening script of the third quarter actually is.

And he was pressed on whether it's a mindset issue:

**Kubiak: "I think that has a lot to do with it. And offensively, the first play of the second half — we've got to play on. I think we have a good play versus what they called, and we didn't execute up front. So you have to get all 11 guys playing well. That's my challenge. Can't wait to attack it."**

**"We had a good play versus what they called and we didn't execute up front."** The call was right. The block wasn't. That's a precise, honest answer — and "can't wait to attack it" is a man who sounds energized rather than defeated.

He was also asked whether defenses are specifically ramping up pressure on Cousins in second halves.

**Kubiak: "I'd have to look at that more. Obviously teams make adjustments, and it's no excuse for us not to protect — especially on that first play. We've got to have the guys ready to play. And like I said earlier, I'm really excited about the challenge of improving that."**

"It's no excuse for us not to protect." Again — no alibi.

## THE ACCOUNTABILITY PHILOSOPHY — AND IT ANSWERS THE SNAP QUESTION

Now my favorite exchange, because a reporter asked the question I've been asking on this channel for a week.

**Reporter: "When you're building for the long term as well as this year — it's one thing when young players make mistakes, but when veterans, guys you paid as free agents, make those mistakes, how is that exacerbated in the frustration level?"**
**Kubiak: "Well, this is the least perfect game in the free world. Everybody makes mistakes — coaches and players, veterans and rookies. So it's really identifying who are your guys that you can count on, and that's a BODY OF WORK. And the more accountable players that we can have, that we learn to trust — those are the guys that get more playing time and get more opportunities. So that's coaching, too. The more you learn about your coaching staff and yourself, you put different guys in different leadership roles. But I don't expect our guys to be perfect. It's a full-speed, four-quarter game with bodies flying everywhere. But it's really the guys that are accountable, ESPECIALLY IN THE TOUGHEST MOMENTS — those are the guys that you rely heavily on."**

Raider Nation, read that again with Kwity Paye and Malcolm Koonce in mind.

**"The more accountable players that we can have, that we learn to trust — those are the guys that get more playing time and get more opportunities."**

That is the principle I argued for two days ago, stated by the head coach himself: **snaps follow trust, and trust is built by a body of work.** He didn't name anybody. He didn't have to. A $48 million edge rusher with no sacks in four games and a teammate who leads the team in sacks on fewer snaps are both inside that sentence.

And **"this is the least perfect game in the free world"** is a lovely line. He's refusing to pretend mistakes are unusual. What he's measuring instead is **who is accountable in the toughest moments** — which is the same thing he praised Adam Butler for after New England.

## THE OFFENSIVE LINE UPDATE — AND A REAL BOUNCE-BACK

Two important pieces here, starting with the man who started in place of Jackson Powers-Johnson.

**Reporter: "After watching the film, what did you like about Caleb Rogers' game?"**
**Kubiak: "Caleb was prepared. There's some good and bad. I see him as a young player that's continuing to improve."**

That's a measured grade — not a coronation, not a benching. "Prepared" is the key word, because that's exactly what the open competition was designed to produce.

And then the one I genuinely did not expect.

**Reporter: "Watching the film, Spencer Burford was clearly much better than the week before and played well. Could you discuss him specifically?"**
**Kubiak: "Spencer has been a really great addition for our team. He's a guy that's continuing to improve. Overall as an offensive line, I really like what we're doing protecting the quarterback. I like how our quarterback's getting the ball out on time. And Spencer and Tyler and Kolton and DJ and Caleb — the whole offensive line knows that we have to improve in the run game blocking if we want to get to where we want to get."**

Raider Nation, I owe you this one straight. **Nine days ago I told you Spencer Burford had allowed 23 pressures — the most of any left guard in the NFL — and that a change needed to be made.** I stand by that analysis of the first five games.

**And the man bounced back.** A reporter who watched the film said he was clearly much better and played well, and the head coach called him a great addition who keeps improving.

That's what accountability looks like from a player under public pressure, and it deserves to be said as loudly as the criticism was. **Protecting the quarterback: this coach likes what they're doing.** The run blocking is the acknowledged gap — "we have to improve in the run game blocking if we want to get where we want to get" — and that's consistent with every number we have: first in pass block success, last in yards per carry.

Asked how much of a work in progress that is:

**Kubiak: "Whether you're undefeated or you've lost them all, every team is a work in progress. It's a long season with people moving in and out of the lineup. I like where we're headed. I like the potential that we have, and I like how hard our backs are working."**

## WASHINGTON JR. — THE NUMBERS ARE NOW UNDENIABLE

Speaking of the backs, here's the stat from Sunday that settles a two-week argument.

**Reporter: "Washington Jr. had nearly double the rushing yards despite having half the carries. Do you think defenses are doing something schematically against Ashton versus Mike, or is there another reason?"**
**Kubiak: "I think we have to get production from all three of our backs. Mike Washington did some good stuff yesterday. And overall I thought our backs ran harder yesterday. I thought they broke tackles. I thought they moved the line of scrimmage when the things weren't there. I thought Coach Omar did a really good job of challenging our guys. I thought they had one of their better games, and I expect that to continue to improve."**

**Nearly double the yards on half the carries.**

Raider Nation, that's the clearest possible vindication of what I argued after the New England film — when a back averaging five yards a carry gets one attempt while others get four and ten, you're leaving production on the table. He got his chance, and he doubled the output on half the work.

And look at the three verbs Kubiak used about the whole group: **ran harder, broke tackles, moved the line of scrimmage.** Those are effort words, not scheme words. Combined with the credit to Coach Omar for challenging them, that's a position coach getting a response out of his room in one week.

"We have to get production from all three of our backs" is now the official policy. Jeanty, Washington, Laube.

## THE NAILOR CORRECTION I OWE YOU

And here's a place where I have to update something, because the public narrative was wrong and the coach corrected it.

The question was about Nailor's first game back from the concussion and his lack of targets.

**Kubiak: "Well, he HAD targets. He got called on it — we threw a double move to him and they had a penalty on him. He caught a choice route. I thought Jalen, it was great to have him back. You could FEEL HIS PRESENCE out there, and we've just got to keep finding ways to get him the ball, because he's a really good player. He's a player that I'm counting on, that we're counting on, that's going to continue to ascend."**

Raider Nation, this matters.

**They threw a double move to Jalen Nailor and drew a penalty on the defender.** That's not an absence of targets — that's a target that produced free yards and an automatic first down, which never shows up in a receiving line. And **he caught a choice route.**

So the "six receptions, five from Tucker" picture is still the story of this receiver room — I'm not walking that back. But the version where Nailor was invisible isn't accurate. He drew a flag on a double move and converted a choice route in his first game back from concussion protocol.

**"You could feel his presence out there."** That's the spacing argument again, and this time there's a penalty to prove it.

## AND HE AGREED WITH THE VERTICAL SOLUTION

Then came the exchange I've been waiting six weeks for.

**Reporter: "After the game, Kirk talked about him and Tre building trust. Do you think going vertical more earlier in the game could help in getting teams from stacking the box?"**
**Kubiak: "Yeah, Tre's shown that he can really stress a defense. And yeah, I agree. Anytime that you can push the ball down the field, it's going to open things up. So yeah, I agree with you."**

**"I agree with you."**

Raider Nation, this is the thesis of this entire channel for a month, confirmed by the head coach. The Raiders have faced the second-most stacked boxes in football because defenses weren't afraid of the pass. The cure is making them afraid — and Tre Tucker, with his speed, is the one player on this roster who can force a safety to stay deep.

**Push the ball down the field early, and the box empties.** Not in the fourth quarter when you're chasing. Early, when it changes the math for the whole afternoon.

He has now agreed with the vertical shot, agreed with slants and rub concepts against man coverage, admitted he abandoned the run too early, admitted he didn't involve Mayer enough, and admitted the halftime process needs review. **That's five specific offensive corrections, all publicly acknowledged.** Now they have to appear on a call sheet.

## LINDERBAUM, PORTER, AND THE YOUNG SECONDARY

Three human moments that I loved.

On Tyler Linderbaum being publicly critical of his own performance: **"For a guy that is hard on himself — that usually tells you that he cares. He is a leader and he is accountable, and that's Tyler."** A center who rips himself after a three-point loss, defended by his coach as proof of caring. That's a healthy building.

On Darien Porter's recent form:

**Kubiak: "He's a competitor. He's got really thick skin. He's a guy that has been picked on by the media AND MYSELF in April — and he got benched. And now he's back playing, and he's got thick skin. I like him, man. I like what he's about."**

Raider Nation, that's an extraordinary thing for a head coach to say. He admitted he personally criticized a player in April, confirmed the player was benched, and is now publicly praising his resilience. **"I like him, man."** That's not coach-speak — that's a man telling you he respects somebody who took a demotion and fought back.

And the advice to a very young secondary — Hezekiah Masses, Darien Porter, Jermod McCoy — from a coach who played defensive back himself:

**"The best advice you can give them is you just keep playing. Especially as a defensive back, you're going to get beat. All right? When you get beat, everybody in the free world is going to know it, because it's on TV. When a defensive lineman misses his gap — they're going to show the guy that missed the tackle. So you've got to have thick skin. You've got to move on to the next play. So you just keep playing, keep competing."**

That's the truest thing anybody said this week. **A corner's mistakes are broadcast; a lineman's are invisible.** And after Masses gave up two touchdowns in Kansas City and this group got attacked over the top in New England, "keep playing, keep competing" is exactly the message a 22-year-old needs.

## THE DEFENSE, HONESTLY GRADED

On the pass rush and coverage working together against Buffalo:

**Kubiak: "We got to the quarterback twice, got two sacks. Number 17 is one of the best in the free world at extending plays. I think he only had five yards rushing, but he definitely made plays with his legs — moving and throwing off schedule. Overall, if you're going to win the game, it's about limiting points. And we have to limit points in the red zone."**

Two sacks, and Josh Allen held to five rushing yards — which matches JJ Watt's praise of the containment plan. But the honest caveat is right: Allen still created with his legs by **moving and throwing off schedule**, which is the hardest thing in football to defend.

And the defensive conclusion is the mirror image of the offensive one: **limit points in the red zone.** Both units lost this game in the same twenty yards.

## HOW HE EVALUATES HIMSELF AT 3-3

And then the moment of the press conference.

**Reporter: "Now sitting at 3-3, how do you evaluate yourself as a first-year head coach through six weeks?"**
**Kubiak: "I don't know why I'm laughing. I think it's like an uncomfortable laugh right there, because I just always think about our team. I'm evaluating our team — and one person as a coach, one player that's playing good or bad, is irrelevant. So how would I evaluate our team right now? I evaluate us as a 3-3 team. YOU ARE WHAT YOUR RECORD SAYS YOU ARE. We have everything in front of us. We have a really good football team coming into our place this week. And like I just told the guys — everything's in front of us. So what's our decision going to be? Is it going to be, 'oh, here we go again'? Or is it going to be, 'let's attack this next game and put ourselves in position'? And that's what I love about these guys. These guys aren't down. Yeah, we were down yesterday — but not today. Today we're getting better. We're making corrections. We know all the good things that we can do. We know what's ahead of us and what we can accomplish as a team. And there's a lot of positivity in that. And that's where I'm at right now."**

Raider Nation, there's a lot in there.

**"You are what your record says you are."** That's a famous coaching line and it's the correct answer — no excuses about three-point margins, no citing the quality of opponents. 3-3. Period.

**"I don't know why I'm laughing... it's an uncomfortable laugh."** He deflected a question about himself toward his team, and he was honest about being uncomfortable doing it. I believe him.

And then the one that matters most: **"So what's our decision going to be? Is it going to be 'oh, here we go again' — or is it going to be 'let's attack this next game'?"**

**"Here we go again."** He said it out loud. That's the ghost that haunts this franchise — two decades of seasons that slid away in October. A first-year head coach just named it in front of reporters and framed the week as a choice between that ghost and the alternative.

**"Yeah, we were down yesterday — but not today."**

## SO WHERE DOES THIS LEAVE US?

Let me put it all together, because this was the most informative press conference of the season.

**What's working:** scoring on six of ten possessions against a 5-1 team. Pass protection the head coach genuinely likes. A quarterback getting the ball out on time. Backs who ran harder, broke tackles and moved the line of scrimmage. Burford bouncing back from the worst performance of his season. Porter earning praise after being benched in April. Nailor drawing a penalty on a double move in his first game back.

**What lost the game:** two of five in the red zone, with a drop, a fourth-down penalty and a rushed two-minute drive. A timeout burned on a twelve-men-on-the-field error that this team has committed more than once. A third quarter with "a complete lack of production." And a run game that cannot convert first-and-goal from the two.

**And here's the honest summary, Raider Nation.** This offense is doing exactly what the rest of the league feared — it moves the ball on anybody and scores on sixty percent of its possessions. It is being beaten by the last twenty yards of the field and the first five minutes after halftime. Both of those are correctable. Neither requires a trade.

**My watch list against the Rams:** red zone touchdown rate. The first play of the third quarter. Washington Jr.'s carry count. And whether there's a vertical shot to Tre Tucker in the first quarter instead of the fourth.
