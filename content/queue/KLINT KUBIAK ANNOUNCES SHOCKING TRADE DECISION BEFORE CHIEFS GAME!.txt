WHAT CESAR RUIZ ACTUALLY FIXES

Let me get specific, because "offensive line help" is a phrase that hides all the important detail.

Klint Kubiak's offense is built on three things: running the football out of heavy personnel, play action off that run game, and quick timing throws from the pocket. Every single one of those depends on the interior of the line.

Here's why. On a wide zone run, the guard has to get movement at the point of attack and then climb to the second level to meet a linebacker. If he gets stalemated, the back has nowhere to cut and you get what we watched at SoFi: 21 carries for 48 yards. On play action, the interior has to sell the run while absorbing a defensive tackle who knows it's a pass. And on a timing throw, interior pressure is the one thing a quarterback cannot escape. An edge rusher beating a tackle gives you an escape lane. A defensive tackle in your lap at two seconds gives you nothing.

That's the Raiders' problem in one paragraph. The edges have been fine. Kolton Miller had a rough afternoon in New Orleans, and DJ Glaze is still learning, but the real damage has come up the middle.

So what does Ruiz bring? Experience at both guard and center, which matters enormously in an offense that uses motion and multiple personnel groups, because a lineman who can slide inside solves two injuries at once. He brings power in the run game, which is the exact trait a two-back offense needs. And he brings something you can't coach: starts. Real NFL starts, against real interior rushers, in a scheme that asked him to climb and reach.

Put him next to Tyler Linderbaum, the center this front office gave three years and $81 million to, and suddenly the middle of this line has two players a defensive coordinator has to respect instead of one.

THE RIPPLE EFFECT NOBODY TALKS ABOUT

And here's the part I really want you to understand, because it's why I think this move matters more than any receiver trade.

Fix the interior and four separate problems improve at the same time.

First, Jeanty's numbers. A back averaging 3.3 yards a carry behind a line getting pushed backwards is not a bad running back, he's a running back with nowhere to go. The sixth pick of the 2025 draft has the vision and the contact balance. He needs two yards of daylight before the first contact, and that's a blocking job.

Second, the play action game, which is the heart of this offense. Play action only works if the defense believes the run. The defense only believes the run if the run has been hurting them.

Third, Cousins. He's been excellent at getting the ball out on time, and part of the reason some of you want more deep shots is that deep shots take longer and this pocket hasn't been reliable enough to hold them. Give him a clean interior and those twenty-yard throws to Tre Tucker become available, not just theoretical.

Fourth, the fourth quarter. This defense has not allowed a single point in a fourth quarter all season, which is a franchise-record-matching streak to start a year. The best way to protect that is to hold the ball on offense, and the only way to hold the ball is to run it on first and second down.

One guard. Four fixes. That's how trench investments work, and it's why Kubiak said at the podium this week that to be a good team, you have to be really good in the trenches.

THE HONEST CAVEAT

Now, the part I always give you. A lineman walking into a new building in Week Four doesn't get to be perfect immediately, and offensive line play is the most communication-dependent job on the field. Protection calls, slide rules, who takes the looping end, who climbs and who stays: all of that is built over months of practice, not days.

So don't expect Ruiz to erase this problem on Sunday against Kansas City. The realistic version is a package of snaps that grows every week, and a line that looks noticeably better by the time this team gets to the New England, Buffalo and Rams stretch. That's still exactly what you want, because the games that decide a season are in November and December, not this weekend.

And understand what the move says about the front office. John Spytek has spent all year identifying weaknesses early and fixing them cheaply, from Foley Fatukasi on a veteran minimum deal to Nolan Smith joining the edge rotation. Now he's reinforced the one spot everybody could see on tape. That's an organization that watches its own film honestly, which is the rarest quality in this business.

NOW: THE FIVE DECISIONS THAT BUILT THIS 3-0

Okay. Part two of this episode, and I want to tell this story differently than everybody else.

Everybody is covering this 3-0 start as a stat sheet: nine takeaways, 29.3 points a game, Cousins' nine touchdowns. Those numbers are real and we went through them in our last episode. But numbers are the result. Today I want to walk through the five decisions that produced them, because this team didn't get lucky. It got built.

DECISION ONE: THE TRADE THEY DIDN'T COMPLETE

The first decision wasn't even made by the Raiders. It was made by a doctor in Baltimore.

On March 6th, Las Vegas agreed to send Maxx Crosby to the Ravens for the 14th pick in the 2026 draft and a 2027 first-round pick. That's not a rumor, that's an agreement. Crosby flew to Baltimore. He posted that he was in town. He took a physical and an MRI on March 10th.

That night, Baltimore backed out over his left knee, which had been surgically repaired in January to fix a torn meniscus. Crosby flew home on a private plane. A deal that looked finished on Friday was dead by Tuesday night.

Now look at what that non-decision produced. Crosby has drawn more double teams than any player in the NFL this season. He has 17 pressures and two sacks through three games despite that. And because protections slide toward him, this defense ranks second in the league in pressure rate at 36.3 percent while blitzing on only 19.5 percent of dropbacks.

That's the entire architecture of this team. Kwity Paye's two-sack debut happens because of Crosby. Malcolm Koonce's strip sack in New Orleans happens because of Crosby. Hezekiah Masses' interceptions happen because quarterbacks are throwing uncomfortable. And now Nolan Smith joins a rotation that already works because one man occupies two blockers.

Two first-round picks would have accelerated a rebuild. Keeping Crosby appears to have ended one. His own description of the spring was simply that it had been a long road to recovery.

DECISION TWO: HIRING TWO FIRST-TIMERS AT ONCE

The second decision was the riskiest thing this organization did, and nobody talks about it that way.

Klint Kubiak was named head coach on February 9th, with general manager John Spytek and minority owner Tom Brady leading the search. He had spent 2025 calling plays for Seattle and had just won Super Bowl 60. He is 39 years old and had never been a head coach.

Then Kubiak's first major move was to promote defensive line coach Rob Leonard to defensive coordinator: 41 years old, also never having held that job, running a system that borrows heavily from what Mike Macdonald built in Seattle.

Think about the risk there. A first-time head coach and a first-time coordinator, installing new systems on both sides of the ball, with a roster that had just lost 14 games. The conventional wisdom says that takes half a season to come together. Players need to learn a language. Calls need repetition. New defenses give up big plays in September while everybody figures out their assignments.

Instead, this defense looked organized from the first snap against Miami, where the Dolphins converted two of ten third downs. And three weeks later it leads the league in takeaways and hasn't allowed a point in any fourth quarter.

Two first-year coaches with one shared language, and a roster that bought in immediately. That's the decision that made the other four work.

DECISION THREE: THE PATIENCE AT QUARTERBACK

The third decision is the one the entire league questioned, and it has aged better than any of them.

Las Vegas held the first overall pick and used it on Fernando Mendoza, the Heisman winner who led Indiana to an undefeated national title. Then they signed a 38-year-old quarterback in April, Kirk Cousins, fresh off two seasons in Atlanta, and told everyone the veteran would start while the rookie learned.

For seven or eight years now, every quarterback taken first overall has started almost immediately. This was the opposite of the league's instinct.

And look at the results. Cousins has completed 67.4 percent of his throws for 661 yards with nine touchdowns and three interceptions, and a 107.2 passer rating. He's tied for the league lead in touchdown passes. He became the first quarterback in NFL history to throw three touchdown passes in each of his first three games with a new team. Kubiak told the Associated Press after New Orleans that Cousins has the experience, has the maturity, and is making plays.

Part of it is fit: Cousins ran a version of this offense with Kubiak in Minnesota, which is why there was no installation period.

And the other half of the decision is what's happening on the sideline, where Mendoza wears his helmet during games so he can hear the play call going into Cousins, then recites it back to the quarterbacks coach and talks through what he's seeing from the defense. Every drive of a 3-0 start is a live classroom for the franchise's future.

The counterargument is honest and it grows weekly: the longer Cousins wins, the harder it becomes to ever answer when Mendoza plays. But that is the best possible version of this problem, and every team in the league would trade for it.

DECISION FOUR: SPENDING THE MONEY ON SPEED AND THE MIDDLE

The fourth decision was about where the money went, and it tells you exactly what this staff believes in.

Nakobe Dean signed for three years and $36 million. Quay Walker arrived from Green Bay. The former Georgia teammates now lead this team with 27 and 24 tackles, and Dean wears the green dot and relays every call. Watch the tape and you'll see what that buys: nobody looking around before the snap, nobody hesitating, everybody reacting. A defense that communicates plays faster than its talent level.

Kwity Paye debuted with two sacks and eight pressures against Miami, which made him the first Raider other than Crosby to post eight pressures in a game since Malcolm Koonce on Christmas Day 2023. That's a nearly three-year gap. One signing closed it.

And on offense, the money went to the middle: center Tyler Linderbaum at three years and $81 million.

Here's the thread connecting all of it. They didn't spend on a marquee receiver. They spent on the center of the offensive line, the center of the defense, and a second pass rusher. The least glamorous positions on a football field. And the result was an offense that went from 14.2 points a game, dead last in the NFL, to 29.3, and a defense that went from 16 takeaways in 17 games to nine in three.

The Cesar Ruiz addition is the same philosophy, applied again, to the one spot where the plan hasn't fully worked yet.

DECISION FIVE: TRUSTING YOUNG PLAYERS IMMEDIATELY

The fifth decision shows up every Sunday: this coaching staff plays its young players and lets them make mistakes.

Pick 175, Hezekiah Masses, a cornerback out of California, picked off Justin Herbert twice at SoFi, becoming the first Raiders rookie with a multi-interception game since Henry Williams in 1979, and won AFC Defensive Player of the Week. He has a third interception now and is tied with Jevon Holland of the Giants for the league lead. One hundred and seventy-four players were drafted ahead of him, and this week the head coach stood at a podium and called him "our starting corner" with no qualifiers, even with Darien Porter healthy.

Pick 122, Mike Washington Jr. out of Arkansas, has 102 yards on 15 carries, 6.8 a pop, including the 36-yard touchdown run that swung the New Orleans game right after the Saints had scored. Kubiak says he's earned more opportunities.

Pick 38, Trayden Stokes, intercepted a pass in his debut, missed Week Three with a concussion, and has now cleared protocol for Sunday.

And Cody White, who wasn't drafted by anybody this year and started this season on the practice squad, has three touchdowns on three catches and has now been promoted to the 53-man roster with Jack Bech on injured reserve.

Here's the lesson. A losing organization protects itself by playing veterans who won't embarrass anybody. A building organization plays the kid, accepts the growing pains, and finds out what it has. Las Vegas is doing the second one while winning, which almost never happens at the same time.

THE THREE QUESTIONS KANSAS CITY WILL ASK

Now let me flip it, because a good coaching staff looks at our 3-0 and sees three specific things to attack. Here's what I expect Andy Reid's staff to do on Sunday.

Question one: can they run the ball on us? New Orleans put up 148 rushing yards. Kenneth Walker has 65 carries for 360 yards with 19 first-down runs and four touchdowns, and he's playing like a top-five back. If Kansas City controls the line of scrimmage, our safeties come down, and that's when the deep middle opens up.

Question two: can they make us stay patient? The Saints went four-for-four in the red zone and this defense didn't force a turnover until the fourth quarter. A team that bends for three quarters and then takes the ball away four times has a real identity, but it also has a real vulnerability: if the opponent doesn't give the ball back, the comeback never happens.

Question three: who do we have to cover besides Brock Bowers? No Raiders wide receiver averages even four catches a game. Bech is on IR. Bowers just caught ten passes for 116 yards and won Offensive Player of the Week, and the entire week of Chiefs game planning will be about taking the middle of the field away from him. The answer has to come from Tucker outside, Cody White on third down, Michael Mayer in two-tight-end sets, and the backs in the passing game.

That's the exam. And I'd rather have the exam at home, at 3-0, with the building wearing black.

THE TWIST THAT MAKES SUNDAY PERSONAL

One more thing, and it's my favorite subplot in football this week.

Kenneth Walker was Klint Kubiak's running back in Seattle last season, on a team that won Super Bowl 60. Kubiak spent an entire year designing ways to get that man the football in space, including on the biggest stage the sport has.

Now he has to take it away from him.

That's why the word out of the building is that the top target on the whiteboard isn't Patrick Mahomes, it's Walker. And it's why I keep saying this Raiders staff has an edge you can't find on a stat sheet: knowing a player from coaching him is different from knowing him from tape.
