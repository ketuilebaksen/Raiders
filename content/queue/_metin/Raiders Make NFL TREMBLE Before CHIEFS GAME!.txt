IS THIS TEAM ACTUALLY DIFFERENT? Six Things That Have to Happen Against Kansas City, and the Answer We Get on Sunday 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, we are days away from the question that has followed this team since Week One.

Is this team actually different?

Not "are they 3-0," because we know they are. Not "is Kirk Cousins playing well," because he is, with nine touchdown passes and no interceptions in the last two weeks. The real question is whether what we've watched for three weeks is a team that changed, or a hot start that the schedule is about to correct.

Kansas City comes to Allegiant Stadium on Sunday with both teams undefeated and sole possession of first place in the AFC West on the line. That's not a measuring stick. That's the whole exam.

So today I'm giving you six specific things that have to happen for the Raiders to win that game, and at the end I'll tell you what I actually expect. No cheerleading, no doom. Just the checklist.

I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. Hit that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we build toward 6,000 believers. Let's work.

KEY ONE: SOMEBODY HAS TO MAKE THEM PAY FOR RESPECTING CROSBY

Start here, because it's the foundation of this entire defense.

Every opponent slides protection toward Maxx Crosby. That's a fact of life. The question every week is whether somebody else cashes in on the attention he draws, and in New Orleans the answer was emphatic. Malcolm Koonce may have been the best defensive player on the field: a strip sack that flipped the game, a tackle for loss, three quarterback hits, and pressure on several other snaps.

That's the whole theory of this front working exactly as designed. If offenses are going to protect against 98, Koonce has to make them regret it.

Now apply it to Sunday. Kansas City's protection will absolutely account for Crosby, and they'll do it with more sophistication than most. So the Koonce matchup, the Kwity Paye matchup, the interior rush from Foley Fatukasi and Adam Butler, those are the snaps that decide this game. One free rusher against the best quarterback of this era is worth more than any coverage call on the whiteboard.

KEY TWO: BOWERS HAS TO STAY UNCOVERABLE

Brock Bowers returned from a knee procedure and caught ten passes for 116 yards and a touchdown. He was named AFC Offensive Player of the Week, with 13 targets and seven first downs by himself.

The play that mattered most was the third-down back-shoulder throw from Cousins. That isn't scheme. That's two players trusting each other, and Cousins told us at the podium that the chemistry is still building, comparing it to how long it took him to fully understand what he had in Justin Jefferson.

Here's why it matters more this week than last. Jack Bech is on injured reserve with a fractured forearm, and Bech was the player who occupied the defender who would otherwise help on Bowers. Without him, Kansas City can put more attention on the tight end.

So the question becomes: can Bowers win anyway, and if they take him away, who eats? Which brings us to the third key.

KEY THREE: THE UNEXPECTED NAMES HAVE TO KEEP SHOWING UP

This is the single most encouraging thing about this 3-0 start, and it's the thing that has to survive contact with a better opponent.

Cody White has been elevated, then promoted to the 53-man roster, and he has three touchdowns on three catches this season, scoring in each of the last two games. Mike Washington Jr. ripped off a 36-yard touchdown run at the exact moment New Orleans had seized the momentum, and Kubiak said he's "definitely earned more opportunities." Michael Mayer answered the question about whether he's more than an elite blocker with three catches, 32 yards and a touchdown. Hezekiah Masses has three interceptions in three games as a fifth-round rookie, and the head coach has publicly called him "our starting corner" even with Darien Porter healthy.

That's four players nobody outside this building was counting on in August. And there are two more coming: Carter Renan and fourth-round rookie Dante Thornton Jr. are both on short-term injured reserve and eligible to return around Week Five, which means this receiver room gets reinforcements right as the schedule gets heavy. Thornton is the one to remember, at 6-foot-5 with a 4.35 forty.

Against the Chiefs, that depth is the difference between a competitive loss and a win. Kansas City can take away your best option. What they can't do is take away your fifth option, and those are the snaps where this game gets decided.

KEY FOUR: FIX THE LEFT SIDE OF THE OFFENSIVE LINE

Now the honest part, because 3-0 does not mean clean.

Kolton Miller gave up a sack on a play that almost became a game-changing fumble by Cousins, and Connor Hayward being in the right place is the only reason it didn't turn into a disaster. He allowed several pressures and looked shaky for stretches. Spencer Burford could have been flagged for holding multiple times, and his play was one of the reasons this team couldn't run the football on the left side.

And now Jackson Powers-Johnson missed practice with a groin injury, which has already pushed Caleb Rogers into the conversation as an option if the guards keep struggling.

That's why Ashton Jeanty is averaging barely three yards a carry. Yards for a running back are a product of what's happening in front of him as much as what he does with the ball, and the head coach said it plainly this week: to be a good team, you have to be really good in the trenches.

Against Kansas City, a leaky interior is how a 3-0 season gets humbled. This is the key I'm least confident about, and it's the reason I keep saying that if John Spytek makes one move before November 10th, it belongs on the interior of this line and not on another receiver.

KEY FIVE: NO FREE POINTS

Here's the quiet reason the Saints game was close at all.

Dylan Laube's special teams fumble was a major mistake, and New Orleans turned it directly into points and the lead. That's the kind of error that gets forgiven when you force four turnovers in the fourth quarter, and it is absolutely not the kind of error that gets forgiven by Patrick Mahomes.

Same with the coverage breakdown that produced the Saints' first touchdown, when Isaiah Pola-Mao missed his assignment and ended up roughly ten yards off at the goal line. That one has a fix: Trayden Stokes has cleared the concussion protocol and was a full practice participant, so the center fielder is back and the middle of the field gets its range and its communication back.

But the general principle holds for Sunday. Against a team this good, you cannot donate points. No special teams turnovers, no busted coverages, no twelve men on the field coming out of a timeout. Play clean, or the margin disappears.

KEY SIX: STOP THE RUN, EVEN THOUGH MAHOMES IS THE NAME

And the sixth key is the one that surprised me when it came out of the building this week: the top target on the Raiders' whiteboard isn't Mahomes. It's running back Kenneth Walker.

The reasoning is sound. Walker has 65 carries for 360 yards, 19 runs that produced first downs, 440 total yards from scrimmage and four touchdowns. He's playing like a top-five back. And a back that good pulls safeties toward the box, which opens the deep middle, which is exactly where Mahomes turns a game into a highlight package.

Take Walker away and you force Kansas City to beat you with the thing you're actually built to defend: throws against a four-man rush that gets home without blitzing.

And the Raiders have an edge here you won't find on a depth chart. Klint Kubiak was Seattle's offensive coordinator last season, when Walker was his running back. He knows how Walker cuts, how he reads a hole, what he does tired and what he does fresh. That's not film study. That's firsthand.

THE HIDDEN KEY: WHAT COUSINS HAS TO DO DIFFERENTLY

There's one more thing I want to flag, and it isn't a criticism so much as a warning about matchups.

Cousins has been excellent at what this offense asks: efficient, accurate, protecting the football, and lethal on third down to Bowers. Nine touchdowns and the league lead through three weeks. But he also hasn't pushed the ball downfield as often as some of you want, and that's been fine against teams that couldn't make him pay for it.

Kansas City can. A defense with a week to prepare for a short, timing-based passing game will sit on those routes, squeeze the windows, and dare a 38-year-old quarterback to throw over the top. We already know he can: eight of twelve for 181 yards and three touchdowns on throws of ten or more air yards at SoFi, after going one for seven in Week One.

So the hidden key is this. At some point on Sunday, probably in the second half, this offense is going to need two or three throws past the sticks to loosen things up, most likely to Tre Tucker, who ran past an NFL secondary for a 40-yard score two weeks ago. If those throws come and connect, the whole underneath game opens back up. If they never come, Kansas City gets to defend fifteen yards of grass all afternoon.

SO WHAT DO I ACTUALLY EXPECT?

Let me be straight with you.

Kansas City is the standard in this division and has been for a decade. Andy Reid has been beating good game plans since before some of you were born, and sometimes great players are just great, which is why Mahomes is Mahomes. If you're looking for me to guarantee a win, I'm not your guy.

But look at the variables lining up. The Raiders get Stokes back for the exact weakness that got exposed last week. They get Bowers in his second game, with more chemistry than he had in the Superdome. They have a head coach with inside knowledge of the opposing team's most important skill player. They have a defense that leads the league in pressure rate without blitzing and is tied for the league lead in sacks and forced fumbles. They're at home, with a fan base being asked for a blackout, and with visiting players having reportedly said this building isn't as loud as advertised.

That's a lot pointing one direction. The things pointing the other way are the interior offensive line and three yards a carry.

So here's my honest answer to the question in the title: I think this team is genuinely different, and I think Sunday will be close either way. If the Raiders win the line of scrimmage, they win the game. If they don't, Kansas City will make them pay for every extra second Mahomes gets.

My prediction: Raiders 27, Chiefs 24, and I want 65,000 people screaming at the top of their lungs when Kansas City has the ball on third down.

WHAT A WIN WOULD ACTUALLY MEAN

Let me finish by putting the stakes where they belong, because I don't want anybody missing how big this is.

This franchise is 3-0 for the first time since 2021, and 2021 was the last season it reached the playoffs. Three games into this season, the Raiders have already matched their entire win total from last year. They're the only team in the common draft era to start 3-0 after taking the first overall pick without that pick playing a single snap.

Beat Kansas City and all of it changes shape. They'd be 4-0, in sole possession of first place in the AFC West, with the national media out of excuses. Every "yeah, but" dies in one afternoon: Miami was rebuilding, the Chargers are a mess, the Saints start slow, fine. You cannot explain away beating the Chiefs at home while undefeated.

And if they lose? They're 3-1, still in first or tied for it, with a young roster that just went twelve rounds with the standard of the conference. That's not a disaster either. It's a measurement.

That's what makes Sunday so much fun. For the first time in five years, a Raiders game in October actually means something.
