## FIRST, LET ME BE STRAIGHT WITH YOU ABOUT WHAT HAS AND HASN'T HAPPENED

Before we go anywhere, two sentences of honesty, because that's the deal on this channel.

**John Spytek has not signed a trade.** Nobody has been acquired. If you clicked expecting a done deal, I'm not going to pretend one exists.

What HAS happened is this: a 2nd-round price tag just got attached to a Pro Bowl receiver with Las Vegas named as the destination, two brand-new names entered the market from the two best-connected reporters in football, and the head coach publicly blocked the activation of the one receiver this roster was counting on — with three specific conditions attached.

That's the shock. Not a signature. A market that just got loud, a standard that just got enforced, and a general manager who is about to be judged on what he does in the next three weeks.

## KUBIAK IS NOT LOWERING THE BAR

Here's the headline, and I want to make sure the framing is exactly right.

**Klint Kubiak is refusing to activate Dante Thornton Jr. simply because the Raiders desperately need help at wide receiver.**

Let's review the situation. Las Vegas has been without Jack Bech since he fractured his left forearm during that Week Three victory in New Orleans, and Thornton looked like the natural candidate to replace some of that missing production. But he suffered a hamstring injury during training camp, opened the season on injured reserve, and has not appeared in a single game in 2026.

He's back at practice now, and he has made it clear he wants to contribute — explaining that he loves football and is excited about the possibility of helping his teammates.

Kubiak, however, established a much higher standard than simply completing practices.

**He said Thornton must demonstrate that he is physically AND mentally prepared to participate in an NFL game.**

## THE THREE REQUIREMENTS

And he got specific, which is what makes this worth a whole segment.

Kubiak identified three requirements. **Thornton must maintain his conditioning, understand his assignments, and make plays when opportunities appear.**

The coach believes the receiver's conditioning has already reached the necessary level — that box is checked. But Thornton missed significant time after receiving limited work during training camp, and according to Kubiak, he "proved some things last week" and will have another opportunity to prove more this week.

**His return could still be delayed until the following week.**

Raider Nation, read requirement number two again: *understand his assignments.* That's the one that matters, and it's the honest reason this is taking time. A receiver who runs a crisp route to the wrong depth on a scramble drill doesn't just fail to help — he produces an interception. A receiver who doesn't know the protection adjustment on a slide gets his quarterback hit. Conditioning is the easy part. The playbook is the job.

## THE FAN DIVISION — AND WHERE I LAND

Now, this approach has genuinely divided this fan base, and I want to represent both sides honestly.

**One group believes Kubiak and John Spytek may not be fully committed to Thornton because he was selected while Pete Carroll was leading this organization.** The front-office-politics theory: he's not "their guy," so he's being slow-walked.

**The other group appreciates that this staff is requiring the young receiver to earn his role instead of allowing the team's depth problems to lower its standards.**

And I'm firmly in the second camp, for one simple reason: there is no evidence for the first one. Kubiak did not rule Thornton out for the season, and he did not suggest the Raiders have lost confidence in him. He simply refused to guarantee activation before the player proves he can execute consistently.

If this staff wanted him buried, they wouldn't have brought him back to practice and they wouldn't be publicly saying he proved things last week. "Prove it and you'll play" is not a grudge. It's a standard.

## AND THIS STANDARD HAS ALREADY WORKED

Here's the argument that settles it for me, and it's sitting right there on the roster.

**Cody White.**

A man who bounced between four organizations and six years of practice squads, who earned a role here by covering kicks and making plays whenever his number was called — and who now has five catches, three touchdowns and five first downs this season. Nobody handed Cody White anything. He met the standard and the standard rewarded him.

That is the exact philosophy Kubiak just applied to Thornton. And if you lower it for a 6-foot-5 receiver because the room is thin, you've just told everybody in that locker room that desperation beats preparation.

Desperation is how you put an unprepared player on the field in a one-score game and lose on a route he ran at the wrong depth. I'd rather wait a week.

## THE MARKET: FIVE NAMES

Of course, Las Vegas could also pursue outside help if Thornton needs more time.

**ESPN reporters Dan Graziano and Jeremy Fowler included Brian Thomas Jr., Jerry Jeudy, Quentin Johnston, Rashod Bateman and Jauan Jennings among several receivers who could potentially generate interest before the deadline.**

Two new names there. Bateman in Baltimore and Jennings in San Francisco — both veterans, both on teams with crowded target hierarchies, both the kind of player who moves for a mid-round pick rather than a premium one.

And the usual caveat applies, as always on this channel: **their availability has not been confirmed, and there is no evidence the Raiders are negotiating for any of them.** This is a market report, not a transaction.

Still, if Spytek identifies the right combination of talent, price and contractual fit, this team's shortage of dependable receivers could force him to explore it.

## THE THOMAS PROPOSAL — WITH A REAL PRICE

And one of those options has now become the subject of a detailed trade proposal.

**Christopher Knox of Bleacher Report identified Las Vegas as a possible destination for Brian Thomas Jr. and projected that acquiring him could require a second-round selection and a sixth-round selection.**

A second and a sixth. Let's examine whether that's worth it, because the two halves of his résumé tell completely different stories.

**The case for him:** Thomas entered this league as a first-round pick and immediately recorded **1,282 receiving yards and 10 touchdowns**, earning Pro Bowl recognition and looking like he was becoming one of the league's premier outside weapons. He's **only 24 years old**, with the size, the speed, and proven production. He'd be part of the future, not a patch.

**The case against him:** through the current season he has produced **only nine receptions for 105 yards and one touchdown.** And Jacksonville rookie Josh Cameron has already recorded 117 yards and two touchdowns — meaning a first-year player has out-produced him, which raises real questions about his role and his future in that offense.

## THE QUESTION SPYTEK HAS TO ANSWER FIRST

And here's the part of this analysis I think is most important, because it applies to every name on the board.

**Jaguars head coach Liam Coen is a respected offensive mind, and Trevor Lawrence is playing some of the best football of his career.**

Raider Nation, sit with that for a second. The offense is working. The quarterback is thriving. The head coach knows what he's doing.

**So if that staff cannot consistently involve Brian Thomas Jr., Spytek needs to understand exactly why before surrendering premium draft capital.**

That's the whole thing. When a struggling receiver plays for a struggling offense, you can tell yourself a story about circumstances — that's the Jeudy argument in Cleveland. When a struggling receiver plays for a *functioning* offense with a quarterback playing his best football, the story gets much harder to tell. Either something is wrong with the player, or something happened inside that building that nobody outside it knows about. Both possibilities should make you cautious.

**My verdict: a second-round selection is too expensive based on his current performance. A third-round offer would create a more reasonable balance between risk and upside.** At a third you're buying a 24-year-old former Pro Bowler's upside at a discount. At a second plus a sixth, you're paying Harrison money for a player having a worse season than Harrison was.

And I'll be consistent with what I said last week: **Jeudy at a fourth is still the best value on this board, and Calvin Ridley at a sixth is still basically free.**

## THE $35 MILLION PROBLEM NOBODY DISCUSSES

Now let me bring up the story that I think is the most under-covered failure of this entire offense.

**Jalen Nailor signed a $35 million contract during the offseason. Through four appearances he has recorded five receptions for 52 yards.**

Five catches. Fifty-two yards. Thirty-five million dollars.

Raider Nation, we have spent a month and a half on this channel discussing who to trade for, what pick to surrender, and whether Spytek should mortgage future capital for an outside receiver. And the whole time, the highest-paid wide receiver on this roster has been producing at a rate that would be disappointing from a practice squad call-up — in fact, Cody White has three more touchdowns than him on the exact same number of catches.

I want to be fair: he missed the Patriots game in concussion protocol, and that's not a performance issue. But four appearances is a real sample, and 13 yards per game is a real number.

**And here's why it matters this week: he is expected to return against Buffalo.** Which makes this matchup an important opportunity to justify that investment.

Honestly, this is the cheapest possible solution to the receiver crisis. You don't need a second-round pick if the man you already paid $35 million starts playing like it. Before I ask Spytek to surrender draft capital, I want to see one Sunday where Nailor wins on the boundary.

## AND LET'S BE HONEST ABOUT TUCKER

One more piece of roster honesty while we're here.

**Tre Tucker has produced valuable explosive plays, but he is better suited to a secondary receiving role than carrying the responsibilities of a true number one wide receiver.**

I said something close to this four weeks ago when everybody wanted to crown him WR1 after his big games. His speed is a genuine weapon, and a defense has to account for it — but asking a player built to win vertically to be your primary target on third down, in the red zone, and against bracket coverage is asking him to be something he isn't.

That's not a knock on Tucker. That's a roster construction problem, and it's the same one we keep circling.

## THE QUARTERBACK UPDATE: COUSINS IS SAFE

Now the news that quiets the loudest conversation in Las Vegas.

**ESPN insider Dan Graziano reported that Kirk Cousins is not in danger of losing the starting job anytime soon.**

According to Graziano, the Raiders appreciated the opportunity to give Mendoza his first live professional experience — but they remain in no hurry to rush the rookie into the lineup. Las Vegas is 3-2, remains positioned to compete, and believes Cousins gives this offense its strongest current opportunity to win.

And then the detail that tells you how firm this is: **even a loss against Buffalo would not necessarily change that plan.** If Cousins performs well and the defeat results from larger team problems, the quarterback situation holds.

That said, the door isn't locked forever. The situation could become different later in the season if the Raiders fall out of playoff contention — at that point, evaluating the number one overall selection becomes a more logical organizational priority.

Jeremy Fowler also expects Mendoza to play eventually, but explained exactly why the Raiders don't need to force it: Cousins gives this team veteran stability while Mendoza is still learning how to operate an NFL offense. The lopsided New England game provided a chance to begin that development without immediately handing over control of the team.

## WHAT KUBIAK ACTUALLY PRAISED — AND WHY IT'S A BIG DEAL

But here's the part of this story that genuinely excited me, because of how specific the compliments were.

**Kubiak praised Mendoza's preparation, his quick decision-making, and his ability to work through his progressions. He also noted that the rookie recognized pressure situations and understood where the football needed to go when the defense created an unblocked rusher.**

Raider Nation, let me explain why that last one is the most encouraging sentence of the week.

An unblocked rusher is the hardest problem in football for a young quarterback. You have maybe a second and a half. Your protection has failed, nobody is going to save you, and the correct answer is almost always a specific pre-determined outlet — the back in the flat, the hot route over the middle, somewhere. Rookies panic. They scramble backward, they eat sacks, they throw the ball away, or they force something downfield into coverage.

**Mendoza knew where to go.** In his first NFL action. In a 28-point road loss.

That is not a stat line. That's evidence that the processing is ahead of schedule, and it tells you more about his future than six-of-eight for 48 yards does.

And notice the circumstance too: the Raiders' first four games remained competitive enough that Kubiak couldn't justify removing Cousins just for developmental snaps. New England changed that — and it let the rookie operate **behind the starting offensive line** in a difficult road environment. Kubiak described live game experience as the best form of development, even though the opportunity arrived because the team was being decisively beaten.

**So let's name it correctly: Mendoza's appearance represents progress, not a promotion.**

Cousins remains the starter against Buffalo and gets a chance to answer the criticism surrounding this offense. If he protects the football, creates explosive plays and keeps Las Vegas competitive, this organization has little reason to accelerate anything. If the losses continue and playoff hope starts disappearing, every additional Mendoza appearance makes the long-term conversation harder to avoid.

## THE JEANTY DECISION IS NOW AN INTERNAL CONVERSATION

And finally, the hardest subject of the week — and there's real news here.

**According to Dan Graziano, the Raiders have discussed internally whether giving Ashton Jeanty a game off could help him recover. But the young runner continues pushing to play.**

That's the first confirmation that this debate is happening inside the building and not just on channels like this one.

And look at the detail from Sunday, because it's revealing. He was listed as questionable before the New England game, **participated in a brief pregame workout, then spoke with trainers and coaches.** The team ultimately cleared him — and he finished with 27 rushing yards on 10 carries.

So a compromised running back went through a pregame audition, got cleared, and produced 2.7 yards a carry in a game his team lost by 28. I said it yesterday and I'll say it again: whatever risk was accepted there, almost no reward came back.

## THE PROMISE VS THE REALITY

And here's the context that makes this genuinely painful.

**Las Vegas expected its running game to become one of this offense's greatest strengths under Kubiak.** His previous offense ranked among the league's most productive rushing attacks. The Raiders then strengthened the line by signing elite center Tyler Linderbaum. The plan was explicit, the investment was real, and the coach's track record supported it.

**Instead, Las Vegas enters the Buffalo matchup ranked among the league's least productive rushing teams, with Jeanty averaging only 3.3 yards per carry.**

From a stated identity to the bottom of the league in five weeks. That gap is the single biggest unmet expectation of this season, bigger than the receiver room, because this one came with a hired coach and a signed center attached to it.

He hurt his ankle in practice shortly before the season and has also dealt with a foot issue. Both of those are real, and neither of them is his fault.

## MY ANSWER ON JEANTY

So here's where I land, and I'm going to be direct.

**Sit him against Buffalo.**

Not because he isn't tough — he's proven that beyond any doubt, and his teammates know it. Not because Washington Jr. is better — he isn't, long term. Sit him because the organization has an obligation the player cannot be expected to fulfill himself.

A competitive 22-year-old will never volunteer to come out. That's exactly why the decision cannot be left to him. Las Vegas has twelve games left, a schedule that includes Buffalo and the Rams, and a sixth overall pick whose entire game lives in his feet. You protect the asset, you get Washington Jr. the carries he's earned at five yards a pop, you find out what Roman Hemby can do, and you get your franchise back at full speed in November when it actually matters.

The internal discussion Graziano reported means somebody in that building agrees with me. I hope they win the argument.

So let me tie it together, because every one of these threads leads to the same place.

Thornton has three boxes to check and conditioning is the only one already checked. The market has five names on it, and my prices haven't moved: Jeudy at a fourth, Ridley at a sixth, Thomas at a third but not a second. Nailor is back and has $35 million worth of explaining to do. Those are the decisions of a staff building something, not a staff panicking. Whether that patience survives a second straight home loss is the question Sunday will answer.
