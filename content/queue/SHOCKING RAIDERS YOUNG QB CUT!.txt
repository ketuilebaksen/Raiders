"HE'S A HOTHEAD": WHAT PATRICK PAUL ACTUALLY SAID

So here's the quote lighting up the timeline: Miami's starting left tackle, Patrick Paul, entering his third year, was asked about facing Maxx Crosby — and in the middle of an otherwise respectful answer, out came "he's a hothead, so there are ways to get into his head." A week before these teams play. Cue the outrage machine.

But Raider Nation, watch the FULL clip before you sharpen the pitchforks, because the honest read is more interesting than the headline. Paul called Crosby a dynamic player. He called him a great player who demands attention for four full quarters. And three or four separate times in the same interview, he came back to one specific phrase: get my hands on him FIRST. Beat him to his spot. Disrupt him before he's comfortable. That's not trash talk, that's a leaked GAME PLAN — a coaching staff's instruction peeking through a player's press conference. The "hothead" line isn't saying Crosby is easy to beat; it's saying Crosby plays with swagger and emotion, and Miami hopes to weaponize that. Honestly? As a scouting report, it's... not wrong. Maxx DOES play hot. It's the furnace the whole engine runs on.

Here's why the plan reads better on paper than it'll play on grass. Crosby's game isn't a predetermined move you can jump — it's REACTION. Jump-set him, and he's already processed three counters before your feet finish. Shoot the hands early, and the double swipe arrives on schedule. Chip him with a tight end, and he's gap-jumping to either side before the chip lands. That marriage of instinct and elite processing speed is precisely why surface-level adjustments never hold up against him for sixty minutes. And credit where due: Paul is a legitimately ascending young tackle — six-foot-seven and a half, real length, flashing as a run blocker, the exact profile that tends to break out in year three or four. Which is what makes this delicious: whenever Crosby's alignment carries him over to that side, Crosby versus Paul is the single most important individual matchup of the entire game. The kid says he wants to put hands on Maxx first. Congratulations, young man — you've just guaranteed the whole football world watches you try.

One more note on the psychology here, because it cuts both ways. Miami's staff wouldn't build a plan around "getting into Crosby's head" unless they respected what happens when they DON'T — a comfortable Crosby dictating a game is a left tackle's nightmare and a coordinator's four-quarter migraine. But there's a risk in poking this particular bear that Paul may not fully appreciate yet: emotional players don't all respond to provocation the same way. Some unravel. The great ones WEAPONIZE it. And Crosby's entire career — undersized recruit, fourth-round pick, doubted rehab — is one long case study in turning slights into sacks. Ask around the league how the "let's frustrate him early" plan has aged for previous opponents. The tape says: poorly.

THE FUEL: WHAT THE BALTIMORE SAGA IS REALLY ABOUT NOW

And there's a layer under Crosby's fire this season that's worth understanding. New chatter around the collapsed Baltimore trade suggests the story wasn't purely the medical framing everyone ran with. According to those close to the situation, the Ravens didn't just flinch at a knee — they reconsidered whether the reported package, TWO first-round picks' worth of value, was really worth it. Sit with that. Whatever the true mix of medicine and money, from Crosby's chair it reads the same: a franchise publicly hesitated on him, and the league spent months whispering that his career might be winding down because of the injury history. For a competitor wired like Maxx — fully healthy now for the first time in a long while — that's not a scar. That's FUEL. And a certain third-year left tackle just poured a little gasoline on it a week before kickoff. Bless him.

JEANTY: SCHEFTER'S GREEN LIGHT — AND THE DISCOUNT NOBODY UNDERSTANDS

Now the news every Raiders fan checks first: Adam Schefter said on his podcast that he believes Ashton Jeanty will be FINE for the opener — official injury report still to come this week, but a real chance to play against Miami. No surprise; every signal in this recovery has pointed the same direction, exactly as we've tracked. And worst case? This looks like a one-game caution, not a saga.

But let me put on the fantasy hat for a minute, because the disrespect out there is genuinely funny to me. In a recent auction draft I heard about, Jeanty went for about forty bucks — a mid-tier price for a back who piled up 1,321 scrimmage yards as a ROOKIE behind the league's worst blocking situation. Houston's own head coach called him one of the league's true star-caliber players after facing him last year. And now? Now he gets one of the best run-game designers in football calling his plays, an $81 million center, and a coaching staff that will demand every receiver and tight end finishes blocks at a level last year's roster never approached. Here's the truth: you could put an average back behind this scheme and get results. Jeanty is NOT average — he's an elite open-field athlete who makes defenders miss at a rate comparable to the most electric backs in the sport. The trajectory says 1,500 total yards is in play this season, and consensus top-five fantasy back by next summer. The discount window is closing, folks. Some of us have been shopping in it since August. And for the real-football translation of all that fantasy talk: a healthy Jeanty against a Miami front that struggled with physical run games all preseason is exactly the opening-week script Kubiak would draw by hand. Even on a managed snap count, his mere presence bends the defense — the play-action holds a beat longer, the boxes get honest, and the passing windows we've worried about all week get a little wider for everyone. That's what a foundational back does: he doesn't just gain yards, he SUBSIDIZES the whole offense.

THE QUIET CHURN: KEENAN'S TAPE, TWO FULLBACKS, AND A WARNING LIGHT

Now to the roster's basement, where this front office refuses to sit still — and I love it. Instead of setting the practice squad and forgetting it, Vegas keeps churning: Gary Smith III, who flashed as a pass rusher in August, AND safety Damon Square — the guy who grabbed a preseason interception — were both let go, clearing room for Tim Keenan III and the returning fullback Patrick Laird.

We introduced Keenan yesterday; now the film notes. The tape confirms the profile: a true NOSE, not a rusher — functional strength to defeat blocks, disciplined in his run fits, even powerful enough to draw a holding flag that wiped out real yardage on one rep. Solid, appropriately valued run defender. Not elite, and not pretending to be. The Laird move is quietly interesting too: carrying TWO fullbacks in the building is a statement in itself, and it hints the staff sees Laird as a genuine long-term option behind Connor Heyward — remember, Heyward also moonlights in the tight end role, so the insurance isn't redundant, it's structural.

But here's the warning light, and I'd be lying if I skipped it: look at the SHAPE of that swap. Out goes a pass-rush-leaning tackle; in comes a run-stopper. That tells you what the staff worries about — and it should worry you a little too. Because outside of Foley Fatukasi, who's been the one great interior surprise of this summer, this defensive tackle room is full of honest questions: Hemingway's preseason didn't show the disruptive juice you want from your young interior piece, and the rest of the rotation profiles as complementary, not anchoring. The uncomfortable scenario: a front that can't win early downs on its own, forcing Rob Leonard to blitz and stack boxes to stop the run — which is exactly how young secondaries get exposed downfield. We flagged defensive tackle as the thinnest room on this roster a week ago. The front office's own moves just co-signed the concern.

THE SLAYTON DEBATE: LET ME ARGUE WITH MYSELF

And finally — Darius Slayton, still a free agent, now with reported interest from around three teams. Earlier this week I made the case FOR Vegas being one of them, and I stand by every word: 4,435 career yards, a deep-threat profile this offense lacks, and a price paid only in money, not assets. But you know this channel's rule — the strongest version of the OTHER argument gets its day. So here it is, and honestly? It's good.

The case against: this roster already has its offensive foundations in Bowers and Jeanty. The receiver room isn't explosive, but the smarter long-term play might be riding with what's here — because somebody in that room is going to inherit 75 to 80 real targets this season, and finding out WHO — Nailor cashing his contract, Bech making the year-two leap, Benson accelerating — matters more for the franchise's future than renting a 29-year-old's floor. Sign Slayton, and you might buy three extra wins' worth of stability while accidentally freezing the development of the exact players you need answers on before next offseason — when the real solutions arrive anyway: a big free-agent swing, a trade, or a receiver class in the draft that scouts genuinely love. That's the argument, and it's not a straw man. It's a philosophy: development information is worth more than veteran insurance in year one of a rebuild.

My verdict, having argued both sides? I still lean toward the call — a locker room chasing respectability shouldn't be precious about competent hands, and Thornton's absence makes the vertical need real TODAY. But I'll admit this out loud: if the front office passes, it won't be negligence. It'll be conviction about the kids. And after the summer this front office has had, conviction has earned the benefit of the doubt. Watch the targets, Raider Nation — whoever starts eating them owns this room's future.
