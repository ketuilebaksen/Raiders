KNOW THE OPPONENT: A 1-1 RECORD THAT LIES

The Saints come in at 1-1, and that record tells you almost nothing about the team we're facing.

Against Detroit, they fell into a 21-point hole and nearly completed a massive comeback. Then they went into Baltimore, fell behind again, and found a way to win the game.

So what are we dealing with? A team that can look completely out of rhythm early and then become extremely dangerous after halftime. That's the identity: slow starts, dangerous finishes.

And here's the flip side of that identity, which is the opportunity. New Orleans has consistently struggled to score in the opening quarter, and that problem goes all the way back to last season. It isn't a one-game fluke. It's a pattern.

KEY ONE: WIN THE FIRST QUARTER

That's why I want this team coming out with a specific mentality from the opening possession.

We cannot spend the first quarter feeling out what works while New Orleans settles into a rhythm. I want the defense forcing uncomfortable situations immediately, the offense moving the chains, and the run game putting pressure on their front from the first series.

Because against a team that can't start games, the first fifteen minutes are worth more than they are against anybody else. If we're up ten before they've figured out their own offense, we've taken their entire script away.

But, and this is the part that worries me, building a lead against this team is only the beginning. Detroit built a 21-point lead and still had to survive a comeback. Baltimore got ahead and couldn't finish the job. So the real question isn't how we get ahead. It's how we keep it.

KEY TWO: ATTACK THE OFFENSIVE LINE INJURY

Now the matchup that jumps off the page. New Orleans won't have starting left tackle Kelvin Banks Jr., which means Austin Richards is expected to handle that position against our defensive front.

To be fair, Richards has some starting experience and he held up reasonably well after entering the Baltimore game. But this is still a line missing an important starter, and we're one of the most disruptive pressure teams in the league: eight sacks through two games, pressure on more than half of opponent dropbacks.

Here's the part I really want you to understand, though, because it's where the chess starts. Maxx Crosby doesn't have to line up directly across from the backup tackle for us to benefit from this injury.

Think about what happens if the Saints start giving Richards extra help with tight ends and chip blocks. They can't provide that help everywhere. Suddenly their right tackle, Taliese Fuaga, has a bigger responsibility with less support. Suddenly a guard is working alone. If New Orleans concentrates protection on one side, Rob Leonard's job is to find what that leaves open on the other.

We saw exactly this against the Chargers. Crosby drew three false starts out of their left tackle without recording a sack on those plays, and the disruption created chances for Malcolm Koonce, who finished with a sack and a half. Attention on 98 is a weapon by itself.

KEY THREE: PRESSURE, BUT WITH DISCIPLINE

Here's where I'm going to push back on the obvious plan, because just sending everybody after Tyler Shough is not the answer.

Shough has been effective against pressure through the first two games. Detroit tried the heavy-blitz approach early in their matchup, and at first it worked beautifully: he threw an interception, lost a fumble on a sack, and the offense couldn't move. But once New Orleans recognized what Detroit was doing, they adjusted and started taking advantage of those same looks.

That's the trap. Pressure that becomes predictable turns into completions. If we blitz on obvious downs from obvious places, this offense will make us pay in the second half, which is exactly when they're at their best.

Fortunately, this is precisely what our defense already does well. We generate pressure while blitzing only about a quarter of the time, because the rush wins one-on-one and the extra bodies stay in coverage. Keep doing that. Change the picture before the snap, make their line communicate, and never let the quarterback get comfortable, but don't hand him free rushing lanes to escape into or empty grass behind the linebackers.

KEY FOUR: DON'T BE THE TEAM THAT FIXES THEIR RUN GAME

New Orleans has been trying to build a running game all year and it hasn't come together. Their early-game rushing production has been especially disappointing. They brought in David Edwards at left guard and added a new lead back specifically because improving that part of the offense was a priority. They've experimented with different concepts, including outside zone, and the results still aren't there.

I do not want the Raiders to be the team that finally lets them establish it.

This is where Foley Fatukasi matters, and where the interior defensive line earns its money. Win first down, force second-and-long, and the whole game plan flips in our favor: now Crosby and Kwity Paye are rushing on third-and-eight against a line missing its starting left tackle, instead of third-and-three.

KEY FIVE: THE COVERAGE WEB

Now their passing game, because it has more than one problem for us.

Chris Olave is the biggest receiving threat we have to prepare for. His route running creates separation, but what concerns me more is how New Orleans adjusts when a defense tries to take him away. Against Baltimore, Marlon Humphrey was on him early, so the Saints started designing ways to get him into better matchups: motion, crossing routes, different alignments.

That means our secondary can't simply look at where Olave is before the snap. The coverage has to recognize how routes develop and communicate when receivers cross the field. If one defender hesitates or loses track of his assignment, Shough has already shown he'll take it.

And we can't put everything on Olave either. Devaughn Vele has become a reliable target, especially when they need somebody to make a difficult catch in traffic or convert a third down; his length and catch radius let him win even when the defender is in position. Their tight end Juwan Johnson has strong chemistry with Shough and gives them another option over the middle.

So the message is discipline over aggression. Chase one receiver too hard and this offense has enough options to punish you somewhere else.

THE SPOT THEY WILL ATTACK: OUR SAFETY

And now the part that genuinely worries me, because I think I know exactly where New Orleans is going to look.

Trayden Stokes is in the concussion protocol and might not be available. He's shown encouraging play speed on the back end, and losing him matters. If Isaiah Pola-Mao steps into that starting role, I expect the Saints to examine that matchup closely, because we've already seen him struggle to track receivers coming in behind him in zone coverage, including a deep completion against the Chargers.

Now pair that with this number, which is the scariest stat of the week. Against Baltimore, when safeties were the primary defenders in coverage, Shough completed all eight of his passes for 118 yards and a touchdown.

Eight for eight. That's not a coincidence. That's a passing game that has found something, and they will go looking for it again on Sunday.

So this is the battle inside the battle. Our secondary has to handle the routes developing in front of it without losing what's happening downfield. If we give up two or three explosive plays over the middle, all the pass-rush numbers in the world won't save the afternoon.

THE OFFENSE: DO NOT ABANDON JEANTY

Now our side of the ball, and I want to be very direct about the mistake I'm afraid of.

We saw the run game work against Miami. We saw the Chargers take it away in Week Two, with Ashton Jeanty finishing at 2.3 yards a carry. And we still won that game, but I don't want this to become a team that has to overcome an ineffective rushing attack every week.

Against this Saints defense, patience is going to matter. They've faced dangerous backs already, including Jahmyr Gibbs and Derrick Henry, and while they haven't shut every run down, they've done enough to keep opponents from controlling games on the ground. So the first few carries may not look good.

Here's the lesson from Baltimore, and it's the whole point. Derrick Henry had been finding some success in the first half. After halftime, he got five carries. Five. Baltimore managed three second-half points, and the Saints came back and won the game.

I'm not telling you that running the ball guarantees a win. I'm telling you that abandoning it against this specific team hands them the ball, hands them momentum, and plays directly into their comeback identity. Keep handing it to Jeanty. Keep working at the line of scrimmage. Even a three-yard carry that keeps the clock moving is doing a job.

THE BOWERS FACTOR AND THE PERSONNEL CHESS

And this is where it gets fun, because Brock Bowers is expected back.

Combine a running back who threatens a defense with a tight end who creates coverage problems, and New Orleans has to prepare for multiple ways we can move the football. We've already been using heavier personnel, over a third of our snaps in two-back looks against Miami. Now add multiple tight end formations with Bowers and Michael Mayer on the field together.

Here's the matchup I want Kubiak to examine. New Orleans has often responded to heavy personnel by keeping its base defense on the field and trusting its linebackers with the extra coverage work. So ask the question: if they keep linebackers out there to stop Jeanty, can we get Bowers matched on one of them in the passing game? And if they adjust their coverage to account for Bowers, what does that open up underneath and outside?

I don't expect it to be easy. Brandon Staley has done an impressive job with that defense, and his linebackers give him flexibility, with Pete Werner looking good in an expanded role and another linebacker who can drop into coverage or rush from different spots. They also use three-safety packages with Jonas Sanker adding flexibility on the back end. So they have answers for Bowers.

But answers cost something. Every defender they commit to our best weapon is a defender not doing something else.

THEIR YOUNG SECONDARY IS WORTH TESTING

One more opportunity. The Saints lost Alontae Taylor in free agency and are leaning on younger defensive backs in important roles. Kool-Aid McKinstry, Quincy Riley, and Sanker have held up well through two games, and credit to them.

But young is still young. I want Cousins testing those matchups, especially with Tre Tucker coming off a 119-yard afternoon where he ran straight past an NFL secondary. If we establish Jeanty on the ground and create receiving chances for Bowers, their corners have to defend multiple threats with less help.

We don't need to abandon our identity to challenge this defense. We need to execute it and make New Orleans react to us.

MY PICK

So here's where I land. Win the first quarter. Make the backup tackle's side a problem without becoming predictable. Stop the run on early downs. Keep feeding Jeanty even when it's ugly. Protect the middle of the field with whoever plays safety. And use Bowers to force decisions.

Do those things and I think we're 3-0. Raiders 27, Saints 23, and I'm telling you right now the fourth quarter will be uncomfortable, because that's who this opponent is.
