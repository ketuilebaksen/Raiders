## THE NUMBER THAT DECIDES A LINEUP

Start with protection, because everything else on this offense depends on it.

The Raiders offensive line surrendered three sacks and allowed Kirk Cousins to face pressure on **44.8 percent of his dropbacks** in the loss to New England.

Raider Nation, that's not pressure. That's a quarterback getting hunted on nearly every other snap. Any passer in this league falls apart at that rate, and a 38-year-old who has never relied on mobility has no chance of surviving it.

But no individual blocker struggled more than Spencer Burford.

**The starting left guard allowed nine pressures against the Patriots — raising his season total to 23, the highest number surrendered by any left guard in the NFL.**

Nine in one game. Twenty-three on the season. Worst in football at his position.

And I want to be fair about the context: Las Vegas signed Burford to a one-year contract worth $3.6 million during the offseason, expecting him to stabilize the interior next to Tyler Linderbaum and Jackson Powers-Johnson. That's a modest, sensible bet on a young lineman. It simply hasn't worked. His repeated breakdowns have created one of this offense's most urgent problems.

## THE OPTIONS ON THE TABLE

Frustrated Raiders fans have begun publicly calling for Kubiak to remove Burford from the lineup, with many suggesting Caleb Rogers should replace him directly.

So let me lay out the three realistic configurations, because this is a genuine football puzzle and not as simple as "bench the bad guy."

**Option one: Rogers in, Burford out, straight swap at left guard.** Simplest, fastest, and it puts your most recent starter back on the field. The problem is that Rogers has played right guard since entering this league, so you'd be asking him to flip sides and relearn his footwork and his combination blocks from the opposite stance.

**Option two: move Jackson Powers-Johnson to left guard and put Rogers at right guard, where he's always played.** This is the one I'd lean toward if the staff believes JPJ can handle the switch. It puts both players in situations where one of them is in his natural spot, and JPJ is the most talented interior lineman on this roster.

**Option three: rookie Trey Zuhn III at left guard eventually** — but his current knee injury makes that impossible right now, so it's a November conversation at the earliest.

And the financial part is easy. Benching Burford would not create a significant long-term problem, because his contract is inexpensive and expires after this season. There's no sunk cost argument here. Nobody has to justify a big investment.

## BUT ONE SWAP DOESN'T FIX THIS

Now the honest caution, and I want you to hear it before you get your hopes up about a lineup change solving Sunday.

**Changing one position would not automatically correct every weakness in this protection.**

Offensive line communication, individual technique, and the coordination of the entire unit all have to improve before Las Vegas faces a Buffalo defense capable of attacking vulnerable protections.

Think about what happened in New England. Cousins himself said it was a different problem on a different play — not one repeated breakdown. That's a unit-level failure, not a single-player failure. You can remove the worst performer and still allow pressure on 40 percent of dropbacks if the slides are late and the communication is poor.

So a change would help. It would not be a cure.

## KUBIAK'S ANSWER, AND WHY IT MATTERS

Reporters asked him directly whether he might shuffle the offensive line.

**He said the staff must evaluate everything, because the improvement he has demanded for multiple weeks still has not occurred.**

And then he did the thing he's done every single week: he placed responsibility on himself for finding a solution rather than blaming only the players.

Raider Nation, that admission raises the stakes on his next decision enormously, and here's exactly why.

**Continuing with the same lineup would indicate that this staff believes Burford can correct his mistakes. Making a change would demonstrate that poor performance now carries immediate consequences.**

Those are two completely different messages to a locker room, and every player in that building will read whichever one he sends. With this offense already failing to establish a run game or sustain drives, Kubiak has to decide whether to promote Rogers, eventually turn to Zuhn, or move Powers-Johnson across the formation — before the protection problems erase the promise of this start entirely.

My vote, for the record: make the change. Not out of anger, but because 23 pressures is a season-long sample, not a bad afternoon. And because a staff that has asked for improvement for multiple weeks without receiving it has to eventually act, or the asking stops meaning anything.

## HARRISON IS GONE — AND HE WENT TO HOUSTON

Now the news that stings.

Las Vegas needs more production from its wide receivers. And one of the most exciting potential solutions is no longer available.

**The Arizona Cardinals traded Marvin Harrison Jr. to the Houston Texans.**

Raider Nation, four days ago I sat here and told you the Harrison price was at least a second-round pick plus additional compensation, that Houston was in the talks, and that two bidders means the price never comes down. I said we should let Houston pay the premium.

Well, Houston paid it. And I want to be honest with you about how that feels, because there are two truths here.

The first truth: I still would not have paid a second plus extras for a receiver with his production history. I stand by the process.

The second truth: that move removed a young, high-upside target who could have changed the structure of this passing game — and he's 24 years old, meaning he would have been part of the future and not just a patch. Discipline has a cost, and this is what the cost looks like. You don't get to be disciplined *and* get the player.

## WHY THIS MATTERS MORE AFTER NEW ENGLAND

And the Patriots game is exactly what makes this sting.

New England exposed how difficult it is for this offense to operate when opponents can crowd the line of scrimmage without fearing consistent production on the outside.

Brock Bowers functions almost like an elite slot receiver — and that's the precise problem. An elite interior threat does not stop a defense from loading the box. You need a player who threatens the boundary.

So this offense still needs another dependable player capable of stretching coverage, winning on the outside, and discouraging defenses from loading up against the run. That's the same sentence I've been writing for a month, and it's now four weeks more urgent.

## THE THREE NAMES THAT REPLACED HIM

Three alternatives have emerged, and each one is a different bet on cost, age and availability. Let me give you all three with real prices.

**Brian Thomas Jr. — the biggest upside, the biggest price.**

The former Jacksonville first-round pick was one of the league's most productive rookie receivers before struggling with drops in his second season and seeing a reduced role in his third. He's 24, which means he could become part of this team's future rather than a temporary repair.

But Tom Pelissero reported that Jacksonville continues to receive periodic calls and has shown **no interest** in trading him. That position could change, but Las Vegas would probably need to offer compensation comparable to the second-round value attached to Harrison.

My read: this is the dream and the least likely. "No interest" from a team that drafted him in the first round is exactly what you'd expect, and the price would be the same one I just declined to pay.

**Jerry Jeudy — the middle ground, and still my favorite.**

Cleveland's additions of Denzel Boston and Conception have increased competition in that receiver room, potentially making Jeudy available despite his 1,229-yard season in 2024.

And here's the number that matters: **a fourth-round selection could be enough** if Cleveland decides to move him.

Raider Nation, that's the price I've been holding out for since the first week we built this board. A fourth-round pick for a receiver with a 1,200-yard season on his résumé, who our head coach coordinated in Denver's passing game in 2022. If that's genuinely the market, you make that call before you finish reading the text message.

**Calvin Ridley — the cheapest shot, and the shortest horizon.**

Tennessee appears unlikely to contend this season, and the 32-year-old could potentially be acquired for a **sixth or seventh-round pick.**

Ridley would not transform this offense by himself. But he could still provide more proven production than the Raiders currently possess at the position, and the cost is basically free. For a team trying to survive twelve more weeks with three healthy receivers, "more proven than what we have" has real value at a sixth-rounder.

## THE DECISION SPYTEK ACTUALLY FACES

So John Spytek has to answer one question, and it's bigger than any of these three names.

**Is this roster close enough to playoff contention to justify surrendering draft capital during an ongoing reconstruction?**

Thomas offers the most upside but demands the largest investment. Jeudy provides the middle ground. Ridley represents a low-cost attempt to improve immediately.

And I have to add the caveat this channel always adds: **none of these players is confirmed to be available, and there is no evidence that Las Vegas is actively negotiating for any of them.** This is a board of possibilities, not a report.

The approaching deadline will force Spytek to determine whether the effect of this weakness — on both the passing game *and* the rushing attack, because they're connected — justifies pursuing one of those uncertain alternatives.

My answer, clearly stated: **Ridley at a sixth, yes, immediately. Jeudy at a fourth, yes. Thomas at a second, no.** The receiver problem is real, but the solution has to be proportional to what this team actually is.

## DAMIAN LILLARD IS DEMANDING CHANGES

Now the part everybody's talking about.

Portland Trail Blazers star Damian Lillard reacted to the embarrassing loss by calling for more playing time for **Jermod McCoy, Hezekiah Masses and Washington Jr.** — while also urging Las Vegas to acquire a wide receiver.

And his frustration reflected exactly what Raider Nation witnessed. The offense failed to move the football consistently. The defense provided little resistance. And special teams missed a field goal, leaving almost no area of this team untouched by the collapse.

Raider Nation, I have to pause on that last detail, because it's a small thing that says a lot. Matt Gay was 10 for 10 on the season. He was the one certainty on this roster — the thing I called a lifeline two weeks ago. In New England, even that broke. When your perfect kicker misses, you know it was the kind of day where nothing held.

## MY TAKE ON LILLARD'S LIST, NAME BY NAME

His proposal is not an official organizational plan — he's a fan with a famous name, not the general manager. But several parts of it genuinely deserve consideration, so let's take them seriously one at a time.

**Jermod McCoy: I agree completely.** He entered the draft process with first-round potential before an injury contributed to his fall into the fourth round. Now that he appears healthy, giving him a larger role could help this staff evaluate whether his talent can strengthen a secondary that struggled badly against New England. When your coverage gets attacked all afternoon and you have a fourth-round pick with first-round traits sitting there healthy, you find out what you have.

**Hezekiah Masses: I disagree with the framing.** He's already received meaningful opportunities and endured a difficult performance in Week Five — but that doesn't erase the encouraging moments he produced earlier in the season. Remember the two interceptions at SoFi and the conference honors. **He remains a fifth-round rookie, which means mistakes and uneven performances should be expected as part of his development.** You don't develop a young corner by yanking him after one bad Sunday against a Super Bowl caliber offense.

**Washington Jr.: I agree, and his usage is much harder to explain from the outside.** The rookie received only one carry against New England despite Jeanty playing through physical limitations and this offense desperately needing another source of energy. It's possible the workload reflected something that happened during preparation — but Kubiak's staff has provided no evidence he was being disciplined. **Without additional information, the decision appears questionable**, and it has intensified calls for him to become a bigger part of this offense. Rightly so.

**And the receiver request connects to the roster's most obvious weakness.** Harrison is gone, Jacksonville reportedly has no interest in Thomas, and the remaining options may offer less dramatic improvement. Lillard is right about the need. The market may not cooperate.

## THE DEEPER QUESTION NOBODY WANTS TO ASK

But underneath Lillard's list is the question that actually defines this season, and I want to give it to you straight even though you won't love it.

**Should Spytek accelerate this rebuild because Las Vegas won its first three games — or stay committed to the long-term plan?**

Those victories created real excitement. I shared it. But here's the number that has haunted me since the first week I mentioned it: **the Raiders' first three opponents have combined for only one victory in fifteen games.**

One win in fifteen. Read that again.

The narrow loss to Kansas City suggested this team could genuinely compete with a strong opponent. New England exposed how far the roster may still be from sustaining that level.

So giving young players more opportunities could help address immediate weaknesses without guaranteeing anything about a playoff run. And Las Vegas must determine whether this season justifies an aggressive trade, or whether the more responsible approach is to keep evaluating its young talent while preserving resources for another offseason.

My honest position: **the Ridley-level move is free, so make it. The Jeudy-level move is proportional, so make it if the price is real. Anything above that is buying a ticket to a race this roster may not be in.** That's not pessimism. That's what one win in fifteen games against your first three opponents means.

## THE QUARTERBACK ANSWER WAS UNAMBIGUOUS

Now let's close the loop on the story that will not go away.

Mendoza's first regular season appearance immediately generated discussion about this team's quarterback future. And Kubiak made it clear: **Kirk Cousins remains the starter.**

When reporters asked about the hierarchy, the answer was direct — **Cousins is the starter, but the organization trusts Mendoza if his number is called.**

That statement does two things at once, and it's well constructed. It prevents one late appearance in a blowout from becoming a manufactured quarterback controversy, while confirming the staff believes its rookie can handle additional responsibility when necessary.

And the football case for Cousins is strong. **He has thrown 12 touchdown passes this season** and continues operating at a level that gives Las Vegas a chance to compete when the protection and the supporting cast function properly. That's the whole caveat, isn't it — when the protection functions.

## WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED ON MENDOZA'S DRIVE

And here's the detail I didn't have yesterday, which makes his debut more interesting.

Mendoza entered after the Raiders fell behind, displayed composure, and moved the unit toward scoring position. But the possession ended **without points after he failed to convert on fourth down.**

**On that final fourth-down play, he escaped the pocket and tried to reach the first down marker — and finished one yard short.**

One yard. And listen to how he handled it.

He accepted responsibility, joked that he needs to improve his speed, and acknowledged that he should have delivered the football to Devin Tomkins across the middle.

Raider Nation, that is a 22-year-old first overall pick, in his first NFL action, identifying the correct read he missed and making a joke at his own expense. He also described the drive as **an overall failure** while praising his teammates for continuing to compete late in the fourth quarter.

He admitted that entering during a decisive defeat prevented the experience from feeling completely positive, but said he appreciated getting regular season snaps he can evaluate and learn from before his next opportunity.

That's the accountability this organization wants from a young quarterback. Add him to the list of people in that building who took blame this week instead of giving it.

## THE RISK NOBODY IS TALKING ABOUT

But here's the thing I need you to think about before you start demanding the rookie plays.

**Playing behind an offensive line that allowed pressure on nearly half of Cousins' dropbacks could expose Mendoza to unnecessary punishment before the protection has been repaired.**

That's the real argument for patience, and it has nothing to do with Cousins' performance. You do not put your franchise's entire future behind a line surrendering 44.8 percent pressure rates and 23 pressures from one guard. That's how promising careers get damaged before they start.

Fix the protection first. Then have the quarterback conversation.

At the same time, his debut gave this staff useful film and gave the rookie experience that cannot be completely recreated in practice. That's real value from an ugly afternoon.

So here's the framework. **If the Raiders remain competitive, Mendoza can continue developing without unnecessary pressure. If the season deteriorates and playoff contention disappears, the calculation changes — because live repetitions become increasingly valuable to the franchise's future.**

For now, one encouraging possession has not changed the plan: Cousins leads this offense while Mendoza develops as his eventual successor.

## FOUR DECISIONS, ONE WEEK

So let me put the whole thing together, because this is the most consequential week of Kubiak's young head coaching career.

Burford's struggles may force a reconstruction of the offensive line, and 23 pressures from one position is a sample large enough to act on. Missing out on Harrison puts even more pressure on Spytek to evaluate Thomas, Jeudy, Ridley or someone else before the deadline. Lillard wants McCoy, Masses and Washington to receive bigger opportunities, and the organization has to balance immediate improvement against the patience a rebuild requires. And although Mendoza showed composure in his debut, Cousins remains the starter while the rookie prepares for whatever opportunity eventually arrives.

Four decisions. One week. A Buffalo team coming to Allegiant.

And here's my closing thought, Raider Nation. Every single one of those decisions is about the same question: **does this organization believe it's good enough right now, or does it believe it's still becoming something?** The answer determines whether they trade a fourth-round pick, whether they bench a starter, whether a rookie corner keeps his job, and eventually, when a 22-year-old takes over this offense.

I don't think they're a contender yet. I also don't think two losses erase what we saw in September. Both of those can be true, and the next three weeks will tell us which one matters more.
