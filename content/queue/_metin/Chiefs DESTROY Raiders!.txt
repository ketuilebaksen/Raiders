3-1 AND KUBIAK IS FURIOUS: Why an Angry Head Coach Is the Best Thing That Came Out of the Chiefs Loss 🏴‍☠️

Raider Nation, the undefeated run is over. Kansas City came into Allegiant Stadium and took first place in the AFC West, and the Raiders are 3-1.

I'm not going to sit here and pretend that doesn't sting. We spent all week talking about the blackout, about earning the building back, about the chance to make the entire league stop treating this start like a September accident. And it didn't happen.

But here's the thing I keep coming back to, and it's the reason I'm not panicking today: the angriest man in that building afterward was the head coach.

Klint Kubiak was hot. Visibly, genuinely furious. Not the polite disappointment we've watched coaches perform here for the last five years. Not the "we'll look at the tape and get better" shrug that this fan base has been fed after every loss since 2021. Real anger, about real things.

And if you've followed this franchise as long as I have, you understand exactly why that's the most encouraging detail of the entire afternoon.

I'm Victor, and this is Raiders Tunnel. If you're still here after a loss, you're the real ones. Hit that like button, drop "I AM A RAIDER" in the comments, and subscribe as we push toward 6,000 believers, because the honest breakdowns matter more after losses than after wins. Let's get into it.

WHY THE ANGER IS THE STORY

Let me explain what I mean, because somebody is going to clip this and say I'm celebrating a loss. I'm not.

There are two kinds of anger after a football game. There's the anger of a coach who got beaten by a better team and can't accept reality, and there's the anger of a coach whose team didn't live up to a standard he spent eight months installing. Those look identical on television and they mean completely different things.

Kubiak's anger was the second kind, and you could tell by what he was angry about. He wasn't fuming about Patrick Mahomes making a throw nobody on earth can defend. He wasn't raging about losing to a team with three Super Bowls in the building. He was angry about the self-inflicted stuff. The things this team controls. The things that were fine for three weeks and then weren't.

That's a coach with a standard. And a standard is the single hardest thing to install in a losing organization, because for years around here, losing was just weather. It happened to you. Nobody's fault, nothing to be done, see you next Sunday.

Watch what he does with this week instead. A coach who gets angry about controllables is a coach who believes they're fixable. Remember what he said at the podium after New Orleans, when this team was undefeated: three games in, long season to go, every week is a new challenge, and we're starting from scratch now. He described that locker room as a mature, goal-oriented group that was "not satisfied with what's been done," and then talked about having a great Wednesday, Thursday and Friday practice.

That's the same man who was furious yesterday. Those two things are the same thing.

WHAT THIS LOSS DOES NOT CHANGE

Before we get into the problems, let's be adults about the scoreboard, because the overreaction industry is already running at full speed.

This team is 3-1. Last season it won three games in seventeen weeks. The Raiders have already matched that total and they're four games into the year. Nobody in this fan base would have taken 3-1 after four weeks if you'd offered it in August, and anybody telling you otherwise today is lying to you.

The structural parts of this team are still structural. The pass rush that ranks near the top of the league in pressure rate while barely blitzing didn't disappear in one afternoon. Nakobe Dean is still the heartbeat of this defense, leading it in tackles, solo tackles, tackles for loss and forced fumbles while playing 98 percent of the snaps with a PFF grade of 84.5, third among all linebackers in football. Brock Bowers is still the best tight end in the sport and the reigning AFC Offensive Player of the Week. Kirk Cousins is still a quarterback who had thrown nine touchdown passes with no turnover problem through three games, which no quarterback in NFL history had done in his first three with a new team.

Those facts survived Sunday. What Sunday did was expose the things we'd already identified, and I want to go through them honestly, because this channel warned you about every single one before kickoff.

PROBLEM ONE: THE RUN GAME IS STILL BROKEN

I said it in the preview and I'll say it again now: the run game has been the missing piece since Week Two, and against Kansas City it cost us.

Ashton Jeanty does not look 100 percent to me. I've watched him since college and I know what that player looks like when he's right, and this isn't it. He's been getting his ankles taped during games, Kubiak has acknowledged that everybody's sore and nobody's at full health, and the sixth pick of the 2025 draft is averaging around three yards a carry for a season.

But I'm not putting this on the running back, because the bigger issue was up front and it was predictable. Jackson Powers-Johnson missed another practice with the groin injury and didn't play, which meant Caleb Rogers made his first start at guard against one of the best defensive fronts this team will see all year. That is a brutal assignment for anybody's first NFL start.

Here's why the run game matters more than casual fans think. A functioning rushing attack does three things at once: it controls the clock, it balances the offense so the defense can't sit on the pass, and it takes the load off a 38-year-old quarterback. Lose the run game and all three disappear together. Suddenly Cousins is throwing on second-and-nine all afternoon, the defense gets no rest, and a team with a limited receiver room has to be perfect to score.

This is exactly why the Cesar Ruiz addition matters now more than it did when it was announced. He can't fix an interior line by kickoff of his first week, and I told you that honestly at the time. But by the time New England, Buffalo and the Rams come around, that group needs to look different.

And the snap distribution has to change. Mike Washington Jr. gave this offense a huge spark in New Orleans with a 36-yard touchdown run and is averaging nearly seven yards a carry on limited work. Kubiak himself said Washington had "definitely earned more opportunities." I'm not saying hand him the starting job. I'm saying that if Jeanty isn't right, you cannot keep feeding him 19 or 20 carries at three yards a pop while a fresher back watches.

PROBLEM TWO: WE LOST THE EARLY-DOWN BATTLE

Here's the tactical heart of the loss, and we laid out the exact formula in the preview.

The entire plan against Kansas City was first down. Don't let Kenneth Walker turn second-and-eight into second-and-three, because the moment Andy Reid gets a manageable distance, his whole playbook opens up and Mahomes becomes impossible. Win early downs and this pass rush gets to attack a quarterback who has to throw. Lose them and you're defending every blade of grass with no idea what's coming.

That's the battle we didn't win often enough, and everything downstream followed from it. The pressure numbers that have defined this defense come from obvious passing situations. Take those away and a front built on winning one-on-one with four rushers has nothing to attack. You can't manufacture third-and-long out of thin air, and Rob Leonard was never going to blitz Mahomes into a mistake, because blitzing him is how you end up on a highlight reel.

We also knew this was going to require the hardest thing in football: linebackers and safeties diagnosing run or pass with zero hesitation, every snap, against a staff that designs specifically to create that hesitation. Three weeks of doing it well against Miami, the Chargers and New Orleans is not the same as doing it against Reid and Mahomes and a tight end who has been running those option routes for a decade.

That's not an excuse. That's the gap between where this team is and where the standard of the conference lives, and it's exactly what Sunday was supposed to measure.

PROBLEM THREE: THE TAKEAWAY WELL RAN DRY

Now the thing I've been warning about for two weeks, and I hate being right about it.

Nine takeaways in three games led the NFL and was never sustainable. Last season this team forced 16 in seventeen games. Three a week is not a repeatable rate for anybody, and when the turnovers stop arriving, you find out whether your defense can get off the field without them.

That's the real lesson of this loss. For three weeks, this defense bent and then took the ball away: 27 points allowed in New Orleans and four fourth-quarter takeaways to survive it. The takeaways masked a third-down problem and a middle-of-the-field problem that were visible on tape the whole time. Against a team that protects the football, the mask comes off.

So the fix isn't "force more fumbles." You can't scheme luck. The fix is third down. Get off the field on third down without needing a strip, and this defense goes from opportunistic to actually good.

And the streak that defined this start — the three straight games without allowing a single fourth-quarter point, the longest such run to begin a season in franchise history — is over. It was a beautiful thing while it lasted, and it told us something real about this team's conditioning and finishing habits. But it was always going to end, and it ended against the team most likely to end it.

PROBLEM FOUR: SOMEBODY ELSE HAS TO WIN

The receiver room. Again.

Jack Bech is on injured reserve with a fractured forearm. No Raiders wide receiver averages even four catches per game. And Kansas City had a full week to design a plan whose first priority was taking Brock Bowers out of the middle of the field, which is what every good defense is going to do from now on, because he just won Offensive Player of the Week with 13 targets.

Here's the uncomfortable truth in that: an offense whose best player is a tight end needs the perimeter to punish single coverage when the help goes inside. Tre Tucker has had one enormous afternoon, the 119-yard game at SoFi. Cody White has three touchdowns on three catches, which is wonderful and also a tiny sample. Michael Mayer is a weapon in two-tight-end sets and a genuine blocker, and he was worth every dollar of that extension.

But you cannot beat the best teams in this league with a passing game that has exactly one must-cover player. That's the whole reason we've spent a month on the Jeudy, Legette and Harrison files, and the reason the November 10th deadline matters. The good news: Carter Renan and Dante Thornton Jr. are both eligible to come off short-term injured reserve around Week Five, and Thornton is a 6-foot-5 rookie with a 4.35 forty who nobody has had to plan for yet.

WHAT THE NATIONAL MEDIA WILL DO WITH THIS

Let's get ahead of the noise, because you're going to hear it all week.

Stephen A. Smith was already saying before this game that it was only a matter of time before the whole thing fell apart. Fox Sports had this team ranked 20th at 3-0. And now every one of those voices gets to say "I told you so" about a loss to a three-time Super Bowl winner with Patrick Mahomes at quarterback, at a point in the season when the Raiders have already matched last year's win total.

Here's my answer, and I want you to use it in your group chats this week.

Before the season, the national projections had this team at six or seven wins. They're 3-1 after four games. Every single one of those models would have to be rewritten upward, not downward, based on what has actually happened. One loss to the standard of this division doesn't validate a prediction that this team would be among the worst in football. It just means Kansas City is still Kansas City.

And the specific "they haven't played anybody" argument cuts both ways now. If beating Miami, the Chargers and New Orleans doesn't count because of who they are, then losing to Kansas City can't be evidence of fraudulence, because Kansas City is the opposite of those teams. You don't get to use opponent quality only when it supports your take.

THE THING THAT WOULD ACTUALLY WORRY ME

Let me tell you what would make me genuinely concerned, because it isn't this result.

It would worry me if this team came out flat next week. It would worry me if the locker room started pointing fingers, if the offensive line stopped communicating, if Jeanty's body language changed, if the young defensive backs started playing scared instead of aggressive. Those are the symptoms of a 3-14 team, and we watched all of them last season.

Nothing about this group suggests that's coming, and the biggest piece of evidence is the thing I opened with. The head coach is angry. Maxx Crosby, who said before the game that this team had to earn a full silver and black crowd and that three wins meant there was still a lot of work to do, is not going to accept a quiet week. Nakobe Dean, who won a national championship at Georgia and a Super Bowl in Philadelphia and describes his superpower as making the people around him better, is not going to let the middle of that defense sulk.

That's what veterans with winning pedigrees are for. Not for the 3-0 weeks. For this week.

FIVE FIXES BEFORE NEW ENGLAND

So let's be useful. Here's my list of what has to change, in order of importance, before this team goes on the road to face the Patriots.

One: the interior offensive line. Get Powers-Johnson healthy, get Cesar Ruiz integrated, and give Caleb Rogers the benefit of a normal practice week instead of a first start against an elite front. Everything in this offense begins here.

Two: third down on defense. The takeaways will come and go. Getting off the field is a skill you can practice: rush lane integrity, coverage discipline on third-and-medium, and not giving up the easy conversion over the middle. This defense allows around 18 points a game and ranks inside the top ten, and it can be better than that.

Three: the run game split. If Jeanty is sore, manage him. Fifteen or sixteen carries for him, eight to ten for Washington, and let the fresher legs find the crease. Protect your 2025 top-ten pick instead of grinding him down in October.

Four: red zone efficiency. Cousins said after New Orleans that this offense had to be better in the red zone early in games, and that was in a win. Settling for field goals against good teams is how you lose by one score.

Five: discipline. This team was fundamentally sound for three weeks, with one glaring exception: the twelve-men-on-the-field penalty at SoFi that it survived only because Dean forced a fumble on the very next play. Against good opponents, you don't get bailed out. If Kubiak was angry about anything yesterday, I'd bet money it was something in this category.

THE SCHEDULE IS NOT WAITING

Here's the part nobody wants to hear on a Monday: the gauntlet is just starting.

New England, Buffalo, the Rams, the Jets, San Francisco, Seattle, Denver. That's what's left of this stretch, and there is no breather anywhere in it.

But let me reframe what success looks like, because I think a lot of fans are about to set themselves up for misery. This team does not have to win all of those games to have a real season. At 3-1, going 4-4 over the next eight puts this team at 7-5 with the finish still in front of it. Going 5-3 makes it a playoff contender in December. Even a 3-5 stretch leaves them 6-6 and alive, which would still be double last season's win total with weeks to spare.

The season is not defined by Sunday. It's defined by whether the structural things we identified in September are real: the pressure rate, the communication, the culture, and a quarterback who protects the football.

THE PERSPECTIVE I NEED YOU TO KEEP

Let me close with the thing I'd want somebody to tell me if I were mad right now.

Four weeks ago this franchise was coming off 3-14 with a fired head coach, a traded quarterback, the first overall pick, and a national conversation about whether its best defensive player should be sold to a contender. The most optimistic thing anybody said about the Raiders in August was that Fernando Mendoza might have a decent situation to inherit in 2028.

Today they're 3-1, with the best tight end in football, a linebacker in the Fred Warner conversation, a rookie corner with three interceptions and a Defensive Player of the Week award at pick 175, a quarterback who made NFL history in his first three games here, and a head coach who gets furious when his team falls below the standard.

Kansas City has been the team everybody in this division chases for a decade. Yesterday the Raiders got measured against that, at home, and came up short. That's not a collapse. That's a measurement, and the measurement said: closer than anybody expected, not there yet.

The anger in that locker room today is how you close the rest of the gap.
