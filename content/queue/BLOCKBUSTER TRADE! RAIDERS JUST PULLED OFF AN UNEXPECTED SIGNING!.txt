FOUNDATION ONE: THE QUARTERBACK PLAN, TOP TO BOTTOM

Everything this season starts with one official sentence: Kirk Cousins is the starting quarterback of the Las Vegas Raiders. Klint Kubiak made it formal for the Miami opener, and while nobody fell out of their chair — Cousins ran with the ones practically all summer while Mendoza worked behind him — the way it happened matters. Kubiak didn't frame it as a placeholder or a babysitting job. He said he's pleased with all three of his quarterbacks, and that Cousins EARNED it.

And the money agrees with him. Look at the structure one more time, because it's the most misread contract in Vegas: two years, twenty million guaranteed — with Atlanta still on the hook for $8.7 million of this season's salary. Teams do not guarantee starter money to a mentor with a clipboard. They guarantee it to a player they intend to USE — and getting his old team to subsidize the bill is the kind of business that makes accountants smile. The résumé he carries into season fifteen is what it is: 88 wins, 77 losses, two ties across three franchises, a 13-and-4 Vikings peak in 2022, and — the part Kubiak keeps highlighting — a command of this exact system that has visibly deepened through their film work together. No, none of that promises wins in silver and black. What it promises is a FLOOR. And a franchise raising a number one overall pick needs a floor more than it needs fireworks.

Which brings us to the man everyone's really watching. Fernando Mendoza — the Heisman, the national title at Indiana, the flashes in August that made the first overall pick feel obvious... and the rookie moments that came right along with them. Exactly as advertised. The challenge now gets sneaky-hard: Cousins owns the first-team reps, so Mendoza's development shifts to the scout team, the film room, and the mental game of staying one snap away. When the press pushed Kubiak on when the rookie plays, he gave them nothing and everything at once: "We'll get to Miami first." Honestly? Beautiful answer. One game in the windshield, zero promises in the rearview. But this channel's flag has been planted since episode one and it's not moving: the Mendoza era arrives before Thanksgiving.

And don't skip the third name, because his story is quietly one of my favorites on the roster. Aidan O'Connell — fourth year, contract year, clearly behind two quarterbacks — was supposed to be trade bait. It never happened, and the why is simple: August ruined the sales pitch. Twenty-two of forty-six, 283 yards, zero touchdown passes and one on the ground — while the quarterback carousel spun elsewhere, McCord landing in Miami and Ewers in Jacksonville, nobody put real value on the table. And I'll keep saying what I've said all summer: GOOD. If Cousins goes down in October, the correct answer is a professional backup who can run the offense — not shoving Mendoza into chaos on someone else's schedule. Plus, one quarterback injury anywhere in the league and Aidan's phone value doubles overnight. Insurance you can still sell later? That's not a failure. That's a portfolio.

FOUNDATION TWO: WHAT THE 53 CONFESSES

Now flip the coin, because a finalized roster is a confession — it tells you what a team truly believes, and where it's bluffing. Read this 53 closely and it confesses plenty.

Confession one: the receiver room is running on fumes. Kubiak publicly challenged this group after Houston, daring somebody to grab a job. And what happened at cut-down? Dont'e Thornton Jr. made the team having missed about a month — every preseason game included — off an underwhelming rookie year. When a 2025 fourth-rounder makes your roster with zero August tape, that's not a reward. That's a room with no one to beat him out. Same message from the other end of the depth chart: Dererick Young survived on special teams value after catching exactly ONE pass for ten yards all preseason. Six receivers made the initial squad, and the story each spot tells is the same — this position needs reinforcements, and everybody in the building knows it. Which, of course, is the exact drumbeat under the Jeudy talk we opened with. The roster confessed the need. Now watch the front office answer it.

Confession two: they believe Jeanty is back SOON. Rewind to August 23rd — the scariest afternoon of camp. Ashton Jeanty unable to put weight on the leg, teammates circling in prayer, a fan base holding its breath. Then the exhale: a sprained ankle, not a catastrophe. But here's the part that tells you what the TEAM believes, beyond any press conference: they kept just three running backs. Three. Ogunbowale was let go, no veteran was added, no insurance body claimed. You only run that thin at running back if you expect your star back quickly — and then Kubiak went and confirmed it with his face, smiling and answering "yes" when asked point-blank if Jeanty plays against Miami. If there's any setback, Mike Washington Jr. — the fourth-rounder Spytek reportedly pounded the table for — has earned the staff's trust and the start. But every structural signal says the same thing: the engine of this offense is nearly back.

And let's be fair to the room behind him, because "three backs" only works when the other two are real. Washington's August was no accident — the 53-yard touchdown burst against Arizona, the six-yards-a-carry night against Houston, the composed first-team work in the finale. Three games, three proofs, and a running style that fits this outside zone scheme like it was tailored. Dylan Laube brings the special-teams motor and change-of-pace hands that won him his spot outright. It's a thin room by design, but it's not a weak one — and that distinction is exactly what the front office bet on.

Confession three: this front office drafts, develops, and shows no mercy at the margins. Not one undrafted rookie survived to the 53 — with a ten-man draft class the math was always cruel, and even Brandon Cleveland, the final pick of the class, didn't make it. Sloan, the camp darling of the edge group, got squeezed out by a numbers game — four edge spots, and Patrick Johnson simply played too well to cut — but cleared waivers and landed right back on the practice squad, exactly where the Raiders wanted him stashed. Ruthless at the bottom, patient underneath. That's modern roster-building.

Confession four — and this is the one that keeps me honest: the interior line is thinner than anyone wants to admit. The Meredith trade squeezed the center depth down to a rookie, Trey Zuhn III, who logged a grand total of seventeen preseason snaps at the position. Jackson Powers-Johnson holds down right guard where he spent all of camp, and Atonio Mafi — who genuinely surprised people by making the team — brings real promise as a run blocker with pass protection that came and went, sometimes at Mendoza's expense. Guard and center are the two spots I'd watch like a hawk, and if veteran help arrives early in the season — and you already know which former first-round interior name the rumor mill attached to Kubiak this week — Mafi's seat gets warm in a hurry.

And the painful confession: this defense goes forward without Kieran Crawford. The third-round rookie had rushed his way into real rotation plans before the Achilles ended his season at zero. Kubiak's response told you about the culture — praise for the work ethic, a "bright future," a promise that Crawford stays in every meeting while he rehabs — but culture doesn't rush the passer. Losing a drafted contributor before his first snap is a real subtraction, and it's why every edge report we've covered this month matters so much.

One more thing before the scorecard, because a baseline is only useful if you know how fast it gets stress-tested — and this one gets tested IMMEDIATELY. Miami's whole offensive identity is speed in space, the exact style that punishes a second level still proving it can tackle. Week 2 sends this team on the road to the Chargers, a division fight with zero warm-up. And somewhere out past the leaves turning sits the marker this channel has circled since day one: the Week 13 bye, the natural crossroads where the Cousins-to-Mendoza question stops being theoretical. The schedule doesn't ease anybody into anything. That's precisely why the depth confessions above aren't offseason trivia — they're the plot of the next three months.
